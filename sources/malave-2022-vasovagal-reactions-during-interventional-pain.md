## Three-line Summary
Narrative review of vasovagal syncope (VVS) during interventional pain procedures; mechanism, incidence, risk factors, prevention, and management.
Incidence 0–4% overall (2.6% Kennedy 2013); risk factors: prior VVS history (strongest), male, age <65, low pre-procedure pain, dehydration, anxiety; Bezold–Jarisch reflex is the central mechanism.
Prevention evidence is weak (small RCTs); acute management is supine + Trendelenburg + IV fluids ± atropine; usually self-limiting within minutes.

## Abstract

Vasovagal reactions are a benign but common outcome of interventional pain management procedures. They can negatively impact patient care through aborted procedures and fear of future procedures. This narrative review covers the pathophysiology, incidence, risk factors, prevention, and management of vasovagal reactions during interventional pain management procedures, with emphasis on the relative lack of research on preventive measures. Moderate sedation and anxiolytics have been used prophylactically but their side-effect profiles limit routine use. Prophylactic antimuscarinics and IV fluids show potential benefit with relatively low side-effect profiles but remain understudied specifically for this procedural context.

## Pathophysiology

Vasovagal syncope is the most common type of syncope, comprising up to 40% of all outpatient syncopal events. The primary mechanism is the **Bezold–Jarisch reflex**: excessive venous pooling leads to decreased blood pressure sensed by baroreceptors (aortic arch, carotid sinus, heart walls, intrathoracic vessels), relaying to the nucleus tractus solitarius, which inhibits sympathetic response and increases vagal tone → hypotension + bradycardia. Tilt-table testing is the standard diagnostic model.

For interventional pain procedures, additional mechanisms include:
- Sympathetic blockade from epidural anesthesia → lower venous tone → decreased cardiac output
- External stimuli (fear, anxiety, pain, sight of blood, heat) → increased cardiac contractility despite underfilled left ventricle → mechanoreceptors relay via vagal afferents → increased parasympathetic tone

**Three VVS types (classification):**
1. **Cardioinhibitory**: HR < 40 bpm
2. **Vasodepressor**: SBP < 80 mmHg or >30% drop without significant HR reduction
3. **Mixed**: HR < 40 bpm AND SBP < 80 mmHg or >30% drop

## Incidence

- Overall range: **0–4%** of interventional pain procedures (multiple studies)
- Kennedy 2013 (n > 8,000 fluoroscopically guided procedures, 5 years): **2.6% overall**; **3.5% for epidural steroid injections** specifically
- Cervical vs. lumbar ESI: conflicting data
  - Trentman 2009: cervical ESI 7× higher than lumbar (8% vs. 1%)
  - Kennedy 2013: cervical 0.47% vs. lumbar 3.67% (training confound acknowledged)

## Risk Factors

| Risk Factor | Magnitude | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Prior VVS history | Strongest predictor | Most important — single best predictor |
| Male sex | 2× odds vs. female | Proposed: greater muscle mass → precipitous BP drop |
| Age < 65 years | 2.4× odds vs. ≥65 | Older patients: more prior medical exposure, higher pain threshold |
| Pre-procedure pain score < 5/10 | VVS rate 3.2% vs. 2.2% | Lower baseline pain → lower threshold for discomfort |
| Less-trained provider | Resident 5.5% > Fellow 4.1% > Attending 2.7% | Schneider 2014, n=4,482 TFESI |
| Dehydration, anxiety, baseline hypotension/bradycardia | Proposed | Less quantified |

## Prevention

### Moderate Sedation (midazolam + fentanyl)
- Kennedy 2015 (n=6,364 spine injections, 214 with sedation): 0% VVS with sedation vs. 3.3% without
- In patients with prior VVS history: 0% recurrence with sedation vs. 23.3% without
- **Limitations**: risk of iatrogenic spinal injury (67% of cervical closed claims involved sedation/anesthesia per ASA data), airway compromise, arrhythmia, hypotension. Not recommended for routine prophylaxis.

### Anxiolytics (benzodiazepines)
- Gebhardt 2018 (n=2,747 low-dose intrathecal procedures, 1,291 with 1–2 mg IV midazolam): VVS 15% without vs. 7.5% with midazolam (p<0.001)
- James 2005: 2–4 mg sublingual lorazepam prevented recurrent VVS in 95% of stereotactic breast biopsy patients
- **Note**: no studies specifically on anxiolytics for interventional pain procedures; extrapolated from other settings
- **Limitations**: sedation, fall risk (especially elderly), confusion, anterograde amnesia, teratogenic concern

### Antimuscarinics (atropine, glycopyrrolate)
- Santini 1999: atropine 0.02 mg/kg resolved cardioinhibitory VVS in 69.7% vs. 21.9% placebo (tilt-table, n=84); no effect on vasodepressor form
- Prophylactic efficacy demonstrated for: femoral sheath removal, cryoablation for AF (Sun 2017 — prevented all 3 forms), C-sections (Chamchad 2011 — bradycardia only, minimal BP effect), ophthalmological surgery
- **Glycopyrrolate preferred over atropine**: greater potency, longer duration, selective inhibitory effect at salivary/sweat glands, minimal CV/CNS effects, smaller tachycardia magnitude (Mirakhur 1982, Yang 1996)
- **Gap**: no RCT specifically studying prophylactic antimuscarinics for interventional pain procedures. Mahajan 2008 recommends glycopyrrolate 0.2 mg IV increments for at-risk patients without references.

### IV Fluids and IV Access
- Rationale: correct dehydration (especially if pre-procedure fasting per ASA guidelines: clear liquids 2 h, solids 6 h before), restore volume status, provide line for medications
- Some authors recommend IV access pre-procedure for high-risk patients
- **Gap**: no studies on standalone IV fluid prophylaxis for interventional pain VVS

## Management (Step-by-Step)

1. Stop procedure immediately
2. Place cold compress on neck
3. Position: supine or **Trendelenburg** (head declined 15–30° below feet)
4. Counterpressure techniques: squatting, leg crossing (improve venous return)
5. If conservative measures fail: start IV fluids (if not running) + vasoactive medications:
   - Ephedrine 5–10 mg IV increments
   - Glycopyrrolate 0.2 mg IV increments
   - Atropine 0.4–1.0 mg IV increments
6. Transfer to ED if: SBP < 90 mmHg, MAP < 65 mmHg, or HR < 50 bpm

All patients should have vital sign monitoring (pulse oximetry, ECG, BP) throughout procedure.

## Key Gaps Identified by Authors

1. No studies specifically on anxiolytic prophylaxis for interventional pain VVS
2. No studies specifically on antimuscarinic prophylaxis for interventional pain VVS
3. No studies on standalone IV fluid prophylaxis for this context
4. Whether antimuscarinics + IV fluids together are more effective for vasodepressor/mixed forms

## Conclusions

VVS is the most common complication of interventional pain procedures (0–4% incidence). Management is well-established. Prevention is understudied. Moderate sedation is effective for patients with prior VVS history but carries iatrogenic risks. Anxiolytics and antimuscarinics show promise from other procedural settings but lack interventional pain-specific RCT data. Glycopyrrolate (lower side-effect profile than atropine) is a candidate for future prophylactic trials.

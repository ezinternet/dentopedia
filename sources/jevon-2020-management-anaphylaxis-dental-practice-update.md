## Three-line Summary

Narrative review / clinical update (British Dental Journal 2020) on recognising and managing anaphylaxis in the dental practice, following Resuscitation Council UK guidance, written around the UK adrenaline auto-injector (AAI) supply crisis that now requires general dental practitioners to draw up and give intramuscular (IM) adrenaline from a glass ampoule.

UK hospital admissions for anaphylaxis rose ~700% (1→7 per 100,000/yr, 1992–2012) with ~20 deaths/yr; dental triggers are antibiotics (mainly amoxicillin; ~50% of UK perioperative anaphylaxis, 4.0 per 100,000 administrations), chlorhexidine (two UK deaths after socket irrigation), latex, local anaesthetic (true allergy <1% of the ~0.1–1% LA adverse events), Recaldent toothpaste and iodoform/Alvogyl; IM adrenaline into the anterolateral middle-third of the thigh, 500 µg for patients >12 y, repeated every 5 min, is the single life-saving intervention and ~40% of UK anaphylaxis deaths received no adrenaline.

Every dental practice should stock adrenaline 1:1,000 (1 mg/mL) ampoules with 23G 25 mm (blue) needles; current IM technique drops gloves-as-routine, alcohol swabbing, the skin–hub gap, aspiration and post-injection massage; an AAI is now a fallback only when it is the sole adrenaline preparation available.

## 2. Key Contributions
- Reframes IM adrenaline competency for GDPs: with AAI supply failing, drawing up adrenaline 1:1,000 from an ampoule and giving it IM with a syringe and needle is a nationally expected skill, not an AAI substitute.
- Consolidates the dental-specific trigger list with UK epidemiology: antibiotics (amoxicillin), chlorhexidine (mouthwash / socket irrigation), latex, local anaesthetic and its excipients, Recaldent toothpaste, iodoform/Alvogyl.
- Documents the current (de-escalated) IM thigh-injection technique — no routine gloves, no alcohol swab, no skin–hub gap, no aspiration, no post-injection massage.
- Restates Resuscitation Council UK position: 500 µg IM for >12 y; AAI only if it is the only adrenaline preparation available in a healthcare setting.

## 3. Methodology and Architecture
- **Design**: narrative clinical update; no systematic search, no pooled data. Authorities cited: Resuscitation Council UK, MHRA, SDCEP, Public Health England, Chief Dental Officer for England, Department of Health, GDC.
- **Structure**: definition → incidence → dental causes → clinical features / differential → adrenaline (dose, route, through-clothing, allergy to adrenaline) → emergency drug kit (AAI vs ampoule, needle) → suggested IM injection procedure → Resuscitation Council UK management algorithm → debrief / documentation / reporting.

## 4. Key Results and Benchmarks

**Epidemiology**
- UK hospital admissions for anaphylaxis: 1 → 7 per 100,000 population/yr between 1992 and 2012 (~700% rise); mortality still rare (~20 deaths/yr UK).
- Antibiotics: main cause of UK perioperative anaphylaxis — almost 50% of cases, incidence 4.0 per 100,000 administrations. A French study identified 17 cases of amoxicillin anaphylaxis given for dental procedures.
- Chlorhexidine: two UK deaths after chlorhexidine mouthwash was used to irrigate extraction sockets; MHRA alert 2014; a UK national report reviewed 266 perioperative anaphylaxis cases (3 potentially avoidable by better history-taking).
- Local anaesthetic: adverse effects ~0.1–1%; ~1% of those are confirmed allergic. Amide allergy very rare; least allergenic amides = mepivacaine and plain prilocaine; esters more allergenic (metabolised to PABA); benzocaine is the only ester used in dentistry (topical). Many "LA allergies" are to excipients — benzoate preservatives (multi-dose vials), metabisulphite antioxidants (in adrenaline-containing solutions) — or are psychogenic / vasovagal.
- Latex: ~1–6% of UK population; worldwide prevalence ~9.7% healthcare workers, 7.2% susceptible patients, 4.3% general population; spina bifida 67%; cross-reactive foods banana, kiwi, avocado. Latex-induced anaphylaxis in dentistry extremely rare — one rubber-dam case report in 20 years; the 266-case perioperative series had zero latex cases.
- Toothpaste: fatal anaphylaxis reported to Recaldent (milk-derived, CPP-ACP) in a dairy-allergic US teenager; a similar non-fatal Australian paediatric case with "tooth mousse".
- Iodoform / Alvogyl: contraindicated with allergy to procaine-type anaesthetic, iodine, or iodine-related compounds; two anecdotal anaphylaxis reports.

**Clinical features / diagnosis**
- Anaphylaxis = rapid-onset generalised reaction: skin (redness, itching), mucosal swelling/angioedema, airway swelling (mouth/throat/tongue), wheeze, tachypnoea, tachycardia, hypotension.
- Series of 593 cases: urticaria and angioedema 87%, shortness of breath / wheeze 59%, hypotension symptoms 33%.
- Assess with the ABCDE approach; a diagnostic triad of criteria (sudden onset + rapid progression + life-threatening airway/breathing/circulation problem, usually with skin changes) makes anaphylaxis likely — *the exact 3-criteria box did not render in the retrieved text*.
- Differential: panic attack, vasovagal syncope — distinguished via ABCDE (box did not render).

**Adrenaline**
- Failure to give adrenaline is the most common cause of anaphylaxis death; UK registry: in almost 40% of anaphylaxis deaths adrenaline was not administered; delay increases risk of death.
- **Dose stated in text**: 500 µg IM for a patient over 12 years of age (Resuscitation Council UK). *The full recommended-dose list, including paediatric doses by age band, was a box that did not render in the retrieved text — verify against the current Resuscitation Council UK anaphylaxis algorithm before relying on paediatric figures.*
- **Repeat interval**: the dose can be repeated at 5-minute intervals until there has been an adequate response.
- **Route / site**: intramuscular, ideally into the anterolateral aspect of the middle third of the thigh (vastus lateralis) — high plasma level within minutes. Deltoid (arm) absorbs more slowly and is not routinely recommended (only "in a desperate situation").
- **Through clothing**: IM ideally into bare leg; AAIs can be given through light clothing; giving ampoule-drawn adrenaline through light clothing is "reasonable" in a time-critical situation — avoid the trouser seam and pocket contents.
- **Allergy to adrenaline**: real but very rare, usually hypersensitivity to sodium metabisulphite (preservative in adrenaline 1:1,000 and in all three UK AAIs). Still administer adrenaline — the life-saving need outweighs the theoretical sulphite risk.

**Emergency drug kit**
- Adrenaline 1:1,000 (1 mg/mL) ampoules should be stocked (prefilled syringes also acceptable; both have had UK supply issues). Chief Dental Officer asked practices to replace AAIs with ampoules to preserve national AAI stock.
- AAIs available in UK: Emerade, EpiPen, Jext (designed for self-use; accidental self-injection reported). Use an AAI only if it is the only available adrenaline preparation in a healthcare setting.
- Needle: 25 mm (blue 23G 25 mm) for all ages; a longer 21G 38 mm needle may be needed in some adults. Safety needles preferred to reduce needle-stick injury.
- Follow Public Health England guidance for what to have available (the itemised list did not render).

**IM thigh injection — current technique (what changed)**
- Routine gloves not necessary (staff will usually be wearing them anyway).
- Routine alcohol-swab skin cleaning no longer necessary.
- Leaving a skin–needle-hub gap "in case the needle breaks" no longer advocated — risks subcutaneous rather than IM delivery.
- Aspirating / drawing back to check for a vessel no longer advised for IM thigh injections.
- Massaging the site afterwards no longer advocated — may dispel drug out of the muscle.

**Opening the glass ampoule**
- Tap the top gently first to dispel drug from the neck. Cuts/lacerations and shattering occur if too much pressure is applied. *The numbered injury-minimisation steps did not render; if the ampoule shatters, discard glass and contents and use another ampoule.*

**Management (Resuscitation Council UK algorithm)**
- *The algorithm figure and the numbered "management of suspected anaphylaxis" list did not render in the retrieved text.* Text confirms: follow the Resuscitation Council UK algorithm; use ABCDE; give IM adrenaline promptly and repeat at 5 min; take a detailed history; SBAR/SBARD communication (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) for calling for help.
- Refractory anaphylaxis: *no dedicated refractory section rendered in the retrieved text beyond "repeat at five-minute intervals until adequate response"* — the paper defers to the Resuscitation Council UK algorithm (which covers repeat IM adrenaline, IV fluid challenge and specialist/critical-care escalation).

**After the event**
- Planned debrief + significant event analysis (the latter best done later).
- Documentation: circumstances immediately before onset (to identify trigger), acute features, time of onset.
- Reporting: suspected drug-related anaphylaxis → MHRA Yellow Card; fatal anaphylaxis in practice may need CQC notification.

## 5. Limitations and Future Work
- Narrative review, no systematic method; much dental-trigger evidence is case reports and anecdote.
- UK-specific regulatory framing (MHRA, CDO, CQC, SDCEP, PHE) — dose principles are Resuscitation Council UK / broadly transferable, but device names and reporting routes are not.
- Retrieved PMC text omitted the dose table, algorithm figure and numbered procedures — the highest-value chairside content must be cross-checked against the current Resuscitation Council UK anaphylaxis guideline.
- Pre-dates the 2021 Resuscitation Council UK anaphylaxis guideline update; paediatric weight/age dose bands and refractory-anaphylaxis guidance may have since been revised.

## 7. Glossary
- **Anaphylaxis**: severe, rapid-onset, potentially fatal generalised (IgE-mediated or non-immune) hypersensitivity reaction with airway, breathing and/or circulation compromise, usually with skin/mucosal changes.
- **Adrenaline / epinephrine 1:1,000**: 1 mg/mL solution; the first-line drug in anaphylaxis; given IM.
- **AAI (adrenaline auto-injector)**: pre-loaded spring device (Emerade, EpiPen, Jext) designed for self-administration; fixed doses.
- **IM (intramuscular)**: injection into muscle — here the vastus lateralis (anterolateral middle-third of thigh).
- **Vastus lateralis**: lateral quadriceps muscle; preferred anaphylaxis injection site for fast absorption.
- **Sodium metabisulphite**: antioxidant preservative in adrenaline 1:1,000 and in AAIs; rare cause of hypersensitivity — not a reason to withhold adrenaline.
- **PABA (para-aminobenzoic acid)**: allergenic metabolite of ester local anaesthetics.
- **Recaldent (CPP-ACP)**: casein phosphopeptide–amorphous calcium phosphate; milk-derived remineralising agent in some toothpastes / "tooth mousse" — hazard in milk-allergic patients.
- **SDCEP**: Scottish Dental Clinical Effectiveness Programme.
- **MHRA Yellow Card**: UK adverse drug reaction reporting scheme.
- **SBAR(D)**: Situation–Background–Assessment–Recommendation(–Decision) structured clinical handover tool.
- **ABCDE**: Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure — systematic emergency assessment.

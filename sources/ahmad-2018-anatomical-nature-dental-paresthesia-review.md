## Three-line Summary

This narrative review examines the anatomy of the pterygomandibular fossa, anesthetic agents, and nerve injury mechanisms to explain dental paresthesia following inferior alveolar nerve blocks. The review synthesizes anatomical studies reporting that lingual nerve fascicle counts range from 7 to 39 (average 20) and inferior alveolar nerve fascicles range from 3 to 14 (average 7.2), and that Articaine 4% accounted for 59% of paresthesia cases between 1999-2008 in one retrospective study.

The review concludes that anatomical variations—including accessory foramina, variant nerve branches, multiple mandibular canals, and variable fascicle numbers—combined with anesthetic neurotoxicity and mechanical needle trauma are likely multifactorial causes of paresthesia. No formal meta-analysis or quantitative synthesis was performed.

Clinically, dentists should appreciate the highly variable anatomy of the pterygomandibular fossa, use proper injection technique, and recognize that most paresthesia resolves within days to months, though rare cases persist beyond six months. The lingual nerve is at greater risk (89% of neuropathies) than the inferior alveolar nerve following mandibular blocks.

## 세줄요약

본 내러티브 리뷰는 하치조신경 차단술 후 발생하는 치과적 감각저하를 설명하기 위해 익상하악와의 해부학, 마취제, 신경손상 기전을 검토하였다. 이 리뷰는 설신경의 신경다발 수가 7~39개(평균 20개), 하치조신경은 3~14개(평균 7.2개)라는 해부학적 연구 결과와, 1999~2008년 후향적 연구에서 아티카인 4%가 감각저하 증례의 59%를 차지했다는 보고를 종합하였다.

이 리뷰는 부속공, 변이 신경가지, 다중 하악관, 다양한 신경다발 수 등의 해부학적 변이와 마취제 신경독성, 기계적 바늘 외상이 감각저하의 다인성 원인일 가능성이 높다고 결론지었다. 공식적인 메타분석이나 정량적 종합은 수행되지 않았다.

임상적으로 치과의사는 익상하악와의 매우 가변적인 해부학을 숙지하고 적절한 주사 기술을 사용해야 하며, 대부분의 감각저하는 수일에서 수개월 내에 회복되지만 드물게 6개월 이상 지속되는 사례도 있음을 인식해야 한다. 하악 차단술 후 설신경이 하치조신경보다 신경병증 위험이 더 높다(89%).

## 2. Key Contributions

- Synthesizes anatomical descriptions of the pterygomandibular fossa and its key neural structures (lingual nerve, inferior alveolar nerve, nerve to mylohyoid)
- Reviews nerve injury classification systems: Seddon (neurapraxia, axonotmesis, neurotmesis) and Sunderland (grades I–V)
- Reports lingual nerve fascicle count: 7–39 (mean 20) and inferior alveolar nerve fascicle count: 3–14 (mean 7.2) at the lingula (Pogrel et al., 2003)
- Documents that Articaine 4% accounted for 59% of paresthesia cases from 1999–2008 (Gaffen and Haas, 2009) and 71% in some studies, though cell culture and animal studies show no higher toxicity
- Identifies that 89% of lingual neuropathies occur following mandibular nerve blocks; lingual nerve injury probability via simulated injection was 4.5% (Morris et al., 2010)
- Notes that 31 of 134 paresthesia patients reported an "electric shock" sensation during injection, suggesting mechanical trauma
- Reviews imaging modalities for nerve injury assessment: panoramic radiography, CT, magnetic source imaging, ultrasonography, and magnetic resonance neurography (MRN)

## 3. Methodology and Architecture

- Design: Narrative literature review; no systematic search protocol, PRISMA, or meta-analysis methodology described
- Scope: Reviews anatomy of pterygomandibular fossa, inferior alveolar nerve block complications, anesthetic agents (especially Articaine and Prilocaine), nerve injury histology, and classification systems
- Data synthesis: Qualitative synthesis of anatomical studies, clinical case reports, and retrospective case series; no formal quantitative synthesis or meta-analysis
- Key references include cadaveric anatomical studies (Kiesselbach & Chamberlain 1984; Khoury et al. 2010; Morris et al. 2010), retrospective paresthesia reports (Haas & Lennon 1995; Gaffen & Haas 2009; Garisto et al. 2010), and nerve injury classification literature (Seddon 1943; Sunderland 1978)
- Outcomes: Descriptive compilation of anatomical variations, reported paresthesia frequencies, and proposed etiological mechanisms

## 4. Key Results and Benchmarks

- **Articaine-related paresthesia proportion —** 71% (Pogrel, 2012) to 33% (Hopman et al., 2017 range); 59% of paresthesia cases 1999–2008 (Gaffen & Haas, 2009)
- **Lingual nerve fascicle count —** Range 7–39, mean 20 (Pogrel et al., 2003)
- **Inferior alveolar nerve fascicle count —** Range 3–14, mean 7.2 (Pogrel et al., 2003)
- **Lingual nerve injury proportion following mandibular blocks —** 89% of lingual neuropathies (Garisto et al., 2010)
- **Simulated injection lingual nerve penetration —** 4.5% (Morris et al., 2010)
- **Lingual nerve position relative to lingual crest —** Direct contact in 62%, superior in 17.6% (Kiesselbach & Chamberlain, 1984)
- **"Electric shock" sensation during injection —** 31 of 134 paresthesia cases (Haas & Lennon, 1995)
- **Incidence increase with general anesthesia —** 5× higher for lingual/IAN damage during third molar extraction (Brann et al., 1999)

## 5. Limitations and Future Work

- Narrative review design without systematic search, inclusion/exclusion criteria, or quality assessment of primary studies
- No meta-analysis or quantitative synthesis performed; reported proportions are descriptive only
- Retrospective case series (Haas & Lennon 1995; Gaffen & Haas 2009; Garisto et al. 2010) are subject to reporting bias and denominator uncertainty (market share data may not reflect true incidence)
- Animal and cell culture studies may not fully translate to human neurotoxicity mechanisms
- Anatomical cadaver studies may not reflect in vivo dynamics during injection (needle trajectory, patient movement, tissue displacement)
- No discussion of needle design (barbed vs. non-barbed) beyond brief mention of Stacy & Hajjar 1994
- Etiological distinction between neurotoxicity and mechanical trauma remains speculative; half of IAN damage cases show no signs of needle trauma

## 6. Related Work

- Haas DA, Lennon D (1995) — 21-year retrospective review (1973–1993) of paresthesia reports in Ontario; Articaine accounted for the majority of non-surgical paresthesia cases
- Gaffen AS, Haas DA (2009) — Retrospective review (1999–2008) of voluntary paresthesia reports; Articaine 59%, Prilocaine second most common
- Pogrel MA et al. (2003) — Anatomical study of fascicle counts in lingual and inferior alveolar nerves at the lingula; proposed variable fascicle number as paresthesia risk factor
- Morris CD et al. (2010) — Cadaveric simulation of inferior alveolar injections; reported 4.5% lingual nerve penetration rate
- Seddon HJ (1943) — Classified nerve injuries into neurapraxia, axonotmesis, and neurotmesis—foundational classification referenced for severity grading

## 7. Glossary

- **IAN (Inferior Alveolar Nerve)**: 하치조신경 — mandibular nerve branch providing sensation to mandibular teeth, chin, and lower lip; key target in inferior alveolar nerve blocks
- **Lingual nerve**: 설신경 — mandibular nerve branch providing sensation to anterior two-thirds of tongue and floor of mouth; often injured during mandibular blocks
- **Pterygomandibular fossa**: 익상하악와 — anatomical space bounded by mandibular ramus, medial/lateral pterygoid muscles, and pterygomandibular raphe; target site for inferior alveolar nerve block injection
- **Neurapraxia**: 신경실용증 — mildest nerve injury with intact axon but temporary conduction block; resolves within hours to days
- **Axonotmesis**: 축삭단절 — more severe nerve injury with axon disruption but intact epineurium; recovery takes months
- **Neurotmesis**: 신경절단 — complete nerve disruption with loss of sensory innervation; most severe injury type
- **MRN (Magnetic Resonance Neurography)**: 자기공명신경조영술 — advanced imaging technique that can distinguish injured from intact nerve fibers and detect intrafascicular hemorrhage
- **Articaine**: 아티카인 — 4% local anesthetic with high bone diffusibility; debated neurotoxic potential with reported paresthesia association in epidemiological studies

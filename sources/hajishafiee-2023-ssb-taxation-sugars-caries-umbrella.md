## Why Ingested
Directly extends [[caries/moynihan-2016-sugars-dental-caries-threshold-intake]] (same lead policy author, same dose-response framework) to quantify the public-health impact of SSB taxation on caries prevention in both high-income and low-middle-income countries — providing the upstream policy evidence that contextualizes why managing the cariogenic environment around [[resin/melo-2023-bioactive-dental-resins-restorative-dentistry]] restorations matters.

## Three-line Summary

Umbrella review (JBI methodology, PROSPERO CRD42022293187) of 14 systematic reviews (SSB tax → consumption/sugars intake, PRISMA: 419 identified → 48 screened → 14 included) and 5 systematic reviews (sugars → dental caries dose-response, 100 identified → 21 screened → 5 included), covering both HIC and LMIC.

A 20% volumetric SSB tax is modelled to cut free sugars intake by 20.0% (HIC) / 18.0% (LMIC) (≥4.0 g/day per capita, up to 4.4 g/day HIC), preventing 0.03 carious teeth per adult, and reducing child caries-surface counts by 0.18 (HIC) / 0.16 (LMIC) and caries occurrence by 2.9% (HIC) / 2.7% (LMIC) over 10 years.

Individual-level effect is modest and LMIC caries estimates likely lean conservative (dose-response coefficients came exclusively from HIC cohorts), but the population-level impact is judged meaningful enough to support including SSB taxation in WHO's Global Strategy on Oral Health.

## 세줄요약

WHO 구강건강전략 위탁 umbrella review (JBI 방법론, PROSPERO CRD42022293187): SR 14편(SSB 과세→소비/당섭취, PRISMA 419편→48편 스크리닝→14편 포함)·5편(당섭취→우식 용량-반응, 100편→21편→5편)을 HIC·LMIC 모두에서 종합.

20% SSB 과세 모델링 결과 자유당 섭취가 HIC 20.0%·LMIC 18.0% 감소(1인당 1일 4.0g 이상, HIC 최대 4.4g) → 10년간 성인 0.03치아 우식 예방, 소아 우식표면수 HIC 0.18·LMIC 0.16 감소, 우식발생률 HIC 2.9%·LMIC 2.7% 감소.

개인 단위 효과는 작고 LMIC 추정치는 보수적일 가능성이 큼(용량-반응 계수가 전부 HIC 코호트에서 도출됨)이나, 인구집단 규모에서는 의미가 있다고 판단되어 WHO 구강건강전략에 SSB 과세 포함을 지지.

## 2. Key Contributions
- First umbrella review to jointly model the impact of SSB taxation on dental caries prevention in both HIC and LMIC within a single framework, commissioned to inform WHO's Global Strategy on Oral Health.
- Synthesizes price-elasticity-of-demand (PED) evidence from 6 systematic reviews with quantitative synthesis (of 14 total for Q1/Q2), spanning US, other-HIC, and LMIC (Mexico/Brazil/multi-country) data.
- Converts tax-driven SSB-consumption change into a quantified sugars-intake reduction using the Atwater Factor (4 kcal ≈ 17 kJ per gram of sugar).
- Applies dose-response coefficients drawn from three original cohort studies — Bernabé et al. (adults), Turck et al. (primary dentition), and the Michigan Study (permanent dentition) — identified within the 5 included Q3 systematic reviews, to model the 10-year caries impact of a 20% volumetric SSB tax.
- Reports SSB taxes are more regressive (larger consumption reduction) in lower socioeconomic status (SES) groups in 3 of the 4 reviews that examined SES subgroups; only a nutrient-based (sugars-threshold) tax design showed a statistically robust consumption effect among the tax types compared.

## 3. Methodology and Architecture
- Design: umbrella review (review of systematic reviews) per JBI Umbrella Review methodology; protocol PROSPERO CRD42022293187 (Jan 2022).
- Four review questions: (Q1) SSB tax → SSB consumption/PED; (Q2) SSB tax → sugars intake; (Q3) sugars intake → dental caries dose-response; (Q4) modelled impact of a 20% volumetric SSB tax on caries averted over 10 years, computed separately for HIC and LMIC.
- Databases: PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL, Dentistry and Oral Sciences Source, plus registries (Cochrane Library, JBI SR Register, PROSPERO), selected via the University of Adelaide library for broad geographic coverage. No language or study-duration restriction; date range 2000–end 2021. Known experts contacted by email to supplement the search.
- Screening, data extraction (JBI extraction form), and AMSTAR quality appraisal were all performed independently in duplicate by two reviewers; disagreements resolved by discussion (a third, non-conflicted reviewer for AMSTAR disputes).
- Quality assessment: AMSTAR (A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews), 11-point scale; 8–11 = high quality, 4–7 = moderate, 0–3 = low.
- PRISMA flow — Q1/Q2: 419 systematic reviews identified after de-duplication → 48 retained after title/abstract screening → 14 included after full-text review (4 high-AMSTAR, 9 moderate, 1 low). Q3: 100 systematic reviews identified → 21 retained after title/abstract screening → 2 included after full-text screening, plus 3 more identified via expert contacts = 5 total (2 high-AMSTAR, 3 moderate).
- Geographic coverage: Q1/Q2 evidence spans the African, European, Southeast Asian, and Western Pacific WHO Regions plus the Region of the Americas.
- Evidence presentation: reverse-chronological tabulated summary with AMSTAR rating, plus a "best available evidence" narrative (most recent high-quality SR described first, compared against moderate-quality/earlier reviews). Where SR-level meta-analysis was unavailable (true for all of Q2's sugars-intake conversion and all of Q3's dose-response data), original studies within the included SRs were separately extracted and summarised (author, year, country, n, age, exposure, outcome, quality).

## 4. Key Results and Benchmarks

**Q1 — SSB tax → consumption (PED), HIC:**
- Teng et al. [high AMSTAR]: 10% tax → 10.0% consumption reduction (95% CI −5.0, 14.7%); PED −1.0 (95% CI −0.5, −1.47). No significant difference in effect by age group (only SR to report pooled age-group data).
- Afshin et al. [high AMSTAR, US-only pooled data]: 10% tax → 7.0% reduction (95% CI −3.0, −10.0%).
- Powell et al. [moderate AMSTAR, US price-elasticity studies]: overall OPE −1.21 (95% CI −0.7, −2.26) → 10% tax reduces consumption 7.1–22.6% (avg 12.1%); 20% tax reduces 14.2–45.2% (avg 24.2%).
- Andreyeva et al. [moderate AMSTAR, US data only]: mean PED estimates (95% CI −0.8, −1.0) → 10% tax reduces consumption 8–10%.

**Q1 — LMIC:**
- Nakhimovsky et al. [moderate AMSTAR, best-available LMIC synthesis]: 10% tax → 6–12% reduction (avg 9.0%); PED −0.90 (range −0.6 to −1.2).
- Cabrera Escobar et al. [moderate AMSTAR, meta-analysis pooling USA/Mexico/Brazil/France, spans both HIC and LMIC]: overall OPE −1.3 (95% CI −1.085, −1.509) → 10% tax reduces consumption 10.9–15.1% (avg 13.0%).

**Q1 — by tax type (10% tax level, single SR reporting this breakdown, not statistically significant across types):** an ad valorem-style tax [rendered "antax" in the extracted PMC text — likely a JATS/OCR extraction artifact] averaged 2.3% reduction (95% CI −11.2, 7.4); a volumetric tax averaged 10.2% (95% CI −4.1, −15.9%); a nutrient-based tax (sugars-concentration threshold) averaged 14.0% (95% CI −7.5, −20.1%) — the most effective design, consistent with separate WHO guidance.

**Q1 — SES and age subgroups:** taxes shown to be more regressive (larger effect) in lower-SES groups by Maniadakis et al., Thow et al. (HIC), and Nakhimovsky et al. (LMIC) — 3 of 4 SES-stratified reviews. Teng et al. (HIC) found no SES difference; some included studies even showed a larger 5% -tax effect in higher-SES groups. Thow et al. additionally found the impact on energy intake (EI) was larger in adults (10.0–48.0% reduction) than children (5.0–8.0%), attributed to substitution (e.g., with milk) in children.

**Q2 — sugars-intake reduction:**
- Nakhimovsky et al. (LMIC, 10% tax): EI ↓ median 18.0 kJ/person/day (range −5.0 to −39.0), i.e., 4.3 kcal/person/day (range −1.2 to −9.3) → 1.1 g sugars/person/day (range 0.3–2.3) via Atwater conversion.
- Maniadakis et al. (HIC, 10% tax): EI ↓ up to a maximum of 50 kcal/person/day (450 kcal/month) → up to 12.5 g/day (112.5 g/month) sugars reduction — a notably higher ceiling estimate than the multi-study average below.
- Across 9 of 16 original studies with usable data (identified within the included SRs): a 20% SSB tax was associated with sugars reductions ranging 1.8–11.0 g/person/day; average 4.0 g/day in LMIC and 4.4 g/day in HIC.

**Q3 — dose-response (sugars intake → caries); no meta-analysis available across the 5 SRs, so best-available original cohort data (lowest risk of bias, RoB) was used:**
- Adults: Bernabé et al. (low RoB) — each 10 g/day increase in total sugars intake → DMFT +0.09 over an 11-year follow-up.
- Children, primary dentition: Turck et al. (moderate RoB) — dmft +1.64 between ages 3–6 per 10 g/day sucrose consumption.
- Children, permanent dentition: the "Michigan Study" (low RoB) — +1% caries increase per 5 g sugar over a 3-year follow-up.

**Q4 — modelled impact of a 20% volumetric SSB tax over 10 years (best-available data from Q1–Q3 combined):**
- Sugars intake: ↓ 20.0% (HIC) / 18.0% (LMIC); ≥4.0 g/day per-capita reduction (4.4 g/day HIC, 4.0 g/day LMIC).
- Adults: 0.03 fewer carious teeth per capita (both HIC and LMIC).
- Children: caries-surface count ↓ 0.18 (HIC) / 0.16 (LMIC); caries occurrence ↓ 2.9% (HIC) / 2.7% (LMIC).

**Supplementary modelling context (Discussion, not part of the formal SR count):** Schwendicke et al., using German National Nutrition Survey consumption data, found a 20% tax's sugars-intake effect varied substantially by sex/SES — largest in lower-income males (up to 13.7 g/day, ages 20–29) and smallest in young females (5.7 g/day, ages 15–19). The UK's tiered, nutrient-based SSB tax (soft drinks industry levy) additionally drove product reformulation, producing a dual benefit (lower sugars content in available products + reduced purchasing of higher-sugars drinks) that is not captured in the tax-type modelling above.

## 5. Limitations and Future Work
- Database coverage gap: LMIC-specific databases (e.g., African Index Medicus/AIMS, Index Medicus for the Eastern Mediterranean Region/IMEMR) were not searched by any included review or by this umbrella review itself, despite otherwise broad database coverage (Web of Science, SciELO, LILACS among sources capturing LMIC output).
- Publication lag: systematic reviews published after the search cut-off (end 2021) and before this umbrella review's publication are omitted.
- Umbrella-review ceiling: a primary study never captured by any published systematic review is, by design, invisible to this umbrella review.
- LMIC dose-response gap: all three Q3 dose-response coefficients (Bernabé, Turck, Michigan Study) come from HIC cohorts — no LMIC longitudinal dose-response data exists, and available LMIC sugars–caries data are cross-sectional only. LMIC caries-reduction estimates are therefore likely conservative, since sugars exposure may have a larger caries effect where fluoride exposure is lower and undernutrition more prevalent.
- EI→sugars conversion bias: most reviews reported energy-intake (EI) change rather than sugars directly; the Atwater-Factor conversion assumes 100% of the EI change is attributable to sugars, which underestimates the true sugars effect whenever SSB is substituted with another energy-containing drink (e.g., milk) rather than simply reduced — e.g., Fletcher et al. found no net EI effect of taxation due to substitution, which does not mean no reduction in sugars intake.
- HIC/LMIC consumption variance: using published pooled mean SSB-consumption estimates for HIC/LMIC (rather than country-specific data) does not capture the considerable between-country variation in baseline SSB intake.
- Duplicate independent screening/extraction/appraisal was used throughout, but inadvertent systematic error cannot be excluded.
- Future work (author-stated): cost-effectiveness analysis building on these estimates to determine healthcare cost-benefit in HIC and LMIC; well-conducted longitudinal cohort studies from LMIC to establish the sugars–caries dose-response relationship; long-term outcome tracking of already-implemented SSB taxes.

## 7. Glossary
- PED: price elasticity of demand — percentage change in consumption per 1% change in price; a PED of −1.0 means a 10% tax causes a 10% consumption reduction.
- OPE: own price elasticity — change in demand for a product given a change in its own price.
- CPE: cross price elasticity — change in demand for a product given a change in the price of a substitute/complement.
- SSB: sugar-sweetened beverage — beverages with added sugars (carbonated drinks, still drinks, sweetened fruit juices); excludes beverages sweetened only with artificial sweeteners.
- AMSTAR: A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews (11-point scale).
- JBI: Joanna Briggs Institute — the methodology body whose Umbrella Review guidance structured this review's design and data-extraction forms.
- RoB: risk of bias — used here to rate the original cohort studies underlying the Q3 dose-response coefficients (low/moderate).
- HIC: high-income countries.
- LMIC: low- and middle-income countries.
- Volumetric tax: a tax applied per unit volume (e.g., per litre) regardless of sugar content, as distinct from an ad valorem tax (on price) or a nutrient-based/tiered tax (on sugar content).
- Umbrella review: a review of systematic reviews, synthesizing multiple bodies of evidence on a defined topic.

## Why Ingested

This multiauthor international narrative review synthesizes evidence on antibiotic indications in endodontics and provides clinical recommendations to counter inappropriate prescribing. It directly informs [[drug/antibiotics]] guidance by surveying European prescribing patterns and clarifying when antibiotics are NOT indicated (irreversible pulpitis, necrotic pulps, localized apical abscess) — a critical gap given that 40–86% of European dentists were inappropriately prescribing in these cases at the time of review.

## Three-line Summary

Narrative review of antibiotic indications in endodontic patient management, spanning 60+ published studies and multiregional European prescribing surveys. Antibiotics are ineffective and unnecessary for symptomatic irreversible pulpitis, necrotic pulps, and localized acute apical abscesses; drainage is the primary intervention. Amoxicillin is first-line for odontogenic infections; clindamycin for penicillin-allergic patients; systemic use is justified only when infection spreads beyond the tooth/periapex or in immunocompromised hosts.

## 2. Key Contributions

- **Antibiotics DO NOT reduce pain or swelling** in symptomatic apical pathosis without systemic involvement (level supported by Fouad et al. 1996, Henry et al. 2001, Keenan et al. 2006; Cochrane SR on irreversible pulpitis by Agnihotry et al. 2016)
- **NO-antibiotic indications clearly defined**: symptomatic irreversible pulpitis, pulp necrosis, acute apical periodontitis (no swelling), chronic apical abscess, acute apical abscess (localized, no systemic signs) — all managed by drainage/endodontic debridement alone
- **YES-antibiotic indications in specific scenarios**: acute apical abscess with systemic involvement (fever, malaise, lymphadenopathy, elevated temperature ≥38°C), medically compromised patients (e.g., infective endocarditis, prosthetic valves, recent joint replacement, immunosuppression), progressive/persistent infections refractory to routine drainage
- **First-line drug selection**: Amoxicillin (or amoxicillin-clavulanic acid) preferred over Penicillin VK due to better absorption and lower side-effect risk; metronidazole added when anaerobic coverage needed. Clindamycin in confirmed penicillin allergy.
- **European prescribing crisis documented**: 40–86% of endodontists/dentists in Spain, Belgium, Lithuania, Turkey prescribing antibiotics for conditions where they are contraindicated (irreversible pulpitis, necrotic pulp with no swelling); survey response rates 30–45% across studies.
- **Antibiotic resistance epidemiology**: oral Porphyromonas and Prevotella species showing rising resistance; α-hemolytic Streptococci, macrolides, penicillins increasingly resistant (Brescó-Salinas et al. 2006; Aracil et al. 2001; Groppo et al. 2004)
- **Dangers of empirical broad-spectrum use**: increases resistance, misuses resources, raises risk of anaphylaxis and unnecessary side effects; drives patient expectations cycle (Gonzales et al. 2001, Costelloe et al. 2010)

## 3. Methodology and Architecture

- **Design**: Narrative review with systematic element (survey collation)
- **Scope**: 
  - Literature review of antibiotic indications and use in endodontics (global, emphasis Europe 1970s–2016)
  - Systematic collation of antibiotic prescribing surveys in European countries (Table 1): UK, Turkey, Sweden, Belgium, Lithuania, Spain, Croatia (response rates 30–45%)
  - Clinical decision tables (Table 2: pulpal/periapical conditions and antibiotic adjunct indication; Table 3: traumatic dental injuries)
  - Additional sections on topical antibiotics (pulp capping, root canal treatment) and traumatic injury management
- **Outcomes**: 
  - Antibiotic first-choice and second-choice agents across countries
  - Indications matrix (YES/NO for systemic antibiotic use by condition)
  - Clinical recommendations for patient selection

### Antibiotic Selection Principles

- **Odontogenic infections are polymicrobial**: gram-positive, gram-negative, facultative and strict anaerobes (Siqueira & Rôças 2014)
- **Empirical prescribing challenge**: dentists do not routinely culture root canal/periapical samples; microorganisms are suspected, not confirmed
- **Resistance phenomenon**: bacteria exchanging resistance plasmids; resistance increasing in oral Porphyromonas spp., Prevotella spp., S. viridans, macrolides
- **Drug of choice sequence**:
  1. Penicillin V (or Amoxicillin) ± metronidazole for anaerobes — still effective in most cases
  2. **Amoxicillin (± clavulanic acid) preferred** over Penicillin V (better absorption, lower side-effect risk)
  3. Clindamycin in confirmed penicillin allergy (lincosamide, alternative coverage for anaerobes)
- **Adjunct timing**: antibiotics assist in preventing *spread* of infection; not in resolving established localized lesions lacking blood supply

## 5. Limitations and Future Work

- **Survey methodology limitations**: cross-sectional design; response rates 30–45%; no control for confounding (practitioner training, guidelines adoption); self-reported behavior may not match actual prescribing
- **Lack of RCT evidence for most indications**: Cochrane reviews conclude "very low-quality evidence" on systemic antibiotics in symptomatic apical periodontitis/acute apical abscess (Cope et al. 2014 Cochrane SR)
- **Polymicrobial complexity**: empirical treatment cannot be truly targeted without culture; resistance patterns vary by geography and time
- **No data on adherence to guidelines** in the studies reviewed — only what dentists report as their practice
- **Future needs**: 
  - RCTs on drainage-only vs antibiotics-adjunct in specific systemic-involvement scenarios
  - Local/topical antibiotic efficacy studies (currently no strong evidence supporting intracanal antibiotics)
  - Surveillance of resistance trends in oral strains
  - Implementation science: why guidelines are not followed despite clear evidence

## 6. Related Work

- **Shamszadeh et al. 2020** (systematic review, meta-analysis): prophylactic oral antibiotics do NOT reduce post-operative pain/swelling after endodontic treatment in adults with pulpal necrosis (8 RCTs, n=690) — reinforces the "antibiotics ineffective for symptom relief" finding
- **Agnihotry et al. 2016** (Cochrane review): no evidence to support antibiotics for pain relief in irreversible pulpitis
- **Cope et al. 2014** (Cochrane review, adults with symptomatic apical periodontitis or acute apical abscess): very low-quality evidence; insufficient to determine effects of systemic antibiotics
- **Matthews et al. 2003** (systematic review): infection must be systemic or patient febrile/immunocompromised to justify antibiotics

## 7. Glossary

- **Symptomatic irreversible pulpitis**: acute inflammation of vital pulp tissue causing spontaneous/provoked pain; radiographically normal to widened PDL; endodontic treatment indicated
- **Pulp necrosis**: loss of pulp vitality; nonvital on testing; may be asymptomatic or lead to apical periodontitis
- **Acute apical periodontitis**: inflammation of apical periodontal ligament; pain on percussion/biting; may progress to abscess if untreated
- **Acute apical abscess**: localized collection of pus at apex; may be confined (localized swelling) or spread systemically (fever, trismus, lymphadenopathy)
- **Odontogenic infection**: bacterial infection of dental origin (pulpal/periapical/periodontal); polymicrobial in >90% of cases
- **Polymicrobial**: involving multiple bacterial species (facultative anaerobes, strict anaerobes, gram-positive/negative)
- **Drainage (or incision and drainage, I&D)**: surgical or therapeutic opening of abscess to evacuate pus; primary intervention in abscess management
- **Antibiotic adjunct**: antibiotics used in addition to mechanical treatment (drainage, endodontic debridement); supportive, not primary therapy
- **Amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (Augmentin)**: combination of amoxicillin (β-lactam) + clavulanic acid (β-lactamase inhibitor) to overcome resistance
- **Clindamycin**: lincosamide antibiotic effective against gram-positive and anaerobic bacteria; used in penicillin-allergic patients
- **Medically compromised host**: patient with systemic disease/immunosuppression increasing infection risk (e.g., infective endocarditis, prosthetic valves, HIV, neutropenia, diabetes, renal/hepatic impairment)
- **Febrile**: elevated body temperature (≥38°C); sign of systemic infection
- **Antibiotic resistance**: bacterial ability to survive and multiply in the presence of an antibiotic due to genetic mutation or acquired resistance genes
- **Empirical therapy**: treatment based on clinical suspicion without laboratory confirmation (culture, susceptibility testing)

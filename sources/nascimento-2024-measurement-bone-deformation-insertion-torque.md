## Why Ingested

The wiki's insertion-torque cluster has always inferred bone compression stress indirectly — from achieved torque values (Trisi, Marconcini, Aldahlawi, Faot), from histology after healing ([[implants/coyac-2019-preclinical-model-links-osseo-densification-misfit]]), or from synthesis reasoning ([[overviews/high-insertion-torque-primary-stability-crestal-bone-overview]]). No held paper has directly *measured* the mechanical deformation of the bone bed at the moment of insertion. Nascimento 2024 does exactly that — strain-gauge extensometry on synthetic bone during implant seating — and reports something the wiki's histology-based evidence could only imply: **machined-surface implant insertion produced visible cracks in the synthetic bone**, a direct, time-zero, physical analogue of the micro-fracture step that Coyac 2019's histology inferred after the fact. This paper supplies the missing mechanical/time-zero layer under the biological (Coyac) and clinical (Marconcini/Aldahlawi) layers already held.

## Three-line Summary

In vitro bench study inserting five dental implant models — varying by surface treatment (machined vs acid-etched), thread profile (triangular vs trapezoidal), and body shape (cylindrical vs conical) — into D2-density-equivalent polyurethane synthetic bone blocks, measuring insertion torque (digital torque driver) and bone deformation (strain gauge extensometry) simultaneously during installation.

Both torque and bone deformation increased with insertion turns; trapezoidal threads required more torque than triangular, and conical bodies required more torque than cylindrical, but conical-bodied and trapezoidal-thread implants produced compression that did not compromise the bone bed, whereas machined-surface implants generated stresses that **exceeded the synthetic bone's mechanical resistance and produced visible cracks**.

Implant macro-geometry (thread profile, body shape) and surface treatment jointly determine whether insertion-generated compression stays within a bone-tolerable range or crosses into mechanical failure (cracking) that the authors link to a necrosis/implant-failure risk pathway — surgeons must weigh all three design axes together, not torque value alone.

## 2. Key Contributions

- **First held paper to directly measure bone deformation during implant insertion** (strain gauge extensometry), rather than inferring compression from achieved torque or post-healing histology.
- **Produces a direct, time-zero mechanical-failure endpoint (cracking)** in the synthetic bone bed — the bench analogue of the micro-fracture step that Coyac 2019's rat histology could only observe after the fact.
- **Isolates three independent design axes** (surface treatment, thread profile, body shape) that each shift torque and deformation, rather than treating "insertion torque" as a single undifferentiated dose.
- **Identifies a specific harmful combination** (machined surface) and a specific favorable combination (trapezoidal thread + conical body) within the same experimental frame — a design-level, not just magnitude-level, account of what makes torque safe or harmful.

## 3. Methodology and Architecture

- **Design**: in vitro bench/engineering study (mechanical measurement, no biological or healing endpoint).
- **Specimens**: five dental implant models differing in:
  - Surface treatment: machined vs acid-etched
  - Thread profile: triangular vs trapezoidal
  - Body shape: cylindrical vs conical
- **Bone analogue**: synthetic polyurethane blocks, D2-equivalent density.
- **Outcome measures**:
  - Insertion torque — digital torque driver
  - Bone deformation — strain gauge extensometry, recorded continuously during insertion

| Factor | Directional effect |
|---|---|
| Insertion turns (more turns) | Torque ↑ and bone deformation ↑ together |
| Thread profile: trapezoidal vs triangular | Trapezoidal requires **higher** insertion torque |
| Body shape: conical vs cylindrical | Conical requires **higher** insertion torque |
| Trapezoidal thread + conical body | Torque/compression generated does **not** harm osseointegration (favorable combination) |
| Surface treatment: machined | Insertion exceeded the synthetic bone's mechanical resistance → **visible cracks** (worst-performing combination) |
| Body shape: conical vs cylindrical (overall) | Conical implants performed **better** than cylindrical |

**Authors' conclusion (paraphrased)**: excessive deformations in peri-implant bone can lead to bone necrosis and implant failure; surgeons must weigh surface treatment, thread profile, and body shape together — not torque value alone — to keep insertion-induced compression within a bone-tolerable range.

## 5. Limitations and Future Work

- **Abstract-only ingest.** No PMC copy exists; exact torque values, strain magnitudes, sample sizes, and statistics are unavailable to this wiki. Any number attributed to this paper beyond the qualitative table above would be fabrication.
- **Synthetic bone ≠ human bone.** Polyurethane blocks (D2-equivalent) reproduce a target density and machining resistance but have no vascularity, no viscoelastic biological response, no healing capacity, and none of the anisotropy of real cortical/trabecular bone. Cracking in polyurethane is not equivalent to micro-fracture in living bone, though it is offered here as a mechanical analogue.
- **No biological or healing outcome measured.** This is a pure mechanical/time-zero study — no osseointegration, BIC, or long-term stability endpoint. It cannot by itself confirm that avoiding cracks in synthetic bone predicts better clinical outcomes; it establishes only that different implant designs impose different peak mechanical demands on the bed.
- **Model-to-factor mapping incomplete from the abstract.** Five implant models are named by combination of three binary factors (2×2×2=8 possible combinations, only 5 tested), but the abstract does not specify which five combinations were used or report a full factorial breakdown — only the directional main effects.
- **No statistics reported in the abstract** — all comparisons above (trapezoidal > triangular, conical > cylindrical, machined = cracked) are qualitative claims from the authors, not measured effect sizes held by this wiki.
- **Future work implied**: quantify the deformation/torque threshold at which synthetic (and by extension real) bone transitions from elastic compression to mechanical failure, and validate the synthetic-bone cracking endpoint against real-bone micro-fracture/histology (e.g., against the Coyac 2019 pathway).

## 6. Related Work

- **Coyac 2019** (`coyac-2019-preclinical-model-links-osseo-densification-misfit`): rat maxillary extraction-socket histology found that excessive misfit-driven densification produces micro-fractures → osteocyte death → resorption without formation → failure. Nascimento 2024 supplies the mechanical, time-zero analogue of the first step in that pathway (bone deformation up to and including visible cracking), measured directly rather than inferred from post-healing histology. Different species/bed/material (synthetic polyurethane vs living rat bone), same conceptual mechanism (excess mechanical compression → structural bone injury).
- **Trisi 2011** (`trisi-2011-high-low-implant-torque-histology-sheep`): found no osteocyte necrosis at 110 Ncm in sheep mandibular cortex — a torque-assigned but bed-specific "safe" result. Nascimento 2024 shows that at the mechanical level, the *implant macro-geometry* generating the torque (not the Ncm number alone) determines whether the bone bed is pushed into elastic compression (Trisi's safe zone) or structural failure (this paper's machined-implant cracking) — reinforcing the wiki's central reframing that torque is a readout of what resisted it, not an independent dose.
- **`high-insertion-torque-primary-stability-crestal-bone-overview`**: this paper adds mechanical/design-level granularity (surface, thread, body shape) underneath the overview's site/compartment/horizon reframing — it does not resolve the overview's human-evidence gap (no held study randomizes torque in humans) but strengthens the mechanistic side of "what determines whether compression is destructive."

## 7. Glossary

- **Insertion Torque (IT, 삽입 토크)**: rotational resistance measured while seating an implant into an osteotomy; here measured with a digital torque driver.
- **Strain Gauge Extensometry (스트레인 게이지 신장계)**: a mechanical measurement technique using a bonded strain gauge to record real-time deformation (strain) of a material — here, the synthetic bone block — during implant insertion.
- **D2 bone density**: a bone-density classification (Misch/Lekholm-Zarb scale) representing dense trabecular bone with thick cortical plate; polyurethane test blocks are manufactured to mechanically approximate this density class for standardized bench testing.
- **Thread profile — triangular vs trapezoidal**: cross-sectional shape of the implant's screw thread; trapezoidal (square-shouldered) threads engage more bone surface per turn than triangular (V-shaped) threads, increasing insertion resistance.
- **Body shape — cylindrical vs conical**: cylindrical implants have a constant diameter along their length; conical (tapered) implants narrow apically, creating progressive compression as they seat, which raises insertion torque relative to a cylindrical body of comparable size.
- **Machined surface**: an implant surface left as-turned (not textured or roughened by acid-etching or blasting) — historically the earliest dental implant surface finish, generally associated with slower/weaker early osseointegration than roughened surfaces in the broader implant-surface literature.
- **Acid etching**: a surface-roughening treatment (typically with strong acids) that increases surface area and micro-roughness, generally associated with improved bone apposition compared to machined surfaces.

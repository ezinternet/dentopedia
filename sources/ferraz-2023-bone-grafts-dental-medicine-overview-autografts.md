## Three-line Summary
Narrative review of bone graft materials in dentistry and oral/maxillofacial surgery, comparing autografts, allografts, xenografts, and synthetic substitutes across defect size, handling, and biological performance.
Autografts remain the gold standard (osteogenic, osteoinductive, osteoconductive) but carry donor-site morbidity and supply limits; xenografts (e.g., Bio-Oss, bovine hydroxyapatite, Ca/P 1.67) are the most used by dentists, while synthetic ceramics/calcium-phosphate cements/bioactive glasses offer availability and moldability but lack osteoinductivity in critical-size (>5 mm) defects.
Best new-bone results came from scaffolds incorporating mesenchymal cells and growth factors; the authors stress that cross-study comparison is difficult and that >30-year-documented xenografts still dominate clinical evidence, with human comparative trials needed for critical defects.

## 2. Key Contributions
- Unifying comparison of the four bone-graft classes (autograft, allograft, xenograft, synthetic) on the shared axes of osteogenesis / osteoinduction / osteoconduction, supply, handling, cost, and ethical/religious issues.
- Restates the seven requirements of an ideal graft: unlimited supply without donor compromise, osteogenesis promotion, no host immune response, rapid revascularization, osteoinduction, osteoconduction, and complete replacement by bone of comparable quantity/quality.
- Frames clinical indications: dental implants, ridge augmentation, sinus lift, socket preservation, and periodontal surgery; small defects regenerate spontaneously, while extensive defects/pathological fractures/infection require a substitute bone.
- Catalogues commercial products by class (e.g., allografts DBX®, Grafton™, Puros®; xenografts Bio-Oss®, Endobon®, Cerabone®; synthetics BonePlast®, Vitoss®, PerioGlass®) with source/chemical constitution, advantages, and disadvantages.
- Reviews the in vivo/clinical evidence comparing xenografts vs synthetics across rodent, minipig, rabbit, dog, and human models, and highlights why translation and cross-study comparison are unreliable.

## 3. Methodology and Architecture
- **Design**: Narrative (non-systematic) review article — no formal search strategy, no PRISMA flow, no meta-analysis; structured as a material-class overview plus a discussion of representative in vivo/clinical studies.
- **n**: No single cohort. As illustrative evidence the paper analyses nine human publications [26,27,30,34,39,41,122,126,127] plus selected animal studies (e.g., rat MD05 study: n = 60 synthetic, n = 72 xenogeneic specimens; up to eight titanium hemispheres per minipig calvaria).
- **Outcomes**: Qualitative and histologic/histomorphometric comparison of new bone formation, material resorption/replacement, inflammatory/foreign-body reaction, and osteoconductive behavior; no pooled effect size reported.

## 4. Key Results and Benchmarks
- **Autografts**: Gold standard — biocompatible, non-toxic, osteogenic, osteoinductive, osteoconductive, and most rapidly vascularized; combining cortical (mechanical support) + medullary (osteogenic) bone is advantageous. Drawbacks: uncertain prognosis, donor-site surgery, infection risk, insufficient quantity/quality with age, metabolic disease, osteoporosis, diabetes; reserved for critical cases (>5 mm defects, jaw reconstruction, congenital defects, tumors) requiring hospitalization and a multidisciplinary team.
- **Allografts**: Same-species, screened/processed/banked bone (living or cadaveric; cortical, cancellous, cortico-cancellous). Source of type I collagen and bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) → osteoinductive and osteoconductive but **not** osteogenic; processing degrades biological/mechanical properties. Controversies: immune rejection, blood incompatibility, disease/tumor-cell transmission, high processing cost, religious issues; not a first choice in dentistry.
- **Xenografts**: Most used by dentists, documented for >3 decades. Osteoconductive via inorganic hydroxyapatite (HA) after organic removal; bovine and porcine most common (also equine, coral, eggshell). Chemical similarity to human bone: calcium/phosphate ratio **1.67**. Bio-Oss® (bovine HA) is the best-known product. Advantages: low cost, high availability, osteoconduction. Disadvantages: ethical/religious/health concerns, disease-transmission risk, disputed osteoinductivity (reported residual collagen/proteins and multinucleated foreign-body reactions), and need for a membrane to contain particulate material.
- **Synthetic materials**: Advantages — biocompatibility, osteoconduction, injectability, moldability, easy handling, minimally invasive single surgery, scar reduction, lower infection risk, and scalable manufacture; bone formation depends on controllable composition, particle size/shape, and porosity. Key limitation: no osteoinductivity → poor for critical-size defects; mitigated by incorporating growth factors, cells, autogenous bone, or therapeutic elements. Tissue-engineering triad: (i) scaffold/carrier, (ii) biological components (growth factors/drugs), (iii) cells.
- **Synthetic subclasses**: Calcium-phosphate cements (brushite sets faster, absorbs more water, lower tensile/compressive/shear resistance; apatite from TTCP/α-TCP, slower); calcium-phosphate ceramics (HA, α-TCP, β-TCP, biphasic calcium phosphate (BCP), amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP)); HA Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2 — close to bone mineral, osteoconductive without local/systemic toxicity, HA nanoparticles <100 nm with higher surface activity and affinity for growth factors/antibiotics/anti-resorptive drugs; β-TCP sintered below ~1125 °C and more resorbable than HA, α-TCP more soluble/reactive (favored in cements); calcium sulphate hemihydrate (plaster of Paris) used as filler since the mid-1920s and as a drug carrier; bioactive glasses (silica/calcium/disodium oxide) releasing Ca and silicate ions, forming an amorphous surface layer, with good osteoconductivity and little inflammatory/foreign-body reaction; polymers PLGA/PLA — degradable but mechanically weak and poorly osteoconductive alone, used as carriers and to improve CPC/bioactive-glass handling and injectability.
- **Evidence-comparison examples**: Rat MD05 (β-TCP coated with rhGDF-5) outperformed bovine bone mineral at 6 weeks; Jensen found β-TCP (Ceros®) resorbed with more new bone than Bio-Oss® at 8 weeks while Bio-Oss® was incorporated without reduction; dog studies favored small-particle xenografts (150–200 µm) and mesenchymal-cell-seeded BCP; the best new-bone results overall were with incorporated mesenchymal cells and growth factors. Host species, defect model, particle size, and follow-up length prevented direct comparison.

## 5. Limitations and Future Work
- Narrative, non-systematic review without a formal search strategy or quantitative synthesis — conclusions carry selection-bias risk and are descriptive, not pooled-effect evidence.
- Various authors disagree on whether xenografts are osteoinductive (absence of organic/cellular content vs detected proteins/collagen), and manufacturer claims of fully organic-free material are contested by histological findings.
- Animal models (rodents, rabbits, minipigs, dogs) differ from human bone and fail to mimic the oral environment and alveolar bone; reporting quality in included reviews was generally low; translation to humans is unreliable.
- Newer synthetic materials remain largely in preclinical phases; clinical trials still favor >30-year-documented xenografts, and recent-technology materials are not yet commercialized.
- Future: nanotechnology-enabled bioactive substitutes that release substances or inhibit osteolysis; human comparative studies are needed to show whether synthetics outperform xenografts in regeneration/remodeling, especially for critical defects >5 mm and defects needing greater mechanical resistance.

## 7. Glossary
- **자가골 (Autograft)**: Bone harvested from the patient's own body; osteogenic, osteoinductive, and osteoconductive gold standard, limited by donor-site morbidity and supply.
- **동종골 (Allograft)**: Bone from another individual of the same species, processed and stored in bone banks; osteoinductive/osteoconductive but not osteogenic.
- **이종골 (Xenograft)**: Bone of animal origin (bovine, porcine, equine, coral, eggshell); osteoconductive inorganic HA with Ca/P ratio 1.67.
- **골유도 (Osteoinduction)**: Induction of osteogenesis from osteocompetent cells in connective tissue or cartilage.
- **골전도 (Osteoconduction)**: Bony ingrowth from local osseous tissue onto a material's surface.
- **골형성 (Osteogenesis)**: New bone formation by living cells capable of differentiating into bone.
- **하이드록시아파타이트 (Hydroxyapatite, HA)**: Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2 ceramic chemically similar to bone mineral; osteoconductive and biocompatible.
- **인산칼슘 시멘트 (Calcium-Phosphate Cement, CPC)**: Injectable, self-hardening bioactive cement; brushite (fast-setting) vs apatite (slow-setting) variants.
- **이상칼슘인산염 (Tricalcium Phosphate, TCP)**: α-TCP and β-TCP ceramics; β-TCP more resorbable than HA, α-TCP more soluble/reactive.
- **생활성유리 (Bioactive Glass, BG)**: Silica-calcium-sodium oxide material releasing Ca/silicate ions and forming an amorphous surface layer that binds bone.
- **임계크기 결손 (Critical-size defect)**: Defect >5 mm that will not regenerate spontaneously without intervention.
- **조직공학 (Tissue Bioengineering)**: Scaffold/carrier + biological components (growth factors/drugs) + cells triad used to improve new-bone formation.

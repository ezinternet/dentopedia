## Why Ingested

기존 [[digital-workflow/alkadi-2023-intraoral-scanner-accuracy-factors]]가 구강스캐너(Intraoral Scanner, IOS) 정확도에 영향을 주는 광학기술·술자경험 인자를 다뤘다면, 본 critical review는 같은 "no single device universally superior / 술자 경험이 핵심 변수" 결론을 **교정과(orthodontics) 임상시험 문헌(2013–2019)만으로 독립적으로 재확인**하면서, 정확도 축에는 없던 환자 선호도·편의성·chairside 시간·학습곡선 축을 추가한다. Systematic review는 아니지만(PROSPERO 없음, risk-of-bias 평가 없음), 7개 DB 전수 검색(inception–2020.10)에 기반한 넓은 범위의 서술적 종합이라 교정과 IOS 도입 판단에 참고할 사각지대(환자 인자·술자 학습곡선)를 메운다.

## Three-line Summary
Critical/narrative review (7 databases searched from inception to October 2020, plus hand-searched gray literature; no PRISMA/PROSPERO, no risk-of-bias assessment) synthesizing orthodontic clinical trials on intraoral scanner (IOS) accuracy, reproducibility, duration, patient comfort/preference, and operator experience versus conventional (alginate/PVS) impressions.

Accuracy and duration results across included studies were contradictory — some studies found IOS as accurate as alginate, others found conventional impressions significantly more precise, with no clear time advantage for either method; patients generally preferred digital scanning and reported reduced gag reflex, but operator experience/learning curve was repeatedly identified as a major factor affecting outcomes.

Clinical implication: IOS adoption in orthodontics is feasible and generally preferred by patients, but the evidence base is heterogeneous and operator-dependent — more high-quality comparative studies are needed before firm accuracy/efficiency claims can be made.

## 세줄요약
서술적 critical review(7개 DB, inception–2020.10 전수검색 + 회색문헌 손검색; PRISMA·PROSPERO 없음, 비뚤림위험 평가 없음): 교정과 임상시험을 대상으로 구강스캐너(IOS)의 정확도·재현성·소요시간·환자 편의/선호·술자 경험을 알지네이트/PVS 재래식 인상과 비교 종합.

포함 연구 간 정확도·소요시간 결과가 상충 — 일부 연구는 IOS가 알지네이트만큼 정확하다 했고, 다른 연구는 재래식 인상이 유의하게 더 정밀하다 했으며, 시간 면에서도 어느 한쪽의 뚜렷한 우위는 없었음; 환자는 대체로 디지털 스캔을 선호하고 구역반사 감소를 보고했으나, 술자 경험·학습곡선이 결과에 영향을 미치는 주요 인자로 반복 확인됨.

임상적 의미: 교정과에서 IOS 도입은 실현 가능하고 환자 선호도가 높지만, 근거 기반이 이질적이고 술자 의존적이므로 정확도·효율성에 대한 확정적 결론 전에 더 많은 고품질 비교연구가 필요함.

## 2. Key Contributions
- Synthesizes orthodontic-specific IOS literature (2013–2019 clinical trials) across accuracy, reproducibility, duration, patient time-perception, comfort, operator experience, and preference — a broader multi-parameter scope than the accuracy-only or preference-only systematic reviews it cites as its own motivation (refs [22] accuracy-only SR, [23] patient-experience-only SR, both by overlapping authorship).
- Documents that accuracy findings across the included studies are genuinely contradictory: some studies show IOS (Lythos, guided scanning) as accurate as or more precise than alginate; others show conventional impressions significantly more precise than digital.
- Reports concrete patient-preference and comfort figures from included primary studies (see Results below), and identifies operator experience/learning curve as a recurring, insufficiently characterized confound across the literature.
- Lists the specific IOS devices evaluated across the included studies: TRIOS Classic, TRIOS 3 Mono (3Shape), LAVA C.O.S. (3M ESPE), CEREC Omnicam (Sirona), iOC (Cadent), CS3600 (Carestream), iTero Element (Align), Lythos (Ormco).

## 3. Methodology and Architecture
- **Design**: Critical review (author-labeled), not a systematic review — no PRISMA statement, no PROSPERO registration, no formal risk-of-bias/quality appraisal of included studies.
- **Databases**: PubMed, CENTRAL, Cochrane Reviews, Scopus, Web of Science, Clinical Trials, Proquest — searched from inception until October 2020, no restriction on study design, year, or location; hand-searching of gray literature; reference-list checking of eligible studies and relevant reviews.
- **Eligibility**: Studies evaluating clinical parameters of IOS (accuracy, reproducibility, duration, operator experience) and person-reported outcomes (perceived time, comfort, preference); animal studies, non-comparative studies, and studies using dry skulls/phantoms/reference models excluded.
- **n**: Not stated as a single pooled number — majority of included studies were cross-over or parallel-group clinical trials; individual study characteristics tabulated in the paper's Supplementary Table S2 (not extracted here — not accessible from the main-text PDF).
- **Outcomes**: Accuracy, reproducibility/reliability, duration (chairside/processing time), patients' perception of time, feeling of comfort (incl. gag reflex, taste/smell/heat, breathing difficulty, powder-related discomfort), operator experience/learning curve, and preference (patient and orthodontist).

## 4. Key Results and Benchmarks
- **Accuracy**: Contradictory across studies [refs 24–35]. Lythos digital models as accurate as conventional for linear/occlusal measurements [26]. Guided-scanning in vivo precision superior to alginate in some trials [27]; Duvert et al. found IOS (iTero, Lythos, TRIOS) precision under ideal conditions lower than conventional materials digitized extraorally [28]. Grunheid and Sfondrini confirmed IOS data as accurate as alginate for orthodontic use [24,25]; other studies found conventional impression materials significantly more precise [29,30]. Maxillary scans less accurate than mandibular [31]. Rhee et al.: 2D deviations smaller with conventional impressions than IOS; second premolar showed the greatest 3D deviations vs. second molar [32]. IOS vs. CBCT 3D models: IOS statistically/clinically acceptable for all direct and indirect measurements; CBCT tended to underestimate lower-arch measurements (still within clinically acceptable limits) [33]. iTero Element and 3Shape TRIOS both clinically adequate accuracy [34]; TRIOS reported higher accuracy than CEREC in another study [35].
- **Reproducibility**: Excellent in three studies [28,36,37]. In vivo vs. ex vivo scanning reproducibility comparable (slight difference) [28,36]. Same reproducibility for conventional and digital models on linear measurements [38]. Both IOS and CBCT scanning of alginate impressions valid/reproducible [39]. Indirect alginate impressions showed statistically significantly higher reproducibility than direct digital impressions, but both proved clinically equivalent for diagnostic purposes [40].
- **Duration**: Contradictory results overall [2,24,25,41–43]. Burhardt et al.: alginate fastest chairside, then CEREC, then LAVA (LAVA longest); no significant difference in chairside time reported among the tested group orders [41]. Multiple studies confirm alginate chairside time shorter than digital [2,24,42]; another confirms alginate requires the least time vs. IOS and PVS [43]. Sfondrini et al.: new-generation powder-free scanners reduce both chairside and processing time [25].
- **Patient time perception**: IOS generally perceived as more comfortable in terms of time [2,25,41,42,44]. Patients preferred IOS despite perceiving it as taking longer [41]; patients perceived digital as faster than expected [42]; some patients still considered IOS scanning time too long but favored the digital method overall in time perception [25].
- **Comfort**: Digital methods generally more comfortable than conventional [2,24,25,41,44,45]. Digital and alginate impressions statistically more comfortable than PVS [43]. Gag reflex highly reduced or absent with digital methods in all three studies examining it [2,41,44]. Powder-related discomfort: 64% of patients scanned with LAVA and 74% scanned with CEREC noticed the powder; of those, 14% and 18% respectively reported powder-related discomfort [41].
- **Operator experience**: Learning rate for iTero faster than for TRIOS, but average scan time greater for iTero; TRIOS learning rate slower but mean scan time less and not influenced by clinical experience; TRIOS learning rate varied with patient intraoral characteristics [47]. Practice, clinical experience, and scanned area all affect fidelity of scanned images [48]. Dental hygienists' views toward IOS improved after training [46].
- **Preference**: Majority of patients preferred digital methods [2,44,45,50]; two independent questionnaire studies (Mangano et al., Yuzbasioglu et al.) each found 100% of patients chose the intraoral scanner over conventional [2,44]. Conversely, one study found participants who experienced alginate showed less preference for digital, while iTero/TRIOS participants said they would rather choose a digitally-equipped orthodontist [42]; another found participants preferred alginate over IOS [24]. Orthodontist survey: 34% planned to switch to digital study models, 29% did not, 37% undecided [52]; among digital users, 56% reported using intraoral scanners, iTero being most popular (86%) [52]. Scanner-vs-scanner preference: operators generally preferred TRIOS over iTero for ease of use/clinical usefulness, but patients rated iTero higher for comfort/convenience [46]; comparing LAVA and CEREC, 44% of patients preferred CEREC and 39% did not differentiate between the two [41].

## 5. Limitations and Future Work
- **Not a systematic review despite a systematic-style search**: 7-database search with a PRISMA-style flowchart and defined eligibility criteria, but no PRISMA statement, no PROSPERO protocol registration, and — critically — no formal risk-of-bias/quality appraisal of included studies. The authors themselves label it a "critical review," and the Discussion freely interleaves author clinical opinion ("Based on our experience...", "Based on our clinical experience...") with the literature synthesis, which is a narrative-review characteristic, not an SR one.
- Only one paper among those reviewed comprehensively evaluated both accuracy and reliability together [37]; statistical methods differ across included studies (t-test vs. Bland–Altman plot), complicating direct comparison.
- Few studies evaluated complete-arch scans acquired directly in the patient's mouth; whole-upper-jaw digital-impression accuracy remains under-studied [54].
- Comfort assessments did not account for the consistency/quantity of conventional impression material used, nor for retake rates due to mistakes or inaccuracies.
- Patient age/maturity and parental presence are potential confounders for time-perception and comfort self-report (VAS-based) that the underlying studies did not fully control for.
- Manufacturers provide little information about their devices' underlying scanning strategies, limiting mechanistic interpretation of why specific devices differ in accuracy.
- High device/annual-fee cost and network-connectivity requirements remain a practical adoption barrier not resolved by any of the reviewed evidence.

## 7. Glossary
- **IOS**: Intraoral Scanning / Intraoral Scanner — device capturing direct optical impressions in the mouth.
- **PVS**: Polyvinyl Siloxane — a conventional elastomeric impression material.
- **VAS**: Visual Analog Scale — patient-reported outcome measurement tool used for comfort/time-perception in the reviewed studies.
- **CAD/CAM**: Computer-Aided Design/Computer-Aided Manufacturing.
- **Chairside time**: Time spent actively performing the impression/scan procedure with the patient in the chair, as distinct from laboratory/processing time.

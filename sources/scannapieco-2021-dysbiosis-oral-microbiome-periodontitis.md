## Why Ingested

Found while mining the reference list of Wen et al. 2026 ("The effect of dental floss sequence on plaque removal efficacy and periodontal health"), which opens by framing dental plaque biofilm as the etiological driver of gingivitis/periodontitis — this critical assessment is exactly that background mechanism paper. It directly challenges the keystone-pathogen/dysbiosis framing in [[wiki/oral-microbiology/hajishengallis-2012-psd-model-periodontal-disease]], arguing that most common (systemically healthy) periodontitis is better explained by increased biomass of a diverse commensal community ("personalized pathology") than by true dysbiosis in the gut-disease sense — reserving "true dysbiosis" for genetically/systemically compromised hosts (e.g., leukocyte adhesion deficiency, aggressive periodontitis) where diversity actually decreases.

## Three-line Summary

Narrative critical-assessment commentary (JPER, 2021) revisiting whether "microbial dysbiosis" correctly describes the pathogenesis of gingivitis and periodontitis, contrasted against classical gut/skin dysbiosis models.

Most common forms of periodontitis in systemically healthy adults show *increased* microbial diversity relative to health (opposite of classical dysbiotic diseases like IBD/dermatitis, which show *decreased* diversity from pathobiont overgrowth), arguing pathogenesis is driven by increased total biomass of a diverse commensal community ("personalized pathology") rather than a specific pathobiont overgrowth; true reduced-diversity dysbiosis is reserved for genetically susceptible (e.g., leukocyte adhesion deficiency) or systemically compromised hosts.

Clinical implication: monitoring or treating periodontitis based on one or a few "biomarker" species (red complex, keystone pathogens) lacks strong evidentiary support for systemically healthy patients, and most cross-sectional microbiome studies cannot establish causality (no accepted definition of "active" disease, pooled/cross-sectional sampling, no site-level biomass validation).

## 2. Key Contributions

- Argues that the term "dysbiosis," borrowed from GI-tract disease models (IBD, ulcerative colitis, Crohn's — all characterized by *decreased* microbial diversity from pathobiont overgrowth), is a poor fit for most common (chronic/adult) periodontitis, whose associated microbiome almost always shows *increased* diversity relative to periodontal health.
- Proposes that periodontal pathogenesis in systemically healthy hosts is better explained by increased total biomass/metabolic activity of an evolutionarily-stable, highly diverse commensal community ("cooperative virulence") than by overgrowth of a few keystone pathobionts.
- Distinguishes "true" dysbiosis (reduced diversity) — seen in genetically susceptible hosts (leukocyte adhesion deficiency) or aggressive periodontitis with tissue-invasive species — from the diversity-increasing shifts typical of common periodontitis.
- Introduces the framing of periodontal pathogenesis as "personalized pathology": a variable, patient-specific constellation of microbes and host risk factors driving site-specific, time-limited ("burst") tissue destruction.
- Surveys the historical arc from Koch's single-pathogen postulates → non-specific plaque hypothesis (Socransky, 1970s) → specific plaque hypothesis (Loesche) → red complex (Socransky 1998) → keystone-pathogen/PSD dysbiosis model (Hajishengallis & Lamont 2012) — and critically re-examines the evidentiary basis for the last of these.
- Discusses an evolutionary framing: periodontal inflammation/bone loss may represent an evolutionarily "normal" host response to contain a diverse indigenous microbiota rather than an inherently pathological process, given ancient humans' shorter lifespans.

## 3. Methodology and Architecture

Narrative/critical-assessment commentary synthesizing historical microbiology literature (Pasteur/Koch-era single-pathogen model), classical dental-plaque ecology studies (Socransky, Loesche, Löe experimental gingivitis model, Gibbons attachment studies), ancient-calculus paleomicrobiology, cross-sectional and longitudinal human oral 16S/metagenomic/metatranscriptomic microbiome studies, and comparative dysbiosis literature from other body sites (gut, skin, vagina, lower/upper respiratory tract). No original data generated; this is a "35,000-foot" conceptual re-analysis, explicitly contrasting the periodontal microbiome literature against accepted dysbiosis criteria from other chronic disease models.

## 4. Key Results and Benchmarks

- Classical dysbiotic diseases (IBD, ulcerative colitis, Crohn's, dermatitis) are characterized by *decreased* microbial diversity from overgrowth of a limited number of taxa; bacterial vaginosis and asthma-associated lower-airway dysbiosis show *increased* diversity but in the context of a normally sparse, low-diversity healthy baseline.
- In periodontitis, health-associated species are not generally lost — they simply become a smaller fraction of an increased total plaque biomass — the opposite diversity direction from most dysbiotic disease models.
- Even the most abundant disease-associated species (e.g., red-complex members) still represent <4% of total microbial species involved in periodontitis at the species level; findings vary widely between individuals and studies.
- Neither increased probing depth (reduced local O2 tension) nor bleeding on probing (inflammation marker) reliably correlates with shifts in microbial community relative abundance/structure — undermining the "local inflammatory/hypoxic microenvironment drives dysbiosis" hypothesis.
- Host systemic background (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, diabetes) can shift microbiome composition independent of periodontal disease severity — a "healthy periodontium + altered plaque" pattern that does not meet the definition of dysbiosis.
- Smoking-associated reduced subgingival diversity is one candidate for "true" periodontal dysbiosis, but Gram-negative anaerobe enrichment from smoking did not always translate into periodontal disease in a separate cohort from the same investigators — weakening a simple diversity-reduction-causes-disease argument.
- True reduced-diversity dysbiosis with tissue-invasive species is documented in leukocyte adhesion deficiency and aggressive periodontitis — genetically/host-susceptibility-driven exceptions to the general increased-diversity pattern of common periodontitis.

## 5. Limitations and Future Work

- Acknowledged as a narrative commentary, not a systematic review — no formal search strategy or quality appraisal of cited studies.
- Notes that most periodontal microbiome studies are cross-sectional (cannot establish causality), lack a formal/accepted definition of "active" periodontitis (so sampling likely occurs during inactive phases), use pooled samples (diluting site-level taxon proportions), and rarely validate relative-abundance sequencing data against absolute biomass (qPCR/cultivation).
- Calls for longitudinal, integrated multiomics studies (metagenomics + metatranscriptomics) combining site-specific host and microbiota profiles, new methods to define periodontal disease *activity* (not just presence), and imaging/biogeography methods to localize taxa spatially within subgingival plaque relative to site-specific disease activity.
- Explicitly limited to "common" (chronic/adult) periodontitis in systemically healthy hosts; does not claim the dysbiosis framework is wrong for genetically susceptible or systemically compromised patients.

## 7. Glossary

- **Dysbiosis**: A state of microbiota imbalance (classically, reduced diversity from pathobiont overgrowth) considered incompatible with host health; originally described for GI-tract disease.
- **Keystone pathogen**: A low-abundance species proposed to disproportionately drive dysbiotic community remodeling and disease (e.g., P. gingivalis in the PSD model).
- **Red complex**: The Socransky (1998) triad of P. gingivalis, T. forsythia, and T. denticola, historically implicated as the principal periodontal pathogens.
- **Non-specific plaque hypothesis**: The view (Socransky, 1970s) that all bacteria in plaque collectively contribute to periodontal tissue destruction, rather than one or a few specific species.
- **Specific plaque hypothesis**: Loesche's proposal that particular bacterial species are causally linked to periodontal disease.
- **Personalized pathology**: This paper's proposed framing — each patient has a variable, individual constellation of microbes and host risk factors driving site- and time-specific ("burst") disease activity.
- **Pathobiont**: A normally commensal organism that becomes pathogenic under permissive host/environmental conditions.

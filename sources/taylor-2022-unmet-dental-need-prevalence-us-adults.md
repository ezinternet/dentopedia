## One-line Summary
Cross-sectional (MEPS 2016, n=25,444 US adults): 6.0% unmet dental need; dental insurance reduced likelihood by 1.7 pp; low-income adults 3.3 pp more likely — cost and coverage are the primary structural determinants.

## 한줄요약
단면연구(MEPS 2016, n=25,444 미국 성인): 미충족 치과 수요 유병률 6.0%; 치과보험이 1.7 pp 감소, 저소득 성인이 3.3 pp 더 많음 — 비용·보험이 주요 구조적 결정인자.

## 1. Document Information
- Journal: Community Dentistry and Oral Epidemiology, Volume 49, Issue 4, pp. 346–353 (2022; epub 2020 Dec 4)
- Setting: US nationally representative cross-sectional (MEPS 2016)
- Funding: NIH Extramural (research support acknowledged)

## 2. Key Contributions
- National prevalence estimate: 6.0% (95% CI 5.5–6.5) self-reported unmet dental need among US adults in 2016
- Insurance effect: dental insurance → 1.7 pp reduction (95% CI −2.8 to −0.6)
- Income gradient fully characterized: low income +3.3 pp, poor/near-poor +4.2 pp vs high income
- Racial paradox documented: Hispanic (−1.7 pp) and non-Hispanic Black (−1.1 pp) less likely to report unmet need than white adults — likely reflects lower perceived threshold for dental need, not better access
- Smoking, education, health status, chronic disease, marital status all independently associated
- First nationally representative study to apply Andersen's Behavioral Model to adult unmet dental need with this level of granularity

## 3. Methodology and Architecture
- Study design: Cross-sectional; Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS) 2016, Household Component
- n=25,444 adults ≥18 years (excluded: under-18, n=9,211)
- Primary outcome: binary self-report of delayed or unmet dental need in preceding 12 months ("unable to get" or "delayed in getting" dental care believed necessary)
- Theoretical framework: Andersen's Behavioral Model — predisposing (age, sex, race, education, marital, smoking), enabling (income, insurance), need (chronic conditions, health status)
- Analysis: chi-square + multivariate logit with marginal effects (absolute risk differences, not ORs)
- Dental insurance: binary (private dental coverage yes/no); income: 4-category federal poverty level grouping

## 4. Key Results and Benchmarks
- Overall unmet dental need: 6.0% (95% CI 5.5–6.5)
- Dental insurance: −1.7 pp (95% CI −2.8 to −0.6) vs uninsured
- Income effects vs high-income (≥400% FPL):
  - Middle income (200–400% FPL): +2.3 pp (95% CI 1.2–3.4)
  - Low income (125–200% FPL): +3.3 pp (95% CI 1.7–5.0)
  - Poor/near-poor (<125% FPL): +4.2 pp (95% CI 2.7–5.7)
- Race: Hispanic −1.7 pp (CI −2.8 to −0.6); Non-Hispanic Black −1.1 pp (CI −2.1 to −0.1) vs White
- Smoking, lower education, poorer health, chronic disease, being unmarried: all associated with higher unmet need

## 5. Limitations and Future Work
- Self-reported outcome: patients may disagree with clinical assessment of "need"
- Single year (2016) — doesn't capture temporal trends
- Binary insurance variable doesn't capture insurance generosity (breadth of coverage)
- Racial effect direction (lower reported need in minorities) likely reflects measurement bias, not better access
- Future research: compare patient vs provider perceptions of dental need; race/ethnicity and perception formation

## 6. Related Work
- Prior US estimate: 5.5% unmet dental need in 2007 (Bloom et al.)
- Vulnerable populations: unmet need much higher in HIV patients (14–49%), pregnant women (25%), homeless (41%)
- Medicare provides no dental benefits — a structural gap for adults ≥65 with low income
- Andersen Behavioral Model applied to dental care utilization

## 7. Glossary
- **MEPS**: Medical Expenditure Panel Survey — nationally representative US survey of families/individuals on health conditions, services, and costs
- **FPL**: Federal Poverty Level — US income threshold system for program eligibility
- **Andersen's Behavioral Model**: framework classifying health service use determinants into predisposing characteristics, enabling resources, and perceived need
- **Marginal effects**: absolute risk differences (percentage points), more interpretable than odds ratios for prevalence outcomes

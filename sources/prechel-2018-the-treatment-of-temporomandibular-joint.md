## Why Ingested

This is the only guideline-level source in the vault for the **reduction technique itself** — the acute manual-reduction algorithm, the extraoral-vs-intraoral success-rate split, and the 3–4 week surgical crossover point are documented nowhere else on the TMJ-dislocation axis, where the existing pages ([[tmj/kapos-2020-temporomandibular-disorders-aetiology-diagnosis-review]], [[tmj/falletti-2026-update-trismus-etiology-diagnosis-treatment]]) treat dislocation only as one cause inside a wider TMD/trismus differential. It also fills the iatrogenic-prevention gap: its GoR B recommendation to screen jaw mobility before and after intubation and to ask about prior dislocations before general anaesthesia is the practice-relevant counterweight to the IANB-complication case held at [[tmj/khalifeh-2024-trismus-following-inferior-alveolar-nerve-block]].

## Three-line Summary

Systematic review published as an AWMF/DGMKG clinical practice guideline (Marburg/Rostock/Hamburg, Dtsch Arztebl Int 2018;115:59–64): PubMed/Cochrane/Embase/ZB Med searched from May 2014 with two updates (Aug 2015, Nov 2016), 24 650 hits screened down to 136 included studies, evidence graded on the Oxford CEBM LoE I–V scheme with GoR A/B/0 and structured interdisciplinary consensus; German TMJ-dislocation incidence estimated at ≥25/100 000/year (3% of all documented dislocations).

Acute non-traumatic dislocation is reduced manually without delay, with the Hippocratic and wrist-pivot techniques judged equivalent and extraoral reduction succeeding in 96.7% of unilateral vs only 54.5% of bilateral cases; manual reduction usually fails beyond 3–4 weeks of persistence (→ surgical reduction), ~30% of presented dislocations are persistent, recurrence follows the first episode in 22%, and recurrent cases respond to autologous blood injection (~80% symptom-free at 12 months) or prolotherapy (91% recurrence-free at 6 months).

Clinically this fixes a defensible chairside default — sitting position, head stabilized with a headrest, bite blocks and gloves, fingers lateral to (never on) the molar occlusal surfaces, and a medication-free first attempt before muscle relaxants/analgesics, analgosedation or general anaesthesia — but the authors concede that no more than a few RCTs exist, so the high-grade recommendations still cannot be issued and the recurrence-prevention data rest on case series of ≤30 patients.

## 2. Key Contributions

- Establishes a **graded, literature-based management algorithm** for non-fractured TMJ dislocation — the first German CPG on the entity — covering diagnosis, acute reduction, persistent dislocation, and recurrence prevention in one document.
- Declares **Hippocratic and wrist-pivot manual reduction equivalent** on success rate, reduction-related pain, and reduction time, and quantifies the extraoral route: **96.7% success in unilateral vs 54.5% in bilateral dislocation**, so the extraoral approach is reserved for raised bite or infection risk.
- Fixes the **3–4 week surgical crossover**: manual reduction attempts are usually no longer successful beyond that interval, at which point redressive/eminectomy/condylectomy/endoprosthesis options are considered.
- Quantifies the natural history: **~30% of physician-presented dislocations are persistent** and **22% recur** after the first episode, making recurrence prevention a guideline target in its own right.
- Proposes **recurrent TMJ dislocation as a new (currently off-label) indication for botulinum toxin** into the lateral pterygoid — the authors explicitly petition for the indication to be added (GoR A; LoE V; expert consensus).

## 3. Methodology and Architecture

- **Design**: Systematic review without meta-analysis, issued as a clinical practice guideline. No pooled effect estimates, no forest plots — recommendation strength is asserted, not computed.
- **Databases**: PubMed, Cochrane, Embase, ZB Med. English and German literature from 2000 onward, search term "temporomandibular joint dislocation", initial search May 2014.
- **Screening flow** (eFigure): 24 650 hits → duplicates removed → 1 296 (title/heading screening, excluding central dislocation, dislocation fractures, subluxation) → 150 (abstract screening, excluding topics too wide or too narrow) → 104 → plus reference-list screening of included articles and two literature updates (August 2015, November 2016) → **136 articles included** and summarized in tables.
- **Evidence grading**: Oxford Centre for Evidence-based Medicine criteria (eTable 1) — LoE I–V with quality suffixes `+` (good quality or n > 100) and `−` (poor quality or n < 10), `a` (meta-analysis/review) and `b` (single study); Grade of Recommendation A (strong, "shall") / B ("should") / 0 (open, "may"); consensus strength graded as strong (>95%), majority (75–95%), consensus (50–75%), or none (<50%).
- **Consensus process**: draft presented to the DGMKG Guideline Group Temporomandibular Joint Surgery for structured consensus (unanimously adopted as a proposal), then interdisciplinary Delphi with external professional societies, AWMF online publication 30 June 2016. Two later TMJ-dislocation studies were checked and did not require revision.
- **Outcomes**: not a trial — the outcomes are recommendation statements (GoR + LoE) covering diagnostic workup, reduction success, pain, reduction time, recurrence rate, and treatment complications.

## 4. Key Results and Benchmarks

### Epidemiology and natural history

| Quantity | Value | Source note |
|---|---|---|
| Share of all documented dislocations | 3% | — |
| German annual incidence | ≥25 / 100 000 population | 2017 DGMKG e-mail survey; "as common as giant-cell arteritis" |
| Typical age band | 25–45 years | with a second incidence peak in older patients |
| Persistent among physician-presented cases | ~30% | low incidence → recommendations rest on case series/reports |
| Recurrence after first dislocation | 22% | Key Messages rounds this to "about 20%" |

### Diagnosis

- Cardinal symptoms: impaired occlusion / inability to close the jaws, plus pain. On examination an **empty TMJ socket**; malnutrition signs in long-term dislocation.
- First-time, non-traumatic dislocation is diagnosable from **history and physical examination alone** (GoR 0; LoE IIIa–V+).
- **Imaging (OPG, CBCT/DVT, MRI) when symptoms allow other differential diagnoses**, to exclude facial fracture and inform treatment planning (GoR B; LoE I–V+).
- Easily missed in sedated patients, post-trauma patients, patients with dementia, and persistent dislocations; milder symptoms plus reduced communication in multimorbid older patients make delayed diagnosis common.
- **GoR B; LoE IV–V+**: ask about previous dislocations and risk factors before any general-anaesthesia procedure, and check functional jaw mobility clinically before and after intubation.

### Acute reduction (Figure 2)

| Route / technique | Details | Key numbers | GoR / LoE |
|---|---|---|---|
| Manual reduction first, immediately | every non-traumatic dislocation | earlier reduction → greater success chance | GoR B; LoE IIIa |
| (a) Hippocratic, modified | one side at a time; thumb **lateral** to the dental arch (oblique line); other fingers on the external inferior aspect of the mandible; press **caudally first, then dorsally**; two steps | most commonly used | — |
| (b) Wrist pivot | both sides simultaneously; thumbs on the inferior external chin pressing **cranially**; index/middle fingers on mandibular teeth or the oblique line pressing **caudally**, wrists rotating **ulnarward** | **at least equal** to Hippocratic for success rate, reduction-related pain, and reduction time | — |
| (c) Extraoral | one side at a time (second usually reduces automatically); thumb on the coronoid process with fingers on the mastoid process as abutment; then fingers around the mandibular angle with thumbs on the zygomatic arch, pressing ventrally to increase ipsilateral dislocation | **96.7% unilateral vs 54.5% bilateral**; more painful and time-consuming than intraoral | GoR 0; LoE I |
| Sedation ladder | attempt manual reduction **without any medication** first; on failure retry under muscle relaxants and/or analgesics, or analgosedation or general anaesthesia | — | GoR 0; LoE IIIa–V+ |
| Chairside aids | patient **sitting** with the head stabilized on a headrest; **bite blocks and gloves** to prevent bite injury and associated infection | — | GoR B; LoE IV–V+ and V+ to V |
| Finger placement | fingers **not on, but lateral to** the occlusal surfaces of the mandibular molars | — | GoR B; LoE V; expert consensus |

The extraoral route is recommended **only** where bite or infection risk is raised (dementia, hepatitis C, known IV drug abuse) — otherwise its lower success rate in bilateral cases does not justify it.

### Persistent (chronic) dislocation

- Manual reduction attempts are usually no longer successful after **3–4 weeks** → **surgical reduction** should be considered (GoR B; LoE I–V+).
- Surgical repertoire: redressive methods (articular capsule opened for reduction), eminectomy, condylectomy, special osteotomy techniques, endoprosthesis.
- Refractory cases with complications such as anterior open bite require an individualized approach ranging from functional TMJ surgery to reconstructive and orthognathic surgery (GoR 0; LoE IIIa–V).

### Recurrent dislocation (recurrence-prevention)

| Minimally invasive method | Evidence base | Outcome | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Botulinum toxin into the lateral pterygoid | case series of up to 21 patients | no further dislocations over **≥5 months** after single or multiple injections | **off-label**; manufacturer's warnings to be observed (GoR A; LoE V; expert consensus); authors petition for the new indication |
| Autologous blood injection (into/around the joint) | case series of up to 30 patients | **~80% symptom-free for the next 12 months**; no aggravation reported | postulated fibrosis was **not** confirmed on MRI follow-up; midterm mobility restriction appears to reduce recurrence; exact histopathological mechanism unclear |
| Prolotherapy (syn. sclerotherapy) | one case series, 30 patients | **91% with no recurrence over 6 months** | similar mechanistic assumptions as autologous blood |

- Open surgery is indicated after failure of conservative and/or minimally invasive methods (GoR 0; LoE IIIa–IV+): **eminectomy** (best documented, most commonly used), blocking or sling procedures, and surgery on the capsular ligament complex.
- Minimally invasive injections are primarily indicated in patients with reduced compliance or increased surgical risk; long-term outcomes are often unsatisfactory and invasive management is eventually required.

### Conclusions stated by the authors

- Treatment should be initiated **as early as possible** to limit degenerative change and raise the success chance of conservative/minimally invasive methods (GoR B; LoE IIIa–V+).
- The best treatment for an individual patient must be decided from a thorough history and physical examination, weighing pathogenesis, age, secondary diagnoses, compliance, treatment goal, and care structures (GoR B; LoE IIIa–IIIb−).
- No higher-grade evidence-based recommendations can yet be issued; RCTs and meta-analyses on TMJ-dislocation treatment are **not expected in the foreseeable future**.

## 5. Limitations and Future Work

- **Self-declared evidence ceiling**: the review concludes that only a few RCTs exist on TMJ-dislocation treatment — particularly for minimally invasive and open surgical treatment — so the guideline's most confident statements rest on LoE IIIa–V, and the paper's own abstract concedes "only limited evidence-based conclusions can be drawn."
- **No meta-analysis, no pooled estimates**: the 136 included studies are heterogeneous in design, endpoints, and follow-up, so recommendations are graded but not quantitatively synthesized.
- **Very short follow-up** across the underlying literature undermines recurrence-prevention claims (5–12 months at best).
- **Small case series throughout the recurrent-dislocation section** (≤21 patients for BTX, ≤30 for autologous blood and prolotherapy) — no controlled comparison, no blinding, and recurrence measured by heterogeneous definitions.
- **Off-label pharmacology**: the botulinum-toxin recommendation rests on expert consensus, and the authors themselves are lobbying for the new indication — a stance the guideline body cannot independently adjudicate.
- **Unverified mechanism**: the fibrosis hypothesis underlying autologous blood and prolotherapy was **not** confirmed on MRI, leaving the pathophysiological rationale for both techniques unexplained.
- **German-context epidemiology**: the ≥25/100 000/year incidence derives from a 2017 DGMKG e-mail survey with acknowledged under-reporting, so absolute risk figures should be read as lower bounds.

## 7. Glossary

- **Grade of Recommendation (GoR)**: A = strong recommendation ("shall"), B = recommendation ("should"), 0 = recommendation open ("may"); paired with the strength of the expert consensus that produced it.
- **Level of Evidence (LoE)**: Oxford CEBM hierarchy I (RCT) → V (case report/expert opinion), with `+` = good quality or n > 100 and `−` = poor quality or n < 10, and `a` = meta-analysis/review, `b` = single study.
- **Hippocratic (modified) method**: the classic manual reduction — one side at a time, thumb lateral to the dental arch, remaining fingers on the external inferior mandible, pressure first caudal then dorsal.
- **Wrist pivot method**: both sides reduced simultaneously by rotating the wrists ulnarward, thumbs pressing cranially on the chin and index/middle fingers pressing caudally on the teeth/oblique line.
- **Extraoral reduction**: reduction performed entirely from outside the mouth, using the coronoid process and mastoid process (then mandibular angle and zygomatic arch) as abutments — indicated for unilateral dislocation or raised bite/infection risk.
- **Persistent (chronic) dislocation**: an unreduced dislocation producing irreversible intra- and pericapsular change; the practical threshold for abandoning manual reduction is 3–4 weeks.
- **Autologous blood injection / prolotherapy**: non-surgical recurrence-prevention injections whose proposed mechanism is a deliberate fibrosis-induced restriction of condylar mobility — a mechanism MRI follow-up has not confirmed.

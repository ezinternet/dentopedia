## Three-line Summary

Narrative review (32 articles selected from 20,121 search hits across PubMed/EBSCO/ScienceDirect/Springer/ProQuest/BMJ/SciELO via BIDI UNAM library, through 31 May 2024) on trigeminal neuralgia (TN) diagnosis and therapeutic strategies aimed at dentists.

Carbamazepine (100–1200 mg/day) is the gold-standard first-line pharmacological agent (near-100% symptom reduction reported), oxcarbazepine (900–2400 mg/day) is the substitute on adverse effects; microvascular decompression (MVD) has 92.7% initial success with 0.7% mortality and 2%/year recurrence; radiofrequency rhizotomy gives up to 97% immediate relief but 42% recurrence at 5 years; low-level laser therapy (LLLT) and botulinum toxin A (25–75 IU) are discussed as adjunct/alternative options.

TN incidence is 4.3/100,000/year (women 5.9 vs men 3.4), and because trigger zones (nasolabial area, lips, chin, cheek, gingiva) and provoking stimuli (chewing, tooth brushing) overlap with dental pain, dentists are frequently the first clinicians consulted — the paper argues pharmacological management should remain central in dentistry, reserving surgery for medication-refractory cases.

## 2. Key Contributions

- Dentist-oriented narrative synthesis of TN etiology (idiopathic / classical / secondary), diagnosis, and the full treatment ladder (pharmacological → surgical → alternative/laser).
- Consolidates dosing table for six anticonvulsant/antispasmodic/toxin agents (carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, gabapentin, pregabalin, lamotrigine, phenytoin, baclofen, botulinum toxin) in one reference.
- Explicitly frames the differential-diagnosis stakes for dentists: overlapping trigger zones/stimuli with dental pain make dentists frequent first-contact clinicians for undiagnosed TN.
- Offers a critical discussion contrasting authors who favor early surgical escalation (e.g., Leclercq et al.) versus those favoring sustained pharmacological management (e.g., Wang et al.), concluding that pharmacology should remain central in dental practice.

## 3. Methodology and Architecture

- Narrative (non-systematic) literature review using the BIDI UNAM Digital Library, spanning PubMed, EBSCO, ScienceDirect, Springer, ProQuest, BMJ, Health Library, Elsevier and SciELO.
- Search conducted through 31 May 2024; keyword combinations ("trigeminal neuralgia", "facial pain and trigeminal neuralgia", "trigeminal neuralgia diagnosis", "dental pain and trigeminal neuralgia", "trigeminal neuralgia incidence", "laser therapy in trigeminal neuralgia") retrieved 20,121 total hits.
- No PRISMA flow, no formal quality/risk-of-bias appraisal, no PROSPERO registration — articles were hand-selected ("most recent... with representative information") down to 32 included references. This is a narrative review, methodologically weaker than khan-2023's umbrella systematic review.

## 4. Key Results and Benchmarks

**Epidemiology**: TN incidence 4.3/100,000/year; female:male ratio 5.9:3.4. Right-side predominance in MS-associated TN (not in idiopathic). V2/V3 branches affected in ~36–42% of patients.

**Etiologic classification**: idiopathic (no cause found) / classical (vascular root compression, most common) / secondary (cerebellopontine angle tumor in 15% of secondary cases, or MS).

**Pharmacological dosing table**:
| Drug | Dose range/day |
|---|---|
| Carbamazepine | 100–1200 mg |
| Oxcarbazepine | 900–2400 mg |
| Gabapentin | 900–3600 mg |
| Pregabalin | 75–600 mg |
| Lamotrigine | 100–600 mg |
| Phenytoin | 300–600 mg |
| Baclofen | 25–135 mg |
| Botulinum toxin | 25–75 IU per infiltration |

**Surgical outcomes**: Radiofrequency rhizotomy — up to 97% immediate relief, 42% recurrence at 5 years. Microvascular decompression (MVD) — 92.7% initial success, 0.7% mortality, 2%/year recurrence, <1% major complication rate (cerebellar injury, hearing loss, facial paralysis, CSF fistula); no significant compression found intraoperatively in 15% of explored cases. MVD reported superior to gamma-knife radiosurgery for drug-resistant TN in pain relief and recurrence.

**Alternative/adjunct therapies**: acupuncture shows reduced pain/response-rate/adverse-events benefit vs. carbamazepine only at very-low evidence confidence — insufficient to recommend. BTX-A shows clinically significant benefit vs. placebo (responder proportion, paroxysm frequency, VAS), effect lasting ≥3 months. LLLT proposed as painless, non-invasive, mechanistically linked to reduced histamine/bradykinin/acetylcholine/PGE2 and increased endorphin/ATP/enkephalin mRNA expression, with animal-model evidence for axonal regrowth.

## 5. Limitations and Future Work

- Narrative (not systematic) methodology: no PRISMA, no formal appraisal, no registered protocol — selection of 32/20,121 articles is subjective ("most recent... representative").
- Treatment-success definitions are inconsistently reported across cited primary studies (medical studies: ≥50% pain relief vs. surgical studies: complete relief), which the authors note but do not standardize.
- Alternative treatments (acupuncture, LLLT) are discussed largely from very-low-confidence evidence; authors explicitly state information on their effectiveness is "limited" and call for future studies.
- No new primary data — this is a synthesis of prior literature with editorial commentary/critique of other authors' positions (Leclercq et al., Wang et al., Ang et al., Maarbjerg et al.).

## 6. Related Work

The paper explicitly engages with and critiques prior reviews:
- Leclercq et al. — favors escalation to surgical therapy when drug treatment fails; authors here disagree, calling it an "extreme approach."
- Wang et al. — favors sustained pharmacological therapy (carbamazepine ± topiramate), considers surgery a last-resort "destructive" option; cited as closer to this paper's own stance.
- Ang et al. — advocates less invasive alternatives such as acupuncture despite carbamazepine's gold-standard status.
- Maarbjerg et al. — recommends combination pharmacotherapy (lamotrigine, baclofen, pregabalin, gabapentin) when carbamazepine/oxcarbazepine maximum doses are not tolerated.

## 7. Glossary

- **TN (Trigeminal Neuralgia)**: paroxysmal unilateral facial pain in the distribution of one or more trigeminal nerve branches.
- **V1/V2/V3**: ophthalmic / maxillary / mandibular divisions of the trigeminal nerve.
- **MVD (Microvascular Decompression)**: non-ablative neurosurgical procedure relieving vascular compression of the trigeminal root.
- **Rhizotomy**: ablative percutaneous technique (balloon compression, radiofrequency, or glycerol) destroying trigeminal nerve fibers for pain relief.
- **LLLT (Low-Level Laser Therapy)**: low-intensity photobiomodulation used as a non-invasive adjunct analgesic modality.
- **BTX-A (Botulinum Toxin Type A)**: neurotoxin used via intradermal/submucosal infiltration for refractory TN pain.

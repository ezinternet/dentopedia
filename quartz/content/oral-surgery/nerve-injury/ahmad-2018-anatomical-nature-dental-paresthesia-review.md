## Three-line Summary

This narrative review examines the anatomy of the pterygomandibular fossa, anesthetic agents, and nerve injury mechanisms to explain dental paresthesia following inferior alveolar nerve blocks. The review synthesizes anatomical studies reporting that lingual nerve fascicle counts range from 7 to 39 (average 20) and inferior alveolar nerve fascicles range from 3 to 14 (average 7.2), and that Articaine 4% accounted for 59% of paresthesia cases between 1999-2008 in one retrospective study.

The review concludes that anatomical variations—including accessory foramina, variant nerve branches, multiple mandibular canals, and variable fascicle numbers—combined with anesthetic neurotoxicity and mechanical needle trauma are likely multifactorial causes of paresthesia. No formal meta-analysis or quantitative synthesis was performed.

Clinically, dentists should appreciate the highly variable anatomy of the pterygomandibular fossa, use proper injection technique, and recognize that most paresthesia resolves within days to months, though rare cases persist beyond six months. The lingual nerve is at greater risk (89% of neuropathies) than the inferior alveolar nerve following mandibular blocks.

## Summary

This narrative review by Ahmad (2018) examines the anatomical basis of dental paresthesia following inferior alveolar nerve (IAN) blocks. The review synthesizes anatomical cadaver studies, retrospective case series, and nerve injury classification literature to explain why some patients develop altered sensation—numbness, burning, or tingling—after routine dental anesthesia. Key anatomical findings include variable fascicle counts in the lingual nerve (7–39, mean 20) and IAN (3–14, mean 7.2) at the lingula (Pogrel et al., 2003), and lingual nerve position relative to the lingual crest (direct contact in 62%, superior in 17.6%; Kiesselbach & Chamberlain, 1984). Retrospective studies report that Articaine 4% accounted for 59% of paresthesia cases from 1999–2008 (Gaffen & Haas, 2009), with reported ranges of 33–71% across studies, though animal and cell culture studies do not confirm higher toxicity. Nerve injuries are classified by Seddon (neurapraxia, axonotmesis, neurotmesis) and Sunderland (grades I–V). The author concludes that multiple factors—anatomical variations (accessory foramina, variant branches, multiple mandibular canals, fascicle number differences), anesthetic neurotoxicity, and mechanical needle trauma (including barbed needles)—likely contribute to paresthesia. Most cases resolve within days to months, with rare persistence beyond six months. The lingual nerve is more frequently affected (89% of neuropathies) than the IAN following mandibular blocks.

## Key Contributions

- Reviews pterygomandibular fossa anatomy: borders (mandibular ramus lateral, pterygoid muscles medial, parotid posterior, pterygomandibular raphe anterior), key nerves (lingual nerve, IAN, mylohyoid nerve), and vascular supply
- Documents lingual nerve fascicle counts (7–39, mean 20) and IAN fascicle counts (3–14, mean 7.2) as potential anatomical risk factors (Pogrel et al., 2003)
- Reports Articaine 4% association with paresthesia: 59% of cases 1999–2008 (Gaffen & Haas, 2009), range 33–71% across studies, but animal/cell studies show no higher toxicity
- Describes nerve injury classifications (Seddon 1943; Sunderland 1978) and clinical severity spectrum: neurapraxia (hours–days) → axonotmesis (months) → neurotmesis (complete sensory loss)
- Highlights lingual nerve vulnerability: 89% of neuropathies following mandibular blocks; 4.5% simulated injection penetration rate (Morris et al., 2010); 62% direct lingual crest contact (Kiesselbach & Chamberlain, 1984)

## Methodology

This is a narrative literature review without a systematic search protocol, formal inclusion/exclusion criteria, or PRISMA guidelines. The author synthesizes evidence from cadaveric anatomical studies (Kiesselbach & Chamberlain 1984; Khoury et al. 2010; Morris et al. 2010; Pogrel et al. 2003), retrospective case series of paresthesia reports (Haas & Lennon 1995; Gaffen & Haas 2009; Garisto et al. 2010), nerve injury classification literature (Seddon 1943; Sunderland 1978), and clinical reports of anesthetic complications. No quantitative synthesis, meta-analysis, or heterogeneity assessment was performed. Key anatomical variables examined include nerve position, fascicle counts, vascular relationships, and accessory foramina. Clinical outcomes are derived from voluntary reporting systems, which are subject to reporting bias and denominator uncertainty. Imaging modalities for nerve injury assessment are briefly reviewed (panoramic radiography, CT, magnetic source imaging, ultrasonography, MRN).

Additional findings: Prilocaine 3–4% was second most common anesthetic associated with paresthesia. Most paresthesia resolves within days to months; unresolved cases (>6 months) are rare. IAN damage cases show no signs of needle trauma in approximately half of cases, suggesting neurotoxicity as an alternative or contributing mechanism. Imaging modalities including magnetic resonance neurography (MRN) can distinguish injured from intact nerve fibers and detect intrafascicular hemorrhage.

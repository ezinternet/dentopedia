## Three-line Summary

In vitro study: hyperhydrophilic (alkali/NaOH-treated) sand-blasted acid-etched (SBA) titanium vs untreated SBA titanium, exposed to partially heparinized human whole blood, assessing clot formation, platelet activation, complement activation, and macrophage/monocyte adhesion.

Blood-surface exposure time itself drove increasing platelet activation, clot formation, and complement activation regardless of surface treatment; alkali-treated (hyperhydrophilic) surfaces recruited significantly fewer macrophages/monocytes than untreated surfaces and produced a thicker, organized, layered fibrin clot versus a thin, patchy, unstructured one on untreated titanium.

This is the mechanistic paper [[implants/surface/plasma/becker-2019-lpp-app-plasma-proinflammatory-cytokines]] cites for its hydrophilic-surface/blood-clot reasoning — it does not measure cytokine gene expression or reproduce Becker's 24-h timepoint, so it offers a plausible contributing mechanism (exposure-time-driven complement/coagulation accumulation), not a confirmed explanation, for Becker's cytokine reversal.

## Summary

This paper is the direct mechanistic citation behind Becker 2019's discussion of why hydrophilic/plasma-activated titanium surfaces behave differently in blood contact. Its central finding relevant to the "why does Becker's cytokine suppression reverse at 24 h" question is that **exposure time between blood and any titanium surface — treated or not — drives increasing platelet activation, clot formation, and complement activation**. This suggests that even a surface which is initially anti-inflammatory (fewer adherent macrophages/monocytes, as alkali-treated surfaces show here) sits inside a blood-contact system whose complement/coagulation activation accumulates with time regardless. Applied to Becker's data: an early (1–8 h) plasma-driven cytokine suppression could plausibly be overtaken by a later (24 h) exposure-time-driven complement/coagulation-linked cytokine rise that has nothing to do with the plasma treatment itself reversing in effect — but this paper does not measure cytokines or run to 24 h, so this remains a candidate mechanism, not a demonstrated one. The distinct, thicker, organized clot architecture on hydrophilic surfaces (vs thin/patchy on untreated) is presented by the authors as favorable for later tissue integration, adding a structural rationale alongside the immune-cell-count finding.

## Key Contributions

- Establishes blood-surface exposure time as an independent driver of platelet/complement/coagulation activation, separate from surface-treatment effects — a baseline any time-course immune study on titanium must account for.
- Shows hyperhydrophilic titanium recruits fewer macrophages/monocytes than untreated titanium, in the same early-suppression direction Becker 2019 reports for plasma-treated implants.
- Documents a structurally distinct fibrin clot architecture (thick/organized vs thin/patchy) as a candidate mechanistic link between surface hydrophilicity and osseointegration outcome.

## Methodology

- SBA titanium disks: alkali (NaOH)-treated (hyperhydrophilic) vs untreated.
- Exposed to partially heparinized human whole blood.
- Endpoints: clot formation/architecture (SEM), platelet activation, complement activation (C5a and related markers), macrophage/monocyte adhesion count.

## Results

- Exposure time ↑ → platelet activation ↑, clot formation ↑, complement activation ↑ (both surfaces).
- Macrophage/monocyte adhesion: significantly lower on alkali-treated vs untreated.
- Clot architecture: untreated — thin (<15 µm), patchy, unstructured. Alkali-treated — thick (>30 µm), layered, organized fibrin network with continuous surface cover.

## Three-line Summary

Randomized crossover bench study (typodont/manikin model), 12 dental hygienists, comparing a novel adaptive-handle curette (silicone-covered core, adjustable up to a 30° bend) against a conventional rigid stainless-steel curette (both configured as Barnhart 5/6 universal curettes) during simulated calculus removal.

The adaptive curette produced significantly less total and per-muscle surface electromyography (sEMG) work, preserved pinch/grasp strength post-instrumentation (vs. a significant strength drop with the conventional curette), and scored significantly better on comfort/fatigue VAS, blade adaptation, and grasp-video analysis (all p<0.05).

Findings support handle adaptability — spreading instrumentation load over a larger hand/finger contact area — as a design strategy to reduce musculoskeletal burden during periodontal instrumentation, but this is a first typodont-model study; in-vivo clinical-patient studies are described as already underway.

## 세줄요약

무작위 교차설계(crossover) 벤치 연구(typodont/마네킹 모델), 치과위생사 12명 대상, 새로운 적응형(어댑티브) 손잡이 큐렛(실리콘 코팅 코어, 최대 30° 굴곡 조절) vs 기존 경성 스테인리스 스틸 큐렛(둘 다 Barnhart 5/6 유니버설 큐렛) 비교, 인공 치석 제거 과제로 평가.

적응형 큐렛이 총 근활동량 및 개별 근육별 표면근전도(surface Electromyography, sEMG) 작업량을 유의하게 감소시켰고, 기존 큐렛 사용 후 유의하게 감소한 집기력·파지력(pinch/grasp strength)이 적응형 큐렛에서는 유지됐으며, 편안함·피로도 시각아날로그척도(Visual Analog Scale, VAS)·블레이드 적합도·파지 영상분석에서도 유의하게 우수했음(모두 p<0.05).

손가락·손 접촉면적을 넓혀 부하를 분산시키는 손잡이 적응형 설계전략이 치주기구조작 중 근골격계 부담을 줄일 수 있음을 시사하나, typodont 모의모델의 첫 연구로, 실제 환자 대상 임상연구는 저자들에 따르면 현재 진행 중.

## Summary

Dental hygiene carries the highest occupational prevalence of carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS, ~45% report symptoms) and musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs, 64–96% of hygienists within a 12-month period) among US occupations, driven by repetitive, high-force, non-neutral-wrist periodontal instrumentation. This UC Irvine (Beckman Laser Institute) study tested whether a design fix — a curette handle that adapts to the hand rather than staying rigid — reduces the physiological cost of scaling. Twelve practicing hygienists scaled a standardized, artificial-calculus typodont model with two Barnhart 5/6 universal curettes in a randomized, within-subject crossover: a prototype "adaptive curette" with a bendable (up to 30°), silicone-covered handle, versus a conventional rigid stainless-steel curette. Outcomes combined objective physiological measures (surface electromyography of four hand/forearm muscles, pinch/grasp dynamometry, video-scored grasp and blade positioning) with subjective VAS ratings of comfort and fatigue.

The adaptive curette won on every measured axis: significantly lower total and per-muscle sEMG workload, no significant post-scaling loss of pinch/grasp strength (versus a significant loss with the conventional curette), significantly better comfort/fatigue VAS scores, less joint hypermobility, and better blade angulation/adaptation to the tooth surface (correct 70° angle achieved twice as often). Years of clinical experience, handedness, and test sequence did not significantly influence any outcome. The authors' mechanistic hypothesis is that a handle that conforms to the hand spreads instrumentation force over a larger contact area, lowering pressure per unit area on the fingers — consistent with ergonomics literature from outside dentistry (tool handles for sewing, gardening, manual trades).

Clinically, this is bench/simulation evidence, not a patient trial — it demonstrates reduced physiological strain during a standardized task on an artificial model, not reduced long-term MSD incidence or improved calculus-removal outcomes in real patients. The authors explicitly frame it as a first study, with expanded in-vivo (patient-based) follow-up already underway.

## Key Contributions

- First study to combine sEMG, pinch/grasp dynamometry, VAS, and video-scored technique analysis in one head-to-head curette-handle ergonomics comparison, with all four measurement axes converging on the same result.
- Ties handle adaptability not just to comfort but to technique quality: less joint hypermobility and better, more consistent blade-to-tooth angulation with the adaptive design.
- Validates a 20-minute inter-arm washout (via a dedicated 2-tester pilot study) sufficient to erase carryover fatigue between crossover arms — a reusable methodological detail for future instrument-comparison studies.
- Frames handle-material/shape adaptability as one design lever among several (diameter, hardness, texture, weight, thermal conductivity) known to affect hand-tool ergonomics, while being transparent that this study bundles several of those variables rather than isolating adaptability alone.

## Methodology

- **Design**: Randomized (1:1, random.org), within-subject crossover; unblinded (instrument appearance precluded blinding). UC Irvine IRB exempt (de-identified typodont-model data only).
- **Testers**: 12 practicing dental hygienists (11 F/1 M; 10 right-/2 left-handed), age 24–68 (mean 36.1); experience 2–5 yr (n=3), 6–10 yr (n=3), ≥11 yr (n=6). Excluded: recent hand/wrist injury or diagnosed upper-extremity MSD.
- **Model**: Standardized artificial calculus (18-h fixed hardening time) on 32 teeth in a typodont mounted on a manikin fixed to a dental chair.
- **Instruments**: Curette A (prototype) = Barnhart 5/6 with a bendable (≤30°), silicone-covered handle core. Curette B = conventional rigid stainless-steel Barnhart 5/6 (smaller handle diameter, dimensions matched to popular commercial curettes).
- **Protocol**: 8 min scaling per arm (1 min × 8 standardized typodont segments/sextants), 20-min rest between arms; 1-min instructional video + 5-min accustomization before first adaptive-curette use.
- **Measures**: sEMG (APB, FDI, FPL, EDC; normalized to 15-s MVC; Butterworth-filtered; integrated area-under-curve = total workload); Jamar pinch/grasp dynamometry (3× pre/post per arm); VAS 0–10 comfort/fatigue post-arm; video-scored grasp type, joint hypermobility, blade angulation (target 70°) and shank-tooth-axis parallelism; free-text tester comments.
- **Statistics**: Paired t-tests + repeated-measures ANOVA with covariates (sequence, age/experience); α=0.05 (SPSS 19).

## Results

| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| Comfort/fatigue (VAS) | Curette A significantly better in all categories (hand, wrist, finger comfort; overall fatigue), p<0.05 |
| Pinch/grasp strength | No pre-scaling difference (p>0.05); significantly reduced post-scaling with curette B only, preserved with curette A, p<0.05 |
| Total sEMG workload | Significantly lower with curette A, p<0.05 |
| Per-muscle sEMG (APB, FDI, FPL, EDC) | Each muscle significantly lower work with curette A, p<0.05 |
| Joint hypermobility/hyperextension | Significantly more frequent with curette B, p<0.05 |
| Blade-shank parallelism to tooth long axis | Significantly better with curette A, p<0.05 |
| Blade adaptation / correct 70° angle | Significantly better with curette A; correct angle achieved ~2× as often, p<0.05 |
| Covariates (experience, handedness, sequence) | No significant effect on any outcome, p>0.05 |

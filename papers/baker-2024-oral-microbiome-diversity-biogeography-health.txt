Title: The oral microbiome: diversity, biogeography and human health
Authors: Baker JL, Mark Welch JL, Kauffman KM, McLean JS, He X.
Journal: Nature Reviews Microbiology, 2024;22(2):89-104
DOI: 10.1038/s41579-023-00963-6
PMID: 37700024
PMCID: PMC11084736
Source: PubMed Central (full text retrieved via PubMed MCP get_full_text_article)

ABSTRACT
The human oral microbiota is highly diverse and has a complex ecology, comprising bacteria, microeukaryotes, archaea and viruses. These communities have elaborate and highly structured biogeography that shapes metabolic exchange on a local scale and results from the diverse microenvironments present in the oral cavity. The oral microbiota also interfaces with the immune system of the human host and has an important role in not only the health of the oral cavity but also systemic health. In this Review, we highlight recent advances including novel insights into the biogeography of several oral niches at the species level, as well as the ecological role of candidate phyla radiation bacteria and non-bacterial members of the oral microbiome. In addition, we summarize the relationship between the oral microbiota and the pathology of oral diseases and systemic diseases. Together, these advances move the field towards a more holistic understanding of the oral microbiota and its role in health, which in turn opens the door to the study of novel preventive and therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

The direct visual observation of bacteria in the oral cavity by Antonie van Leeuwenhoek in 1670 using his self-designed microscope marked the discovery of the oral microbiota. Subsequent study of the human oral microbiome has revealed that the microorganisms residing in the oral cavity are a major contributor to overall host health and that dysbiosis in the oral microbiome is frequently involved in the pathogenesis of both oral and systemic diseases. The oral microbiome is acquired through both maternal transmission and the environment, in an organized pattern, with the eruption of teeth providing new ecological niches and increasing diversity.

The accessibility of the oral microbiota enables the process of biofilm and community assembly to be directly captured at the sites of interest, and hence the oral microbiota offers a powerful model system for exploring and understanding complex microbiomes. Distinct habitats within the mouth are colonized by microbiotas that are widely different in both composition and spatial organization. Within the oral cavity, bacteria, archaea, eukaryotes and viruses coexist and interact with each other and with the human host. Recently developed technologies including culture-independent metagenomic sequencing, single-cell sequencing, FISH-based microscopy, metatranscriptomics, metaproteomics and metabolomics have revolutionized the scale and level of resolution of oral microbiome research.

Diversity of the oral microbiome

Oral microbiome studies have shown that there is a diverse set of more than 700 bacterial species drawn largely from a few dozen genera across seven phyla: Actinomycetota, Bacteroidota, Bacillota, Fusobacteriota, Pseudomonadota, Saccharibacteria (TM7) and Spirochaetota. Metagenomics enables detection of organisms that lack 16S rRNA genes, expanding the known oral microbiome beyond bacteria to viruses, fungi, protozoa and archaea.

Candidate phyla radiation (CPR) bacteria are a large monophyletic group now thought to account for more than 25% of global bacterial diversity. Of the more than 70 phyla identified within CPR, Saccharibacteria (formerly TM7), 'Absconditabacteria' (SR1) and 'Gracilibacteria' (GN02) are routinely detected in the human oral microbiome. The first isolated oral Saccharibacteria (TM7x) has an ultrasmall cell size (200-300 nm), a reduced genome with limited de novo biosynthetic capability, and an epiparasitic lifestyle dependent on a physically associated bacterial host (Actinomyces odontolyticus). Association of TM7x with its bacterial host increased host survival during acid stress via the arginine deiminase system; another study found Saccharibacteria decreased bone loss caused by Actinomyces in a mouse periodontal disease model, contrary to earlier hypotheses that Saccharibacteria were overt periodontal pathogens.

Microeukaryotes (fungi, amoebas, flagellates) and archaea: amplicon-based studies have identified more than 100 fungal genera in the mouth, though oral mycobiomes are generally dominated by Candida or Malassezia. Archaea, amoeba and amitochondriate flagellates live primarily in periodontal pockets and are associated with periodontal disease. Methanobrevibacter oralis is the most common oral archaeal taxon; archaeal abundance increases with obesity and smoking.

Viruses: most oral viruses are bacteriophages. More than 60,000 species-level groups of phages have been identified in the oral microbiome, exerting substantial selective pressure. Greater than 90% of humans are chronically infected with viruses in the Herpesviridae and Anelloviridae families, commonly detected in saliva, with herpesviruses potentially contributing to periodontal disease.

Biogeography of the oral microbiome

The oral cavity contains many distinct microenvironments supporting different microbial communities: hard tooth enamel surfaces (supra- and subgingival), keratinized surfaces of the palate/gingiva/tongue papillae, and soft buccal mucosa surfaces. Site specialization has been observed for decades; genera and families contain species specialized for the tongue, dental plaque or gums. The microbiotas of dental plaque, tongue dorsum and keratinized gingiva are the most distinctive from one another.

CLASI-FISH (combinatorial labelling and spectral imaging fluorescence in situ hybridization) imaging demonstrated that oral bacteria build complex, spatially organized communities where taxonomically and metabolically disparate bacteria are directly adjacent. Corynebacterium was found to form a physical bridge between the base of the biofilm and its outer layers, with Streptococcus spp. localizing to the "corncob" assemblage at the tips of Corynebacterium filaments — challenging the classic model in which Fusobacterium nucleatum had the key bridging role between early and late colonizers.

Species-level analysis of the genus Streptococcus using short-read metagenomics found that S. mutans, S. sanguinis and S. salivarius occupy different mouth regions: S. salivarius on buccal mucosa, S. mutans in dental plaque, and S. sanguinis on the tongue dorsum. S. mutans was found either dispersed throughout biofilms or densely packed in a "rotund" architecture that generated a lower-pH microenvironment and greatly increased demineralization of the underlying enamel, linking spatial organization directly to caries onset. Bacterial aggregates in saliva bind to teeth as large units already including late colonizers, at a growth advantage over single cells, helping explain detection of late colonizers as soon as 30 minutes after tooth brushing.

The oral microbiota and oral disease

Dental caries: caries is associated with dysbiosis favoring biofilm-forming, acid-producing, acid-tolerant species. Cultivation-independent analysis established caries can occur without substantial (or detectable) S. mutans, so caries is now understood as a result of complex ecological shifts rather than infection by a single species; when present, S. mutans strongly correlates with caries via extracellular glucan production driving biofilm dysbiosis. Nitrate-reducing bacteria (Neisseria, Rothia) are associated with good dental health, motivating investigation of nitrate as an anticaries prebiotic and nitrate-reducing bacteria as probiotics.

Periodontal disease: an inflammatory disruption of host-microbial homeostasis in the periodontal pocket. Gingivitis is reversible; periodontitis involves irreversible bone resorption, staged/graded by severity. Beyond canonical taxa (P. gingivalis, Tannerella forsythia, Treponema denticola), Filifactor alocis and Saccharibacteria are now recognized as potential periodontal pathogens. The experimental gingivitis model identified three inflammatory responder phenotypes (high, low, and a newly recognized "slow" responder) with distinct microbial and host signatures, suggesting personalized treatment targets.

Oral cancers: ~90% of oral squamous cell carcinomas (OSCC) in the USA are driven by HPV infection; >90% of nasopharyngeal carcinomas are associated with EBV. A dysbiotic mycobiome rich in Candida was prevalent in OSCC patients, while higher Malassezia abundance correlated with better OSCC survival, suggesting a prognostic biomarker role.

Roles in systemic health and disease

Periodontal pathogens (P. gingivalis, F. nucleatum) are linked to Alzheimer disease, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, colorectal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, rheumatoid arthritis, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease and obesity, via two mechanisms: (1) direct translocation of oral bacteria to distal sites and (2) indirect effects of dysbiotic oral communities. F. nucleatum enrichment at colorectal tumours (via Fap2 binding host Gal-GalNAc) drives tumour progression through FadA adhesin upregulating annexin A1 and Wnt-beta-catenin signalling, and via TIGIT/CEACAM1 binding that suppresses antitumour T-cell and NK-cell activity. P. gingivalis translocation raises IL-6 and skews immune cells toward osteoclastogenesis (bone loss in rheumatoid arthritis); P. gingivalis gingipains degrade tau protein, correlating with Alzheimer disease and neuronal inflammation; P. gingivalis and A. actinomycetemcomitans promote host protein citrullination, generating anti-citrullinated protein antibodies implicated in rheumatoid arthritis. In mice, periodontal pathobiont-specific T cells migrate to the gut and exacerbate colitis; experimental periodontitis induces bone-marrow-mediated trained innate immunity (a systemic maladaptive pro-inflammatory state).

Perspectives

Large-scale longitudinal human microbiome studies must include oral alongside gut samples; saliva alone under-samples subgingival plaque, a key site for bacterial entry into the bloodstream. Key open questions include microbial growth/turnover rates in situ, the fraction of cells lost to phage predation, and dispersion rates to epithelium/extra-oral sites. CPR bacteria, archaea, microeukaryotes and viruses, while lower in biomass, are increasingly recognized as ecologically and functionally important, motivating novel prebiotic/probiotic strategies against both oral (caries, periodontitis) and systemic (IBD, cancer, Alzheimer disease, cardiovascular disease) diseases.

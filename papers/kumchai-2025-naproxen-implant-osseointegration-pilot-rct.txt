Title: Randomized, Placebo-Controlled Pilot Study of Naproxen During Dental Implant Osseointegration.
Authors: Kumchai H, Taub DI, Tomlinson RE.
Journal: Clinical and Experimental Dental Research. 2025 Feb;11(1):e70065.

BACKGROUND AND RATIONALE
Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are widely used for post-operative dental
pain, yet there is long-standing concern that they may impair bone healing. NSAIDs inhibit
cyclooxygenase (COX), reducing prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) synthesis. PGE2 and the COX-2
pathway are known to be critical for craniofacial bone repair; COX-2 knockout mice
demonstrate calvarial non-union, underscoring the mechanistic plausibility that NSAIDs could
compromise early osseointegration of dental implants. Despite this concern, no prior
randomized trial had directly measured osseointegration outcomes in humans under NSAID
exposure. This pilot study was designed to generate effect-size and sample-size estimates for
a future definitive trial.

METHODS
Design: prospective, randomized, placebo-controlled pilot trial. Patients receiving maxillary
dental implants were randomized to either naproxen 220 mg every 8 hours for 7 days (n=7) or
matching placebo (n=5). Both groups additionally received acetaminophen 500 mg every 6 hours;
rescue analgesia was acetaminophen with codeine. A total of 12 patients were analyzed; one
naproxen-group subject was removed from analysis after the implant failed at 1 week.
Exclusion criteria included smokers, patients with uncontrolled diabetes, and chronic NSAID
users. Implant stability was assessed by the Implant Stability Quotient (ISQ) using resonance
frequency analysis (Osstell device) at baseline and at 1, 4, and 16 weeks. Marginal bone loss
(MBL) was measured on periapical radiographs.

RESULTS
Implant stability increased over time in both groups but the trajectories differed. At 4 weeks,
ISQ had increased approximately +1% in the naproxen group versus +41% in the placebo group; at
16 weeks the increases were +34% (naproxen) versus +67% (placebo). These between-group
differences were not statistically significant, reflecting the small sample size. Marginal
bone loss was greater in the naproxen group: approximately 55% more MBL at 4 weeks and 52% more
at 16 weeks compared with placebo, again not statistically significant (p >= 0.05). All
implants had ISQ greater than 40 by the end of the study. One implant failure occurred at 1
week, in the naproxen group.

Regarding analgesia, the placebo group required rescue medication far more often (80%) than the
naproxen group (14.3%), confirming that naproxen effectively relieved post-operative pain.

POWER / SAMPLE-SIZE ANALYSIS
Using the observed effect sizes, the investigators estimated that a definitive trial powered on
the ISQ endpoint would require at least 64 subjects per group at the 4-week time point, and up
to hundreds of subjects to detect the effect definitively. In contrast, the MBL endpoint would
require only about 7-10 subjects per group. The authors therefore recommend MBL as the more
efficient primary endpoint for future trials.

SUPPLEMENTARY QUESTIONNAIRE
A separate questionnaire of 18 patients who had experienced early implant failure found that
67.7% reported post-operative NSAID use, compared with roughly 26% NSAID use in the general
population (NHANES reference); 32.3% of the failure patients did not use NSAIDs.

CONCLUSION
There was no statistically significant difference between naproxen and placebo in either ISQ or
MBL, consistent with the study being an underpowered pilot. However, the point estimates were
directionally consistent: naproxen was associated with slower stability gain and greater
marginal bone loss, suggesting NSAIDs may impair early osseointegration. The study delivers
effect sizes and sample-size estimates for a future adequately powered trial and identifies MBL
as the more efficient endpoint. The findings do not establish a definitive clinical effect but
support caution and further investigation.

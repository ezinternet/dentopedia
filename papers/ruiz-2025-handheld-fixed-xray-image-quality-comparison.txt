Introduction

Initially developed in the early 1990s to aid dental care for soldiers in military operations, handheld portable intraoral diagnostic X-ray equipment was an alternative to fixed intraoral X-ray equipment, facilitating intraoral radiographic acquisitions in the field. Over the past decade, use of handheld portable intraoral diagnostic X-ray equipment has expanded in daily clinical practice, highlighting the need for studies exploring its characteristics, applicability, and implications of widespread use.

A primary concern is the dose of ionizing radiation delivered to patients and operators; the consulted literature indicates biological risks are comparable to fixed intraoral X-ray equipment. Studies have also evaluated aiming precision, potential distortion in intraoral radiographs, the relationship between battery levels and tube voltage, and use in forensic dentistry. While results are generally promising, a critical gap remains regarding the impact of handheld portable intraoral diagnostic X-ray equipment on radiographic quality. This study aimed to evaluate the objective image quality of radiographs obtained with handheld portable intraoral diagnostic X-ray equipment using different CMOS sensors, compared against radiographs obtained with fixed intraoral X-ray equipment.

Material and methods

Brightness, noise, and uniformity were evaluated using a 30 x 40 x 30 mm acrylic block radiographed with a size-2 CMOS sensor (Digora Toto system, Scanora software, Soredex, Tuusula, Finland) and separately with a size-1 CMOS sensor (SnapShot system, Cliniview software, Instrumentarium Imaging, Milwaukee, US). Six radiographs per sensor were obtained using the paralleling technique with the Eagle X-ray handheld portable equipment (Alliage, Sao Paulo, Brazil) at 60 kVp, 2.5 mA, 0.45 s exposure, with the handheld unit mounted on a stable platform to eliminate hand-movement variability. Additional radiographs (six per sensor) were obtained with a Focus fixed intraoral X-ray unit (Instrumentarium, Tuusula, Finland) at 60 kVp, 7 mA, 0.16 s exposure. Exposure times were selected via pilot subjective evaluation by two dentomaxillofacial radiologists (5 years' experience) to yield similar mAs products (1.125 mAs handheld vs 1.12 mAs fixed).

All 24 resulting radiographs (6 x 2 equipment x 2 systems) were exported as 8-bit TIFF and analyzed with ImageJ (NIH, Maryland, US). Brightness and noise were assessed via a central ROI covering 16% of the radiograph area (mean and SD of gray values). Uniformity was assessed via a central 4x4mm ROI plus four symmetric peripheral ROIs of the same size, averaging their SDs; ROI placement was standardized using ImageJ macros.

For contrast, an aluminum step-wedge (six steps, 2-12 mm thickness) was radiographed under identical parameters/equipment/systems (24 radiographs total). A vertical line was drawn centrally, a horizontal line equally divided the image, and 4x4mm ROIs were placed on the 10mm step (ROI1) and 4mm step (ROI2); percentage of contrast variation was calculated as (Mean ROI1 - Mean ROI2)/(Mean ROI1) x 100. Comparisons used Student's t-test (alpha=5%; power analysis 95% for all variables; SPSS 25.0).

Results

For acrylic-block radiographs, brightness and noise differed significantly by equipment type and CMOS sensor (p<0.05). With the Digora Toto system, handheld equipment produced increased brightness and noise (p<0.0001) vs. fixed equipment. With the Snapshot system, handheld equipment produced increased brightness (p<0.0001) but lower noise (p=0.001) vs. fixed equipment. Uniformity did not differ significantly between equipment for either sensor (p>0.05).

Contrast (aluminum step-wedge): for both digital systems, contrast on radiographs from handheld equipment was decreased compared to fixed equipment (p<0.0001).

Numerically (mean +/- SD): Digora Toto - handheld brightness 65.41 (0.86) vs fixed 57.66 (1.04), p<0.0001; noise 14.48 (0.24) vs 13.10 (0.27), p<0.0001; uniformity 9.48 (0.23) vs 9.53 (0.12), p=0.661; contrast 31.85% (0.04) vs 32.99% (0.03), p<0.0001. Snapshot - handheld brightness 85.38 (0.19) vs fixed 68.95 (0.27), p<0.0001; noise 1.05 (0.02) vs 1.12 (0.01), p=0.001; uniformity 1.00 (0.08) vs 1.03 (0.00), p=0.445; contrast 42.81% (0.18) vs 47.69% (0.02), p<0.0001.

Discussion

Handheld portable intraoral diagnostic X-ray equipment produced radiographs with higher brightness and lower contrast compared to fixed intraoral X-ray equipment, regardless of digital system used. Noise effects varied by sensor: higher with Digora Toto, lower with Snapshot when paired with handheld equipment - suggesting that CMOS sensor sensitivity, image-processing algorithms, scintillator materials, and detector electronics interact with the equipment type to determine noise characteristics.

This finding contrasts methodologically with Nitschke et al., who used a subjective geometric-distortion evaluation (examiner assessment of periodontal ligament space, lamina dura, periradicular bone, alveolar crest, pulp chamber, root canals, dental crowns) and reported no meaningful handheld-vs-fixed differences; the present study's objective, software-based gray-value analysis is complementary rather than contradictory, each capturing a distinct aspect of radiographic quality.

Despite statistically significant differences, the magnitude of the noise difference was minimal relative to brightness/contrast differences, and the authors argue these noise differences would likely be imperceptible to the human eye and would not impair diagnosis in real clinical scenarios - consistent with prior literature showing that PSP-plate brightness increases over prolonged use without compromising caries-lesion diagnosis. Previous diagnostic-outcome studies (rather than objective image-quality studies) found that radiographic diagnosis of proximal caries lesions was not compromised by handheld equipment, despite the gray-value differences objectively measured here; the authors note this apparent discrepancy reflects that statistical differences in quality parameters do not necessarily translate into clinically significant diagnostic differences, and that existing diagnostic-performance literature is limited mostly to caries detection, leaving root resorption, root fracture, and periodontal bone loss diagnostic performance with handheld equipment understudied.

Methodological notes: an acrylic block (rather than anatomical structures) was used for brightness/noise/uniformity to minimize the variability inherent to anatomical density/shape differences, consistent with prior gray-value-analysis literature. The handheld unit was mounted on a stable platform (rather than hand-held) specifically to isolate equipment-intrinsic effects from operator hand-movement/fatigue effects - handheld equipment typically weighs 2.5-5 kg and requires the operator to support it through multiple exposures. All radiographs were obtained with a fully charged handheld unit, since a prior study found reduced tube voltage as battery charge decreases, which could independently affect quality; battery-management effects in routine (non-fully-charged) clinical use were not evaluated here and are flagged as a needed area of future research. Future research directions proposed: effect of extended exposure time on handheld vs fixed image quality; whether software-based brightness/contrast post-processing can compensate for the handheld equipment's inherent brightness/contrast profile; and whether the physical act of manually holding (rather than platform-mounting) the heavy device introduces additional quality effects via unintentional movement.

Conclusion

Based on objective analyses, radiographs obtained with handheld portable intraoral diagnostic X-ray equipment have higher brightness and lower contrast compared to radiographs obtained with fixed intraoral X-ray equipment, regardless of the digital system used. The effect on noise varies depending on the CMOS sensor used. The use of handheld portable intraoral diagnostic X-ray equipment does not affect uniformity values.

Xerostomia: From Pharmacological Treatments to Traditional Medicine—An Overview on the Possible Clinical Management and Prevention Using Systemic Approaches

Sardellitti L, Bortone A, Filigheddu E, Serralutzu F, Milia EP
Current Oncology 2023;30(5):4412-4426
DOI: 10.3390/curroncol30050336
PMID: 37232794 | PMCID: PMC10216964
Published: 2023-04-24

ABSTRACT

Despite high incidence rates and severe complications, the management of xerostomia lacks clinical guidelines. The aim of this overview was to summarize the clinical experience derived from the last 10 years of treatments and prevention using systemic compounds. Results showed that the cytoprotective drug amifostine, and its antioxidant agents, are the most discussed as preventive agents of xerostomia in head and neck cancer (HNC) patients. In the presence of the disease, the pharmacological treatments have been mainly directed to stimulate secretion of the damaged salivary glands, or to counteract a decreased capacity of the antioxidant system, in view of an increasing of reactive oxygen species (ROS). However, the data demonstrated low ability of the drugs, together with a great number of side effects, which strongly limit their use. Concerning traditional medicine (TM), valid clinical trials are so limited that neither the efficacy nor the absence of interferences to concomitant chemical therapies can be validated. Consequently, the management of xerostomia and its devastating complications remain a very significant void in daily clinical practice.

1. INTRODUCTION

Saliva is an important defense in maintaining well-being and oral health — 99% water, remaining 1% electrolytes/proteins responsible for speech, swallowing, tasting. Enzymes digest starches/fats; epidermal growth factors promote tissue growth/wound-healing; antibacterial/antifungal/antiviral agents balance oral biofilm preventing dysbiosis; mineral components maintain tooth integrity; salivary glycoproteins (mucins) + electrolytes protect mucosal structures. ~90% of salivary secretion from submandibular, parotid, sublingual glands (autonomic nervous system control); minor glands (labial/lingual/buccal/palatal) produce remaining 10%. Normal output 0.5-1.5 L/day.

Xerostomia = subjective dry-mouth symptoms from reduced saliva or altered composition. Manifests when fluid reduced to 40-50% of normal, or with compositional change. Occurs when resting saliva ≤0.1 mL/min and stimulated saliva ≤0.5 mL/min. Incidence ~30% in population aged 65+, more common in women; increases to 61% in aging population due to comorbidity/polypharmacy.

Causes: autoimmune conditions (Sjögren's Syndrome), rheumatic/dysmetabolic diseases (diabetes, hepatitis C), chronic use of >400 medications (antidepressants, antihypertensives, anticancer agents, opiates, bronchodilators, PPIs, antipsychotics, antihistamines, diuretics). Radiation therapy for head and neck cancer (HNC) is the most prevalent cause of salivary dysfunction — affects major/minor salivary glands, temporary/permanent impairment in up to 40% of patients. SARS-CoV-2 infection reported to cause xerostomia in 45.9% of infected subjects.

Symptoms: dry mouth → mucosal ulceration, aphthosis, mucositis, infections, pain, burning mouth (mainly tongue/hard palate due to greater saliva evaporation there). Pharynx mucosa lubrication impairment → speech/cough issues. Sleep disturbances (need to humidify mucosa at night, when saliva secretion is circadian-reduced). Chewing/swallowing difficulty → continuous water sipping. Taste dysfunction affects saliva production — impairs neoplastic patients' survival via psychological anxiety and malnutrition (major morbidity factor). Lowered pH of viscous saliva → bacterial shift/dysbiosis → gingivitis, caries, mucositis, oral candidiasis (esp. immunocompromised) — diminishes QoL and poses major health problem in cancer patients.

Management remains a significant clinical challenge. Treatments directed at increasing saliva flow (pharmacological) or local salivary substitutes (relieve dryness sensation). Traditional medicine (TM) and complementary medicine (CM) evaluated given WHO-sustained demand (2003-05, 2011-23), but clinical conclusions/benefits remain mostly ambiguous; compromised patient health/medication intake narrows treatment choices. This review examines literature from Jan 1 2012 - Jan 30 2021 (PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, Google Scholar) on xerostomia prevention/therapy.

2. RESULTS

2.1 Systemic Chemical Treatments — wide range of pharmacological interventions, particularly for HNC radiotherapy patients and Sjögren's Syndrome patients.

2.2 Para-sympathomimetic drugs — stimulate salivary secretion via parasympathetic nervous system; acetylcholine activates acinar cell muscarinic receptors (mAChRs) → G-protein (Gq family) → intracellular calcium ↑ → Cl-/K+ channel opening. 5 mAChR subtypes (M1-M5); M3 key role, M1/M5 may also influence output.
- Pilocarpine HCl (choline ester): most common para-sympathomimetic for dry mouth; tested for prevention/cure of xerostomia post-HNC radiotherapy and in Sjögren's patients. Cochrane review (Davis et al.) found pilocarpine can be effective but statistically significant difference vs placebo remains unclear; doses 2.5-10mg up to 3x/day. Side effects: headache (15%), urinary frequency (14%), vasodilatation (12%), dizziness (10%), dyspepsia (10%), nausea (8%), asthenia (8%), diarrhea (5%) — predominant reason for study withdrawal.
- Cevimeline HCl: 2 RCTs found quite effective.
- Bethanechol (choline ester, muscarinic): studied in HNC patients post-irradiation; increased basal salivary flow vs artificial saliva, but side effects (hyperthyroidism, peptic ulcer, asthma, bradycardia, hypotension, coronary disease, epilepsy, Parkinsonism) limit use.

2.3 Para-sympatholytic drugs — oppose para-sympathomimetics, inhibit saliva secretion; in radiotherapy animal testing, inhibition of secretion might protect salivary glands from later damage.

2.4 Cytoprotective agents — administered before/after cancer therapy to prevent/reduce normal-tissue damage without compromising efficacy. Amifostine: organic thiophosphate, indicated against radiation/chemo harm including acute/late xerostomia. Mechanism: free radical scavenging, DNA protection/repair acceleration, cellular hypoxia induction. Prodrug → active metabolite WR-1065 accumulates in normal tissues, protects membranes/DNA. Some studies show reduced xerostomia from radiotherapy; but no clear benefit vs normal saline IV placebo in some. Increased risk of vomiting, hypotension, nausea, allergic responses reported; also differences in worst-grade skin/mucosal toxicity between arms post-radiotherapy. Controversy whether amifostine reduces cancer treatment efficacy (overall/progression-free/disease-free survival, QoL).

2.5 Antioxidant agents — oxidative stress implicated in xerostomia (Sjögren's, radiotherapy, systemic sclerosis). CoQ10: required for cell membrane protection, ATP production, secretory function; ubiquinone/ubiquinol capsules vs placebo increased salivary production in Sjögren's patients, ubiquinol showing higher efficacy. Vitamin E (α-tocopherol): free radical scavenger; improved salivary parameters (pH, potassium, amylase activity) and flow rate in radiotherapy patients. Vitamin E 100 IU + Vitamin C 500mg during radiotherapy → greater VAS improvement vs control. Lycopene (natural carotenoid): decreases serum lipid peroxidation/LDL oxidation; lycopene-enriched virgin olive oil more effective for dry-mouth relief than isolated compound; lycopene-DNA methylation-inflammation correlation warrants further study in HNC survivors.

2.6 Biological agents — for Sjögren's Syndrome, targeting immunological dysfunction (lymphocytic infiltration in exocrine/non-exocrine epithelia).
- Rituximab (anti-CD20 mAb, B-cell depletion): double-blind RCT showed increased stimulated/unstimulated whole saliva flow + lacrimal function + decreased extra-glandular manifestations initially, but benefits lost by 48 weeks; only ocular dryness VAS improved significantly at 48wk; side effects: serum sickness-like disease, infections.
- Epratuzumab (anti-CD22 mAb): improved lacrimal function/unstimulated saliva flow, but acute infusion reactions/infections.
- Belimumab (anti-BLyS mAb, 10mg/kg monthly): no change in unstimulated saliva flow; caused infections incl. pneumococcal meningitis.
- Ianalumab (VAY736) and baminercept (lymphotoxin-β receptor fusion protein): low efficacy, associated toxicity.
- Abatacept (CD80/86-CD28 blocker, T-cell targeting): controversial results — some reduction in salivary gland inflammatory markers, others no change; side effects: lymphocytic foci, lupus-like cutaneous lesions.
- Efalizumab: no increase in salivation; withdrawn from market due to multifocal leukoencephalopathy risk.
- Infliximab, etanercept (TNF-family), tocilizumab (IL-6), anakinra (IL-1): no significant change in salivary/lacrimal function vs placebo.
- Interferon alpha: may upregulate aquaporin-5 (abnormally distributed in SS salivary/lacrimal glands); double-blind placebo-controlled study + other trials showed improved oral/ocular dryness and increased unstimulated salivary flow vs placebo; but high GI adverse effect rate limits use.

2.7 Traditional Medicine (TM) — WHO definition: medicine with long history, sum of knowledge/skill/practices based on theories/beliefs/experiences of different cultures, used in health maintenance/prevention/diagnosis/treatment. Studies on TM for dry mouth symptoms report controversial, difficult-to-interpret results due to methodological differences.

2.7.1 Traditional East Asian Medicine — based on Yin/Yang theory, 5 elements, visceral manifestation theory; herbal medicine prescribed via pattern identification. Difficult to compare across studies (often published in Asian languages, only English abstracts available). Notable herbs/formulas for oral mucositis (often co-occurring with xerostomia from radio/chemotherapy oxidative stress/inflammation): Plantago major L. (wound-healing, anti-ulcerative, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antiviral, antioxidant); Japanese traditional Kampo medicine mixture (inhibits COX2, effective in oral inflammation); investigated for oral mucositis/recurrent aphthous stomatitis; Zingiber officinale Roscoe rhizome relieves 5-fluorouracil-induced oral ulcerative mucositis/pain; quercetin (flavonoid) — anti-inflammatory/antioxidant, prevents mucositis; Malva sylvestris L. (Mallow) + Althaea officinalis studied in a 4-week clinical trial — significant VAS efficacy vs artificial saliva. Hsu et al.: various Chinese herbs (decoctions, topical applications, powders) during HNC radiotherapy — less severe dry mouth (subjective + clinician-assessed) vs control, though not statistically significant. Common herbal mixtures (Park et al.): Shennongbaijie decoction, Xuanmaizengyehuadu decoction, Yunnanbaiyao capsules, Niancianchuanqipipa gel, Jiaweizengye decoction, Sanganhuayin decoction. Overall: very-low-to-moderate quality evidence — high risk of bias in blinding, incomplete outcome data, poor reporting of randomization/allocation concealment/blinding/dropouts; more rigorous studies needed.

2.7.2 Traditional Western Countries' Medicine — popular medicine references dry-mouth-related symptoms, mostly treated via plant/herb mixtures or water-extraction infusions/decoctions. Scarcity of documentation limits medical-field application; 15-73% of cancer patients (per European research) use TM as auto-medication against radio/chemotherapy side effects. European Medicines Agency (EMA) + Committee on Herbal Medicinal Products (HMPC) guidelines: well-established medical use (>30 years) + absence of documented toxicity (>15 years) needed to recognize herbs pharmaceutically. Most European studies used laboratory (in vitro/cell line) methods rather than clinical evaluation; very few clinical trials avoided standard oral administration in favor of tradition-modifying approaches (e.g., mouthwash instead of oral beverage) — e.g., Aloe vera + honey; propolis + Ficus carica + Malva sylvestris + Althaea officinalis mixture (mouthwash, cancer patients). Sage (Salvia) studied as spray against acute pharyngitis and oral mucositis; Echinacea/sage spray against sore throat. In vitro/cell-line studies widely investigated antimicrobial/anti-inflammatory/antioxidant capacity of various herbs and essential oils against oral pathogens (including Candida spp.) and reference strains causing septicemia in compromised/mucositis patients. Limitation: in vitro/cell-line behavior doesn't necessarily translate in vivo; need to characterize pharmacodynamic/pharmacokinetic properties of individual active compounds. Insufficient clinical evaluations remain the greatest limitation to validating TM efficacy/safety in Europe.

3. DISCUSSION

Reviewed last 10 years of literature on preventing/treating xerostomia via systemic chemical drugs and TM. Despite many agents evaluated, no compound has achieved prevention/resolution of xerostomia.

Prevention mainly concerns HNC radiotherapy patients (xerostomia as secondary effect of radiation-induced oxidative process through salivary glands, even with IMRT). Amifostine (free radical scavenger/DNA protective/repair-accelerating/hypoxia-inducing) may antagonize salivary gland dysfunction under radiation; some studies suggest efficacy in preventing post-radiotherapy dryness, but side effects limit current use; effect on survival outcomes remains debated.

Antioxidants (CoQ10, Vitamin E ± Vitamin C, natural carotenoids) act directly against oxidative stress — a key factor in Sjögren's Syndrome, systemic sclerosis, and radiotherapy-induced salivary gland failure; generally show increased salivary fluid vs baseline, but no detailed guidelines exist for adoption.

Pilocarpine most-assayed para-sympathomimetic (HNC + Sjögren's); statistically significant benefit vs placebo remains unclear despite widespread use; side effects prominent. Cevimeline and bethanechol show similarly low relief capacity with substantial side effects (headache, urinary frequency, vasodilatation, dizziness, dyspepsia, nausea, asthenia, diarrhea reflect indiscriminate stimulation of all 5 mAChR subtypes) — highlighting need for a selective salivary-output stimulant.

Biological agents (rituximab, epratuzumab, belimumab, ianalumab, baminercept — monoclonal antibodies targeting lymphocytic infiltration) show low efficacy with potentially serious toxicity (allergic reactions to systemic infections) in Sjögren's patients. Interferon alpha similarly limited by GI adverse effects despite immune/inflammatory regulation benefit.

Non-pharmacological (traditional/indigenous) therapy: East Asian TM (Shennongbaijie decoction, Xuanmaizengyehuadu decoction, Yunnanbaiyao capsules, Jiaweizengye decoction, Sanganhuayin decoction, and herbs like Plantago major, ginger, quercetin) commonly applied against oral dryness/mucositis, all administered as oral systemic compounds — but very-low-to-moderate evidence quality reduces strength of evidence. Western TM: herbs/mixtures (chamomile, sage, mallow, marshmallow, propolis) for oral infections/ulcers/cough/sore throat/anxiety, generally as water-extraction infusions/decoctions; most European studies were in vitro/cell-line rather than clinical, with few trials modifying traditional oral-administration route (mouthwash vs. beverage) due to Ethical Committee restrictions; further limited by the need to isolate/characterize individual active compounds, which may not capture the synergistic complexity of whole-herb mixtures.

CONCLUSION: Xerostomia currently lacks drugs able to prevent disease development in predisposed patients, and no systemic chemical treatment is indicated for relief in clinical practice. Regarding TM, the very low number of clinical studies does not allow scientific validation of popular remedies. Management of xerostomia and its devastating complications remains a very significant void in daily clinical practice.

## ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis network meta-analysis compared short-term pink esthetic outcomes (PES-SO) among three socket shield technique (SST) protocols: SST (no grafting), SST-GBR (with guided bone regeneration), and SST-nJP (no jumping gap). And two conventional immediate implant placement protocols: IIP (no grafting) and IIP-GBR (with guided bone regeneration).
MethodsPubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Scopus, CNKI, VIP, Wanfang, and SinoMed were searched for comparative studies published from January 2010 to January 2026. The only outcome was short-term PES-SO. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane tool and certainty of evidence using CINeMA. A random-effects network meta-analysis was conducted. A clinically restricted analysis (excluding patients with thin labial plates and studies with reporting limitations that could affect comparability) and a supporting analysis were additionally performed.
ResultsIn the full network (21 studies), IIP was associated with significantly lower PES-SO than IIP-GBR, SST and SST-GBR, whereas no statistically significant differences were identified among SST and SST-GBR. The findings regarding SST-nJP in this study should be interpreted with caution, as the analysis is primarily based on a single small-sample study and indirect comparisons. The restricted and supporting analyses showed broadly consistent patterns. Nevertheless, procedure rankings and some indirect estimates varied across analyses.
ConclusionWithin the limitations of the available evidence, SST-GBR, SST, and IIP-GBR were associated with more favorable short-term PES-SO than IIP. The findings of SST-nJP should be interpreted cautiously because it was informed by a single study and imprecise estimates. Additional well-designed randomized trials directly comparing SST protocols are warranted. Due to the unquantifiable transitivity and exploratory nature of the MCID, our findings are exploratory in nature and require further validation, they should not be interpreted as precise clinical guidance.

## Introduction

Dental implants are widely recognized as an effective treatment option for edentulous patients. However, following tooth extraction, the alveolar ridge undergoes dimensional changes, with irreversible resorption and socket atrophy [1–4], particularly at the labial/buccal plate [5–7]. These alterations may result in combined soft- and hard-tissue deficiencies [8–10], thereby compromising peri-implant esthetics and the patient’s smile [11–14].

In 2010, Hürzeler et al. introduced the socket shield technique (SST) [15], which aims to preserve the periodontal ligament–root complex by retaining a buccal root fragment during extraction. By maintaining local vascular supply, SST may mitigate post-extraction remodeling and help preserve ridge contour, potentially contributing to more stable esthetic outcomes. Long-term observational cohorts with follow-up exceeding 10 years have further reported favorable clinical performance of SST [16, 17].

With ongoing development, several SST operative protocols have been described. The original protocol involved direct implant–shield contact with no jumping gap (SST-nJP) [15]. Subsequently, the root membrane technique left a jumping gap without grafting (SST), based on the premise of stable clot formation within the gap [18]. Later, protocols incorporating guided bone regeneration (SST-GBR) were proposed to fill the jumping gap with a bone substitute material [17].

In recent years, randomized controlled trials and cohort studies have compared SST with conventional immediate implant placement (IIP), suggesting potential advantages of SST in limiting ridge remodeling and improving esthetic outcomes [19]. Nevertheless, direct clinical comparisons and quantitative syntheses focusing on head-to-head differences among SST operative protocols remain limited [12, 20–23]. Therefore, this study performed a network meta-analysis to compare three SST protocols (SST-nJP, SST, and SST-GBR) with IIP with or without guided bone regeneration, with the primary objective of evaluating short-term (4–12 months) pink esthetic outcomes using the Pink Esthetic Score (PES-SO).

## Method

This network meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA statement and the PRISMA extension for network meta-analyses [24, 25]. The study protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD420261297690).

### PICOS criteria

Based on the PICOS framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study design), we defined the eligibility criteria and methodological scope as follows. Population: adults requiring immediate implant placement after tooth extraction in the esthetic zone (between the second premolars, including the second premolars). Interventions: socket shield technique without grafting (SST), socket shield technique with guided bone regeneration (SST-GBR), and socket shield technique without a jumping gap (SST-nJP). Comparators: conventional immediate implant placement without grafting (IIP) and with guided bone regeneration (IIP-GBR). Outcome: the primary outcome was the Pink Esthetic Score assessed in the short term (4–12 months) after implantation [20]. Study design: controlled clinical trials and cohort studies.

Studies were included if participants were ≥ 18 years old and received implants in the esthetic zone. Studies were excluded if the sample size was < 5, follow-up was < 4 months, the required outcome data were unavailable, participants had a labial/buccal plate fracture, participants had systemic conditions or used medications that could impair osseointegration, or the report had not undergone peer review.

### Search strategy

Two reviewers (C.L. and W.Z.) conducted a systematic literature search in January 2026 using the following English-language databases: PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library. To ensure comprehensive coverage, we additionally searched Chinese databases, including China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), the Chinese Science and Technology Periodical Database (VIP), Wanfang Data, and the Chinese Biomedical Literature Service System (SinoMed). To minimize the risk of missing eligible studies, we also screened relevant meta-analyses in these databases and checked their reference lists for additional potentially eligible articles. The full search strategy is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

All retrieved records were imported into EndNote for reference management, and duplicates were removed prior to screening. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text assessment according to the predefined eligibility criteria. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion and, when necessary, adjudication by a third reviewer. ### Quality assessment and risk of bias

For randomized controlled trials, risk of bias was assessed using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2.0). For non-randomized studies, the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool was used [26]. To enhance transparency, we provided study-level explanatory notes in Supplementary Table 2 to support selected RoB 2.0 and ROBINS-I judgments and to document reporting limitations related to “study comparability”; however, this table was not intended as an independent quality assessment instrument.

Certainty of evidence was assessed using the CINeMA (Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis) framework to rate confidence in each relative treatment effect [27]. CINeMA, which is informed by the GRADE approach, evaluates six domains: within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence. For the assessment of imprecision and heterogeneity, we applied the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) defined below. Overall certainty for each comparison was categorized as high, moderate, low, or very low. Any disagreements were resolved by group discussion.

### Statistical analysis

Data analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.3; RStudio), JAGS, and the gemtc package. For more details on statistical methodology, please refer to Supplementary Table 3. Continuous outcomes were summarized as mean differences (MDs) with 95% Credible intervals (CrIs). Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic and τ (between-study standard deviation). To facilitate interpretation of heterogeneity in terms of clinical relevance, we prespecified the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for PES-SO. Since no MCID for PES has yet been derived using the Delphi method, and because the reports of included studies were incomplete, no reliable patient-reported outcome measures related to PES were found to serve as anchors for the anchor-based method, we estimated the MCID using a distribution-based approach [28]. Specifically, we calculated the pooled standard deviation (SDpooled) of PES-SO across included studies and defined the MCID as 0.5 × SDpooled [29]. The formula was: MCID = 0.5 × SDpooled. In this study, SDpooled was 1.238, yielding an MCID of 0.619. This MCID (0.619) is estimated using a distribution-based method and reflects only the characteristics of the data, it has not been validated through clinical anchoring. Therefore, it should be regarded as an exploratory reference for decision-making rather than a clinically validated minimum clinically important difference.

A random-effects network meta-analysis was performed irrespective of statistical heterogeneity to account for between-study variability and to estimate an average effect more representative of clinical practice. Network geometry was summarized using a network plot in which node sizes reflected sample sizes and edges represented available direct comparisons. Relative treatment effects were presented using forest plots. Local inconsistency was assessed using node-splitting; results are reported in the node-splitting forest plot and corresponding summary table. Publication bias was explored by visual inspection of funnel plot symmetry and Egger’s test.

### Strategies for exploring the potential threats to transitivity and heterogeneity

After completing the primary analysis including all eligible studies, we explored heterogeneity and potential threats to transitivity (transferability) to identify plausible effect modifiers and sources of between-study variability. We noted that several studies provided limited reporting of key methodological features, such as randomization procedures, blinding, sample size estimation, and follow-up, which may reduce transparency and comparability across studies (Supplementary Table 2). These reporting limitations were concentrated in specific comparison arms (SST-GBR vs. IIP-GBR). When evidence is unevenly distributed and heavily driven by a single comparison, indirect estimates may depend disproportionately on that comparison, thereby weakening the plausibility of the transitivity assumption and reducing the robustness of network inferences.

In addition, osseointegration and related clinical outcomes may differ between patients with thin labial bone plates (< 1 mm) and those with non-thin labial bone plates [30]. Therefore, we performed an additional clinically restricted analysis including only patients with non-thin labial bone plates from studies without reporting limitations that could affect between-study comparability. This analysis aimed to assess whether the effect patterns observed in the full network were consistent in a more clinically comparable evidence network. To broaden applicability beyond the clinically restricted population while maintaining methodological comparability, we also performed a supporting analysis including all patients from studies without such reporting limitations.

## Result

### Result of literature search

As of January 2026, a total of 9,810 records were retrieved from the electronic searches (PubMed, n = 872; Embase, n = 763; Cochrane Library, n = 1078; Web of Science, n = 2388; Scopus, n = 3,765; CNKI, n = 276; VIP, n = 164; Wanfang, n = 226; SinoMed, n = 278). To identify additional potentially eligible studies, we also screened relevant systematic reviews and meta-analyses on the prespecified interventions and comparisons and checked their included studies; this process yielded 7 additional records.

After removing 3082 duplicates and excluding 1534 records published before 2010, titles and abstracts were screened, and 64 articles were assessed in full text. Following full-text evaluation, 21 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the analysis [31–51]. ### PES-SO

#### Full inclusion analysis

At study initiation, we performed a full inclusion analysis including all eligible studies (n = 21) [31–51]. The full evidence network comprised 1013 implants from 21 studies [31–51]. Pairwise relative effects from the network meta-analysis are presented in the forest plot (Fig. 3B). For PES-SO, IIP was significantly inferior to all other interventions (IIP-GBR vs. IIP: MD = 1.13, 95% CrI 0.19–2.00; SST vs. IIP: MD = 2.30, 95% CrI 1.55–3.05; SST-GBR vs. IIP: MD = 2.57, 95% CrI 1.49–3.57; SST-nJP vs. IIP: MD = 3.09, 95% CrI 0.95–5.19). In addition, IIP-GBR was significantly less effective than SST, SST-GBR, and SST-nJP (SST vs. IIP-GBR: MD = 1.17, 95% CrI 0.46–1.95; SST-GBR vs. IIP-GBR: MD = 1.45, 95% CrI 0.94–1.93; SST-nJP vs. IIP-GBR: MD = 1.96, 95% CrI 0.06–3.89). No statistically significant differences were detected among SST, SST-GBR, and SST-nJP (SST-GBR vs. SST: MD = 0.28, 95% CrI − 0.67 to 1.13; SST-nJP vs. SST: MD = 0.80, 95% CrI − 1.30 to 2.82; SST-nJP vs. SST-GBR: MD = 0.52, 95% CrI − 1.32 to 2.38).

In the probability ranking, SST-nJP showed a numerically potential signal (Fig. 3C); however, this estimate was informed by a single study and was imprecise, and therefore should be interpreted cautiously. The subsequent ranking order was SST-GBR, SST, IIP-GBR, and IIP. Notably, comparisons involving SST-nJP showed wide 95% CrIs, indicating low precision.

Heterogeneity was low according to I2 (2%), whereas the between-study standard deviation was τ = 0.72 (95% CrI 0.46–1.11). The point estimate of τ exceeded the prespecified exploratory MCID, suggesting that heterogeneity could be clinically relevant; however, the corresponding 95% CrI included the MCID, indicating uncertainty. Node-splitting analyses (Fig. 3D) showed P > 0.05 for all evaluated comparisons, supporting consistency within the closed loop.

Because several studies had limitations in methodological reporting that could affect between-study comparability (Supplementary Table 2), we conducted an exploratory stratified analysis based on study characteristics [20]. Seven studies [32, 36, 40, 42, 48–50] (n = 7) evaluated only SST-GBR versus IIP-GBR, potentially contributing to heterogeneity and an imbalance of evidence across the network. The remaining studies[31, 33–35, 37–39, 41, 43–47, 51] (n = 14) formed a connected network with τ = 0.57 (95% CrI 0.20–1.18) and I2 = 6%. In this subset, the point estimate of τ was below the prespecified exploratory MCID, suggesting limited clinical impact of heterogeneity; however, the 95% CrI still included the MCID.

Next, within the subset of studies without reporting limitations that could affect comparability [31, 33–35, 37–39, 41, 43–47, 51], we examined intervention-related factors, including graft material, barrier membrane use, shield height/thickness, and jumping-gap width. None of these factors materially reduced heterogeneity (Table 4).

Finally, we evaluated patient-related factors. Evidence suggests that osseointegration may differ in patients with a thin labial bone plate (< 1 mm) [30]. In addition, the ITI Treatment Guide proposes that immediate implant placement is indicated when the labial bone plate thickness is > 1 mm [52]. Accordingly, inclusion of thin-plate cases may reduce clinical comparability across studies. When restricting the analysis to patients with non-thin labial bone plates in eligible studies [33–35, 37–39, 43–47, 51] (n = 12), heterogeneity further decreased (τ = 0.41, 95% CrI 0.05–0.96; I2 = 4%). The point estimate of τ remained below the prespecified exploratory MCID, although its 95% CrI included the MCID. Node-splitting analyses did not detect statistically significant local inconsistency (all node-splitting P > 0.05).

Stratified analyses were defined a priori based on prespecified intervention characteristics. For example, “use of a GBR membrane” and “use of xenogeneic bone alone” were only applicable to studies comparing SST-GBR with IIP-GBR, whereas “jumping gap < 2 mm” was applicable to all studies except those involving SST-nJP. To maintain sufficient data for network estimation, stratification was applied only when the relevant characteristics were explicitly reported.

#### Clinically restricted analysis

Based on the exploration of potential threats to transitivity and heterogeneity, we identified two main contributors to between-study variability: (1) studies with limitations in methodological reporting that may affect comparability across studies (Supplementary Table 2) and (2) inclusion of patients with thin labial bone plates. Therefore, we performed an additional clinically restricted analysis (including patients with non-thin labial bone plates from studies without limitations in methodological reporting that may affect comparability across studies) to examine whether the effect pattern observed in the full inclusion analysis would persist in a more clinically comparable evidence network.

The clinically restricted analysis included 430 implants from 12 studies [33–35, 37–39, 43–47, 51]. As shown in Fig. 5A, the network geometry was similar to that of the full inclusion analysis. Node-splitting analyses (Fig. 5D) did not detect statistically significant local inconsistency (all P > 0.05). Compared with the full inclusion analysis and the supporting analysis, heterogeneity was lower (I2 = 4%; τ = 0.41, 95% CrI 0.05–0.96). Treatment rankings in the clinically restricted analysis were consistent with those of the full inclusion analysis (Fig. 5C). Moreover, none of the pairwise effect directions observed in the full inclusion analysis were reversed (Fig. 5B). As heterogeneity decreased, point estimates remained stable and credible intervals generally became narrower.

Notably, the comparison between SST-nJP and IIP-GBR, which was statistically significant in the full inclusion analysis, was no longer statistically significant in the clinically restricted analysis (Mean = 1.44, 95% CrI − 0.21 to 3.07).

From the network plot (Fig. 5A), the SST-nJP versus IIP-GBR comparison was informed solely by indirect evidence along the linear path SST-nJP—SST-GBR—IIP-GBR. As indicated by Table 2 and the network structures in the full inclusion and clinically restricted analyses (Fig. 3A and Fig. 5A), the direct comparison between SST-nJP and SST-GBR relied on the same study of patients without thin labial bone plates [35]. Because that direct comparison remained unchanged, the qualitative change in SST-nJP versus IIP-GBR was most plausibly driven by changes in the pooled estimate for SST-GBR versus IIP-GBR after excluding studies with limitations in methodological reporting that may affect comparability across studies, many of which evaluated SST-GBR versus IIP-GBR (Table 2). This change in the SST-GBR versus IIP-GBR estimate was then propagated through the indirect comparison chain, leading to the observed change in the SST-nJP versus IIP-GBR result.

#### Supporting analysis

To expand the target population beyond the clinically restricted analysis, we conducted a supporting analysis including all patients from studies without limitations in methodological reporting that may affect comparability across studies [31, 33–35, 37–39, 41, 43–47, 51].

The network plot (Fig. 6A) was structurally similar to that of the clinically restricted analysis. Node-splitting analyses (Fig. 6D) did not detect statistically significant local inconsistency (all P > 0.05). However, the relative ranking of SST and SST-GBR shifted modestly. In the clinically restricted analysis, SST-GBR had a slightly higher probability of being better than SST (SUCRA 72.44% vs. 63.03%; difference 9.41%). In the supporting analysis, this order was reversed (SUCRA 65.68% vs. 73.84%; difference 8.16%). This change was accompanied by reduced precision for the SST versus SST-GBR comparison: the pooled effect estimate changed from Mean = 0.195 (95% CrI − 0.824 to 1.04) in the clinically restricted analysis to Mean =  − 0.135 (95% CrI − 1.37 to 0.850) in the supporting analysis (Fig. 6B). Correspondingly, the credible interval width increased by approximately 19.1%.

This reduced precision was plausibly related to the inclusion of two studies involving patients with thin labial bone plates [31, 41]. These studies (SST vs. IIP-GBR; IIP-GBR vs. IIP) contributed indirect evidence to the SST versus SST-GBR comparison. Both had relatively small sample sizes (Gurbuz: 12 vs. 12; Jacobs: 19 vs. 14), and thin-plate cases may differ in osseointegration compared with non-thin cases; thus, their inclusion may have increased heterogeneity and reduced precision.

Across comparisons, 95% CrIs in the supporting analysis were generally wider. For example, SST versus IIP changed from Mean = 2.40 (95% CrI 1.80–3.00) in the clinically restricted analysis to Mean = 2.29 (95% CrI 1.62–2.96) in the supporting analysis, with the CrI width increasing by 11.67% (Fig. 6B). Consistent with this, heterogeneity was higher in the supporting analysis than in the clinically restricted analysis (supporting analysis: I2 = 6%, τ = 0.57, 95% CrI 0.20–1.18; clinically restricted analysis: I2 = 4%, τ = 0.41, 95% CrI 0.05–0.96). These findings support thin labial bone plate status as a contributor to heterogeneity in the supporting analysis. Nevertheless, the point estimate of τ remained below the prespecified exploratory MCID, suggesting that the clinical impact of heterogeneity may not be substantial; however, the 95% CrI for τ included the MCID, indicating uncertainty.

### Note on the assessment of publication bias and the assessment of evidence quality

Because the full inclusion analysis incorporated known sources of clinical heterogeneity and τ exceeded the prespecified exploratory MCID, we considered its pooled estimates potentially less clinically interpretable. In contrast, the clinically restricted analysis provided greater clinical comparability and lower heterogeneity. Accordingly, we assessed publication bias and certainty of evidence primarily within the clinically restricted analysis to provide an evaluation based on a more interpretable subset of evidence.

## Discussion

This study provides the first network meta-analysis comparing PES-SO across three socket shield technique operative methods (SST, SST-GBR, and SST-nJP) and two conventional immediate implant operative methods (IIP and IIP-GBR) in the esthetic zone. In terms of PES-SO improvement, IIP was significantly inferior to SST-GBR, SST, and IIP-GBR. No statistically significant differences were observed among SST, and SST-GBR in pairwise comparisons. Probability ranking suggested that SST-nJP may have an numerically potential signal; however, the findings regarding SST-nJP in this study should be interpreted with caution, as the analysis is primarily based on a single small-sample study [35] and indirect comparisons.

Our findings are partially consistent with prior pairwise meta-analyses, which reported that socket shield techniques yield better short-term esthetic outcomes than conventional immediate implant placement [12, 20–23]. However, previous reviews generally compared one socket shield approach with one conventional approach and did not differentiate among socket shield operative variants [12, 20–23]. By leveraging the network structure, the present study enables a unified comparison of the relative performance of five operative methods.

Biologically, the socket shield technique retains a portion of the buccal root, preserving the periodontal ligament and associated blood supply, thereby mitigating physiologic alveolar bone resorption after extraction [2]. In the present analyses, two socket shield technique operative methods (SST and SST-GBR) achieved significantly greater PES-SO improvement than IIP and IIP-GBR. These findings align with the proposed biological rationale of the socket shield concept [15]. In contrast, conventional immediate implant placement (with or without GBR) may not fully prevent resorption of the labial bone plate [5, 53, 54], whereas retaining the buccal root fragment may provide a more stable hard-tissue substrate for favorable esthetic outcomes.

Across all analyses, no statistically significant differences were detected among SST and SST-GBR. This pattern suggests that the presence of the shield itself may be a dominant determinant of short-term PES-SO, rather than whether grafting is performed within the jumping gap (SST-GBR vs. SST). SST-nJP showed an numerically potential signal in probability ranking. However, the findings regarding SST-nJP in this study should be interpreted with caution, as the analysis is primarily based on a single small-sample study and indirect comparisons; therefore, it is insufficient to justify a clinical priority recommendation at present.

The supporting analysis, which included patients across all labial bone plate thicknesses, showed increased heterogeneity and a modest instability in ranking between SST and SST-GBR. Anatomically, thin labial bone plates may have less favorable perfusion, and osseointegration may differ from that in non-thin plates [30]. In such patients, decisions regarding grafting and gap management may exert a more pronounced influence on healing, thereby increasing variability and reducing precision.

In addition, several studies had limitations in methodological reporting that may affect comparability across studies, with these limitations concentrated in specific comparison arms (SST-GBR vs. IIP-GBR) [32, 36, 40, 42, 48–50]. These issues likely contributed to heterogeneity in the full inclusion analysis.

Despite being based on 12 studies, the clinically restricted analysis still relied on single-study evidence for some comparisons involving SST-nJP [35] and did not form a closed loop for all contrasts, indicating a fragile evidence pathway. Consistent with this, CINeMA assessment rated SST versus SST-nJP as very low certainty due to within-study bias and imprecision, indicating substantial uncertainty for this comparison.

Although the funnel plot appeared symmetric and Egger’s test was not statistically significant (P = 0.16), the limited number of included studies reduces power to detect small-study effects. The stability of the main efficacy pattern across sensitivity analyses partially mitigates this concern; however, publication bias or small-study effects cannot be excluded.

This analysis is limited to PES-SO. However, surgical procedures using sleeve abutments require an evaluation of safety and biology-related outcomes (e.g., abutment exposure, infection, marginal bone resorption, implant survival, and soft tissue recession). Due to the limited number of studies included and inadequate reporting of outcome measures, this study was unable to comprehensively discuss the aforementioned safety and biology-related outcomes, which constitutes one of its limitations.

Given the significant differences among the included studies in terms of baseline bone phenotype, gap width, shield dimensions, grafting materials, and tooth position, the assumption of transitivity is questionable. Furthermore, due to incomplete reporting of covariates in the included studies, we were unable to conduct a quantitative assessment of transitivity. Therefore, our findings should be considered exploratory, and the observed results await validation in future well-designed head-to-head trials.

Residual clinical heterogeneity may also remain, because reporting of operative details (e.g., shield height, thickness, and morphology of the retained root fragment) varied across studies [20]. Moreover, while the full inclusion analysis was retained to reflect the full scope of available evidence, it was not accompanied by a parallel formal assessment of publication bias or certainty of evidence, which should be considered a limitation.

Caution is advised when interpreting the PES-SO MCID (0.619) used in this study. This MCID was estimated using a distribution-based approach and reflects only the characteristics of the data, it does not incorporate anchor validation based on patients or doctors rating and lacks clinical relevance. Therefore, the MCID in this study should be regarded as an exploratory reference tool to aid statistical decision-making, rather than a clinically validated, precise minimal clinically important difference. All conclusions drawn based on it should be interpreted as exploratory. Future studies should establish anchor-based MCIDs for PES-related outcomes to provide more clinically grounded thresholds for interpreting esthetic differences.

Finally, this analysis focused on short-term follow-up (4–12 months) and therefore cannot address potential long-term differences among operative methods. Although cohort studies suggest long-term advantages of socket shield techniques [16, 17], head-to-head long-term comparisons across socket shield variants remain limited.

Future randomized controlled trials are warranted to directly compare SST-nJP, SST, and SST-GBR. The numerically potential signal for SST-nJP suggested by probability ranking should be validated in adequately powered RCTs.

## Conclusion

In the overall evidence network including all 21 eligible studies, IIP was associated with significantly worse PES-SO than IIP-GBR, SST, and SST-GBR, whereas no statistically significant differences were detected among SST, and SST-GBR. The findings regarding SST-nJP in this study should be interpreted with caution, as the analysis is primarily based on a single small-sample study and indirect comparisons.

The assumption of transitivity included in this study is open to debate and cannot be quantitatively assessed. The prespecified exploratory MCID used in this study was estimated using a distribution-based approach and reflects only the characteristics of the data, it does not incorporate anchor validation based on patients or doctors rating and lacks clinical relevance. Therefore, the findings of this study should be considered exploratory rather than definitive clinical guidelines.

In the clinically restricted analysis (patients with non-thin labial bone plates from studies without limitations in methodological reporting that may affect comparability across studies), the overall pattern of effects observed in the full evidence network remained unchanged. In the supporting analysis (all patients from studies without limitations in methodological reporting that may affect comparability across studies), the overall pattern similarly remained unchanged. Nevertheless, ranking orders and some indirect comparisons varied across analyses.

Collectively, these exploratory findings suggest that thin labial bone plate status may be an important source of clinical heterogeneity and warrants targeted investigation in future well-designed clinical trials. Further high-quality head-to-head randomized controlled trials are needed to consolidate these findings and inform clinical decision-making.

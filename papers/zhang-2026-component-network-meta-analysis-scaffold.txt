## ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlveolar ridge preservation (ARP) involves the use of multiple treatment components, including scaffold materials, platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) adjuncts, and barrier membranes. However, conventional meta-analyses generally evaluate complete treatment protocols and cannot determine the independent contribution of individual components. This study aimed to investigate the component-level effects of ARP interventions using component network meta-analysis (CNMA).
MethodsRandomized controlled trials evaluating ARP after tooth extraction in adults were identified from PubMed, Cochrane CENTRAL, Embase, and Web of Science from inception to July 2026. Complete treatment strategies were initially compared using random-effects network meta-analysis. Subsequently, interventions were decomposed into scaffold, PRF, and barrier membrane components for additive and interaction CNMA. The primary outcomes were horizontal ridge-width change and buccal vertical ridge-height change.
ResultsThirty-one randomized controlled trials were included. Standard network meta-analysis demonstrated that several scaffold- and membrane-containing strategies were associated with reduced ridge dimensional loss compared with natural healing. In additive CNMA for horizontal ridge-width change, barrier membrane showed the largest estimated component effect (MD, −1.31 mm; 95% CI, −2.18 to −0.44), followed by allograft (MD, −0.77 mm; 95% CI, −1.16 to −0.38) and PRF adjunct (MD, −0.40 mm; 95% CI, −0.66 to −0.14). Interaction analyses did not demonstrate convincing evidence that the effect of PRF varied according to scaffold type, although estimates were limited by imprecision.
ConclusionsThis component network meta-analysis suggests that the effects of alveolar ridge preservation are associated with the combined contribution of individual treatment components rather than complete treatment packages alone. Barrier membranes and allograft components showed relatively favorable component-level estimates for horizontal ridge-width preservation, whereas PRF showed a smaller incremental effect. However, these findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of superiority over other regenerative components, given network inconsistency, sparse evidence for some components, and the low or very low certainty of several component-level estimates.Systematic Review Registration: identifier CRD420261459356.

## Introduction

Tooth extraction initiates a remodeling process that can lead to substantial dimensional changes of the alveolar ridge, particularly during the early stages of healing (1). Horizontal ridge reduction and buccal plate resorption may compromise prosthetically driven implant placement, increase the need for additional augmentation procedures, and affect esthetic outcomes (2). Alveolar ridge preservation (ARP) has therefore been widely adopted to minimize post-extraction dimensional alterations and maintain favorable conditions for subsequent implant rehabilitation (3, 4). However, ARP is not a single therapeutic procedure, and the most effective combination of biomaterials and adjunctive approaches remains unclear.

Current ARP protocols involve a variety of materials and techniques, including xenogeneic, allogeneic, alloplastic, collagen-based, and tooth-derived scaffolds, as well as biologic adjuncts such as platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) and different barrier or socket-sealing strategies (4, 5). In clinical practice, these approaches are frequently combined rather than applied independently. For example, a graft material may be used together with PRF or a membrane to enhance space maintenance, wound stability, or tissue regeneration (6). As a result, differences observed between ARP protocols may reflect not only the choice of a specific product but also the underlying combination of treatment components.

Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have investigated the effectiveness of ARP interventions; however, the relative contribution of individual treatment components remains incompletely understood (5–7). Pairwise meta-analysis can provide estimates for specific head-to-head comparisons, whereas standard network meta-analysis allows comparison of multiple complete treatment strategies using direct and indirect evidence (8). Nevertheless, these approaches generally evaluate each protocol as an integrated intervention and cannot determine whether observed effects are primarily attributable to the scaffold material, PRF adjunct, barrier membrane, or their combination. This limitation is clinically relevant because treatment decisions in ARP are typically based on selecting and combining multiple components rather than choosing a single intervention. Component network meta-analysis (CNMA) offers an alternative framework by separating complex interventions into predefined components and estimating their individual contributions while accounting for the structure of the treatment network (9, 10). Given the multifactorial nature of ARP protocols, CNMA may provide additional insight into which components are most consistently associated with ridge preservation and whether potential interactions exist between different treatment elements.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the comparative effectiveness of scaffold materials, PRF adjuncts, and barrier membranes for alveolar ridge preservation after tooth extraction using evidence from randomized controlled trials. Standard network meta-analysis was conducted to compare complete ARP strategies, while CNMA was applied to estimate component-level effects and explore potential interactions between treatment components.

## Materials and methods

### Protocol and reporting

This systematic review and component network meta-analysis was conducted according to PRISMA and the PRISMA extension for network meta-analysis (11, 12). The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO before formal data extraction (CRD420261459356).

### Research question and eligibility criteria

The primary research question was formulated as follows: “Among adults undergoing permanent tooth extraction followed by alveolar ridge preservation, what are the comparative effects of different scaffold materials, platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) adjuncts, and barrier membrane strategies on alveolar ridge dimensional preservation and related clinical outcomes?”

The eligibility criteria were defined according to the PICOS framework. Population (P): Adults (≥18 years) requiring extraction of permanent teeth and subsequent alveolar ridge preservation. Studies involving third molar extraction alone were excluded unless the reported outcomes were directly relevant to implant-site preservation. Intervention (I): ARP strategies involving scaffold materials (including xenografts, allografts, alloplasts, collagen-based materials, and tooth/dentin-derived grafts), PRF-based biological adjuncts, barrier membranes, or combinations of these components. Comparator (C): Eligible comparators included spontaneous healing/blood clot healing, PRF alone, scaffold-based approaches, membrane-assisted approaches, or other ARP protocols. Outcomes (O): The primary outcomes were horizontal ridge-width change and buccal vertical ridge-height change after ARP. Secondary outcomes included histomorphometric parameters, implant-related outcomes, patient-reported outcomes, and treatment-related adverse events. Study design (S): Only randomized controlled trials (RCTs), including parallel-group, split-mouth, and multi-arm designs, were eligible.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) non-randomized studies, observational studies, case reports, case series, reviews, editorials, and conference abstracts without sufficient extractable data; (2) animal or in vitro studies; (3) studies evaluating extraction procedures unrelated to ARP, such as immediate implant placement without a separate ARP intervention; (4) studies focusing exclusively on third molar extraction without relevance to implant-site preservation; (5) studies without sufficient information for intervention classification, quantitative synthesis, or component coding.

### Information sources and study selection

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and Web of Science from database inception to July 2026. The search strategy was developed using controlled vocabulary and free-text terms related to alveolar ridge preservation, socket preservation, platelet-rich fibrin (PRF), graft materials, barrier membranes, and randomized controlled trials. Detailed search strategies for each database are provided in Supplementary Tables S1, S2.

All retrieved records were imported into a reference management system, and duplicates were removed before screening. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text assessment according to the prespecified eligibility criteria. Any disagreement between reviewers was resolved through discussion, with adjudication by a third reviewer when consensus could not be reached. The reasons for full-text exclusion were documented, and the overall selection process was presented using PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1) (11).

[FIGURE] PRISMA flow diagram of study selection.

### Data extraction and component coding

Two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized and pilot-tested extraction form. Extracted information included study characteristics (first author, publication year, country and study design), participant and socket characteristics, intervention and comparator details, PRF preparation protocols, membrane characteristics, follow-up duration, outcome definitions, and numerical outcome data. For multi-arm trials, data from each eligible intervention arm were extracted separately. For split-mouth trials, the paired design and unit of analysis were recorded to account for potential dependence between treatment sites. When multiple reports originated from the same trial, they were considered as a single study and the most complete report was used as the primary data source.

Interventions were coded at two levels. First, complete treatment strategies were classified as treatment nodes for the standard network meta-analysis. Second, each intervention was decomposed into predefined components for component network meta-analysis (CNMA). The prespecified component framework included xenograft, allograft, alloplast, collagen-based scaffold, tooth/dentin-derived scaffold, PRF adjunct, and barrier membrane. In the primary CNMA, different PRF preparations, including L-PRF, A-PRF, injectable PRF, titanium-prepared PRF and related variants, were grouped as a single PRF adjunct component because of their shared biological rationale and limited comparative evidence. Alternative PRF coding strategies were evaluated in sensitivity analyses when data availability permitted. To assess the separability of component effects, we examined the distribution of each component across the treatment network, including its occurrence as a standalone intervention and in combination with other components.

### Outcomes and risk of bias

The primary outcomes were changes in horizontal ridge width and buccal vertical ridge height following tooth extraction, measured in millimeters. For dimensional outcomes, greater preservation was defined as a smaller reduction in ridge dimensions compared with baseline or natural healing. Secondary outcomes included changes in lingual or palatal vertical ridge height, histomorphometric parameters (including newly formed bone, residual graft material and non-mineralized tissue), requirement for additional augmentation, implant placement feasibility, patient-reported outcomes and adverse events. Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool (13). The assessment covered the predefined RoB 2 domains, with additional consideration given to methodological issues specific to split-mouth trials, including appropriate handling of paired data, potential carry-across effects and the independence of extraction sites. When multiple follow-up assessments were available, a single prespecified time point closest to the typical implant-site evaluation period was selected for the primary analysis to avoid multiple inclusion of data from the same study. A 4–6-month follow-up window was prespecified for sensitivity analysis to reduce clinical heterogeneity related to differences in healing and remodeling periods and because this interval commonly corresponds to clinical reassessment of ridge dimensions and implant-site readiness after ARP. For consistency, the measured outcomes were termed “horizontal ridge-width change” and “buccal vertical ridge-height change,” whereas “ridge-dimensional loss” referred to the broader post-extraction dimensional reduction process.

### Statistical analysis

Direct comparisons supported by at least two studies were initially synthesized using random-effects pairwise meta-analysis. Standard random-effects network meta-analysis (NMA) was subsequently performed using complete intervention strategies as treatment nodes, allowing estimation of relative effects among all connected interventions through direct and indirect evidence (8, 15). Continuous outcomes were summarized as mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For parallel-group trials, the participant or extraction site was used as the analytical unit according to the design and reporting of the original study. For split-mouth trials, the paired design and reported unit of analysis were recorded during data extraction. When paired effect estimates or within-participant correlation coefficients were unavailable, arm-level data were analyzed as reported; the resulting potential unit-of-analysis limitation was considered in the interpretation of the findings. For multi-arm trials, correlations among comparisons arising from the same study were accounted for within the NMA framework to avoid double counting of shared intervention or comparator groups. For dimensional outcomes, effect directions were harmonized such that negative MDs indicated less dimensional loss and therefore favored better ridge preservation. Network geometry was examined using network plots, while between-study heterogeneity and network inconsistency were assessed using established statistical approaches. For component network meta-analysis (CNMA) (9, 10), complete interventions were decomposed into predefined components, including scaffold materials, platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) adjuncts and barrier membranes. Additive CNMA was used to estimate the independent contribution of each component, assuming that the overall effect of a combined intervention could be represented by the additive effects of its constituent components. The additive CNMA model assumes that the relative effect of a complete intervention can be approximated by the sum of its predefined component effects in the absence of specified interactions. Component exchangeability assumes that a given component contributes a sufficiently comparable underlying effect across the treatment combinations in which it occurs. Transitivity was considered by examining potential effect modifiers across treatment comparisons, including follow-up duration, socket morphology, tooth location, measurement method, PRF preparation, and membrane use. Consistency between direct and indirect evidence was evaluated using global and local inconsistency assessments. Given the heterogeneity of materials and techniques within several component categories, component-level estimates were interpreted as average effects within the observed evidence network rather than as product-specific effects.

Prespecified interaction models were further explored to assess potential effect modification between PRF and scaffold or membrane components when sufficient evidence was available. The standard NMA, additive CNMA and interaction CNMA models were compared using approximate AIC, residual deviance, between-study heterogeneity, and model complexity. A lower approximate AIC was interpreted as indicating a more favorable balance between model fit and parsimony rather than uniformly better absolute model fit. All analyses were performed in R software using the netmeta and netcomb packages (14).

### Sensitivity analyses and certainty assessment

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the findings under different analytical assumptions. These analyses included exclusion of studies at high risk of bias, restriction to the prespecified 4–6-month follow-up window, alternative coding strategies for PRF-related interventions, and alternative classifications of scaffold materials. For the primary dimensional outcomes, potential publication bias and small-study effects were assessed descriptively using comparison-adjusted funnel plots where applicable. Formal statistical tests for funnel-plot asymmetry were not performed because several treatment nodes and direct comparisons were informed by a limited number of studies, making such tests unreliable. The certainty of evidence for treatment effects derived from the standard network meta-analysis was assessed using CINeMA (16, 17), incorporating within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity and incoherence. For CNMA estimates, certainty was assessed using a structured narrative approach because formal grading methods for component-level effects are not yet fully standardized. The assessment considered the availability and distribution of supporting evidence, component identifiability within the network, precision of the estimate, heterogeneity and inconsistency, model dependence, and risk of bias of the contributing studies. Lower certainty judgments were generally associated with sparse or unevenly distributed evidence, imprecision, limited component identifiability, sensitivity to model specification, or important heterogeneity or inconsistency within the underlying network.

## Results

### Study selection

The database search identified 3,161 records, of which 1,294 duplicates were removed. After title and abstract screening, 130 articles were retrieved for full-text assessment. Following application of the prespecified eligibility criteria, 31 randomized controlled trials were included in the qualitative synthesis. Of these, 16 studies provided eligible data for horizontal ridge-width change and were included in the corresponding standard NMA and CNMA, whereas 14 studies provided eligible data for buccal vertical ridge-height change and were included in the corresponding standard NMA and CNMA. The remaining studies were retained in the qualitative synthesis but did not contribute to the primary quantitative networks because they did not report the relevant dimensional outcomes, provided insufficient numerical data for meta-analysis, reported outcomes that could not be harmonized with the prespecified definitions, or reported only secondary outcomes. The study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (Figure 1).

### Study characteristics

The 31 included randomized controlled trials were published between 2008 and 2026 and were conducted across 17 countries or regions (18–48). The sample sizes ranged from 8 to 88 participants, with a total of 932 participants and 853 extraction sites reported across the included studies. Most trials adopted parallel-group designs, while seven used split-mouth designs and five included three or more intervention arms. Follow-up durations ranged from approximately 8 weeks to 7 months, with the majority of ridge dimensional and histomorphometric assessments performed within 3–6 months after tooth extraction. ### Risk of bias

Among the 31 included trials, 6 were judged to have a low risk of bias, 20 had some concerns, and 5 were judged to have a high risk of bias. The main sources of uncertainty were related to incomplete reporting of the randomization process and limitations in outcome measurement and blinding. High-risk judgments were predominantly attributed to concerns regarding outcome assessment. For split-mouth trials, additional considerations included the handling of paired data, potential carry-across effects and the independence of extraction sites (Figure 4; Supplementary Table S6; Figure S1).

### Standard network meta-analysis of primary outcomes

For all dimensional outcomes, negative MDs indicated less dimensional loss relative to the comparator and therefore favored ridge preservation.

For horizontal ridge-width change, the random-effects network meta-analysis showed that several complete ARP strategies were associated with reduced ridge dimensional loss compared with natural healing. The largest effects were observed for dentin-derived grafts (MD −2.07 mm, 95% CI −3.17 to −0.97), xenograft combined with barrier membrane (MD −1.78 mm, 95% CI −2.57 to −0.99), and allograft combined with barrier membrane (MD −1.78 mm, 95% CI −3.06 to −0.50). Allograft combined with PRF was also associated with less horizontal ridge-width reduction (MD −1.28 mm, 95% CI −1.93 to −0.63). In contrast, PRF alone showed a favorable direction of effect, although the confidence interval included the null value (Supplementary Tables S7, S8).

For buccal vertical ridge-height change, allograft combined with barrier membrane demonstrated the greatest reduction in vertical ridge-height loss compared with natural healing (MD −3.03 mm, 95% CI −4.86 to −1.20), followed by dentin-derived graft combined with PRF (MD −2.49 mm, 95% CI −3.85 to −1.12) and xenograft combined with barrier membrane (MD −2.23 mm, 95% CI −3.53 to −0.93). Several additional interventions showed directionally favorable effects, although estimates were associated with wider confidence intervals due to limited evidence for some treatment nodes. Complete network estimates and league tables are presented in Figure 5A, Table 3 and Supplementary Tables S9, S10.

### Component network meta-analysis

#### Independent component effects

In the additive component network meta-analysis for horizontal ridge-width change, barrier membrane was associated with the largest independent component effect among the evaluated ARP components (MD −1.31 mm, 95% CI −2.18 to −0.44). Allograft also showed a statistically significant component-level association with reduced ridge-width loss (MD −0.77 mm, 95% CI −1.16 to −0.38).

The PRF adjunct component demonstrated a smaller but statistically significant effect (MD −0.40 mm, 95% CI −0.66 to −0.14). Estimates for xenograft (MD −0.57 mm, 95% CI −1.20 to 0.06) and collagen-based scaffold (MD −0.42 mm, 95% CI −1.07 to 0.23) were directionally favorable but did not reach statistical significance. No statistically detectable independent effect was observed for the alloplast component (MD 0.01 mm, 95% CI −0.51 to 0.53). Component-level estimates are presented in Figure 5B, Table 4 and Supplementary Table S11.

#### Interaction effects

Interaction component network meta-analysis was performed to explore whether the effect of PRF differed according to scaffold type. The estimated interaction effect between xenograft and PRF was 0.28 mm (95% CI −1.21 to 1.76), while the interaction between alloplast and PRF was 0.60 mm (95% CI −0.97 to 2.18). Neither interaction estimate provided statistical evidence of a component-level interaction. The collagen scaffold × PRF interaction could not be estimated because of insufficient connected evidence. Interaction estimates are presented in Figure 6A and Supplementary Table S12.

#### Model comparison

The additive CNMA showed improved model parsimony compared with the standard NMA and interaction CNMA, with a lower approximate AIC (39.24) and lower between-study variance (τ2 = 0.072) than the standard NMA (AIC = 42.44; τ2 = 0.101) and interaction CNMA (AIC = 42.32; τ2 = 0.091). However, the residual deviance of the additive CNMA was higher than that of the standard NMA, indicating that its lower approximate AIC and reduced between-study variance should not be interpreted as uniformly better absolute model fit. Rather, the lower approximate AIC indicates a more favorable balance between model fit and complexity and supports a more parsimonious representation of the available evidence. The interaction model introduced additional parameters without providing a clear overall advantage across the model-comparison criteria. Model comparison results are summarized in Figure 6B and Supplementary Table S13.

### Secondary outcomes

Histomorphometric outcomes were reported in 16 studies, with the most frequently assessed parameters being newly formed bone, residual graft material and connective or non-mineralized tissue. Quantitative synthesis using network or component network meta-analysis was not performed because of substantial variation in outcome definitions, measurement methods, biopsy protocols and follow-up periods. Across the available studies, scaffold-based ARP strategies generally showed greater new bone formation or hard-tissue maturation compared with spontaneous healing; however, the magnitude of these effects varied according to biomaterial characteristics and assessment methods (Supplementary Table S18).

Patient-reported outcomes, including postoperative pain, swelling, early soft-tissue healing and analgesic consumption, were reported inconsistently across trials, with considerable variation in measurement scales and assessment time points. Safety outcomes, including infection, wound dehiscence, membrane exposure and graft loss, were infrequently reported and were generally based on small numbers of events. Owing to the limited and heterogeneous reporting of these outcomes, they were summarized descriptively rather than incorporated into network-level analyses (Supplementary Table S19).

### Sensitivity, heterogeneity and inconsistency

Sensitivity analyses showed that the overall pattern of component effects was largely consistent across alternative analytical scenarios. After excluding studies at high risk of bias, barrier membrane and allograft remained associated with favorable component-level estimates, whereas the estimated contribution of PRF remained smaller. Restriction to the prespecified 4–6-month follow-up window resulted in fewer connected comparisons but did not substantially alter the direction of the main findings. Alternative PRF coding strategies, including separate classification of PRF–membrane preparations and other PRF formulations, produced comparable component estimates (Supplementary Table S15).

Network heterogeneity was moderate for the primary outcomes, with variability observed across treatment comparisons. Global inconsistency testing indicated evidence of inconsistency within the primary networks, particularly for horizontal ridge-width change (between-design Q = 19.90, P = 0.006). However, node-splitting analyses did not identify statistically significant disagreement between direct and indirect estimates for individual comparisons. Potential contributors to heterogeneity and inconsistency included differences in socket morphology, follow-up duration, outcome measurement methods, scaffold composition, membrane use and PRF preparation protocols (Supplementary Table S14; Figure S5).

Comparison-adjusted funnel plots for the primary dimensional outcomes were examined descriptively for potential small-study effects (Supplementary Figures S6, S7). No formal statistical tests for funnel-plot asymmetry were performed because of the limited number of studies contributing to several treatment nodes and comparisons. Accordingly, the presence of publication bias or small-study effects could not be reliably excluded.

## Discussion

### Main findings and clinical interpretation

The main finding of this study is that the effects of alveolar ridge preservation (ARP) are better understood as the combined contributions of individual treatment components rather than the performance of a single preferred intervention protocol. While standard network meta-analysis (NMA) identified several effective scaffolds- and membrane-containing strategies, component network meta-analysis (CNMA) provided additional insight by separating the independent contributions of scaffold materials, barrier membranes and platelet-rich fibrin (PRF).

In the additive CNMA, barrier membrane showed the largest independent contribution to horizontal ridge-width preservation (MD −1.31 mm, 95% CI −2.18 to −0.44), followed by allograft (MD −0.77 mm, 95% CI −1.16 to −0.38). PRF demonstrated a statistically significant but smaller incremental effect (MD −0.40 mm, 95% CI −0.66 to −0.14). These findings suggest that structural components involved in space maintenance and stabilization may contribute more substantially to dimensional preservation than biologic adjuncts alone. However, the results should not be interpreted as evidence that one material is universally superior, because component effects were estimated within an uneven evidence network. Treatment rankings from the standard NMA should be interpreted cautiously because several nodes were supported by only one or a few small trials. P-scores reflect the relative ordering of treatments within the available network and should be interpreted alongside the corresponding effect estimates, 95% CIs, the amount of supporting evidence, and evidence certainty, rather than as definitive evidence of treatment superiority.

From a clinical perspective, these findings provide an evidence-informed framework for interpreting the potential contributions of individual components within ARP strategies (3, 49). Within this framework, membrane and scaffold components showed relatively favorable associations with dimensional preservation, whereas PRF appeared to provide a smaller adjunctive contribution (53, 54). The absence of identifiable scaffold–PRF interactions further suggests that the available evidence does not currently support selecting PRF according to a specific scaffold combination.

### Comparison with previous evidence and biological interpretation

Previous systematic reviews have demonstrated that ARP reduces post-extraction ridge dimensional changes compared with spontaneous healing (49). Consistent with this evidence, the present NMA showed favorable effects for several graft- and membrane-containing strategies. However, previous pairwise and network meta-analyses mainly compared complete treatment protocols, making it difficult to determine whether observed benefits were driven by graft materials, membranes, biologic adjuncts or their combinations (50–52).

The CNMA framework provides a different interpretation of these findings. A highly ranked treatment protocol may include multiple active components, and treatment-level comparisons cannot determine which component contributes most to the observed effect. In the present analysis, the favorable performance of several complete protocols appeared to be largely associated with membrane and scaffold components rather than PRF alone.

The contribution of barrier membranes is biologically plausible because membranes may stabilize the socket environment, limit soft-tissue collapse and maintain a protected space during early healing (53, 54). Nevertheless, the membrane component represented different barrier materials and clinical approaches, including collagen-based, synthetic and PRF-derived membranes. Therefore, the estimated effect should be interpreted as an average component-level effect rather than evidence supporting a specific membrane type. Similarly, the allograft component showed a consistent favorable association, which may reflect its ability to maintain space and provide a scaffold during early remodeling (50, 51). However, the present findings do not establish superiority of allograft over other graft categories. Differences in evidence volume, component combinations and clinical application may partly explain the precision of the allograft estimate.

PRF has been proposed to enhance angiogenesis, cellular recruitment and early wound healing through its fibrin matrix and bioactive mediators (55). In the present analysis, the estimated PRF component effect of approximately 0.4 mm was statistically detectable but modest in magnitude. Its clinical relevance remains uncertain and may depend on the clinical context; even a small reduction in dimensional loss could be relevant in selected esthetically demanding or anatomically constrained sites. However, the current evidence is insufficient to support PRF as a substitute for scaffold or membrane components when structural support and space maintenance are required. The relatively smaller dimensional effect observed in this analysis does not contradict these biological functions. Rather, it suggests that biologic stimulation alone may have a limited influence on maintaining external ridge dimensions, which are strongly affected by mechanical stability and space preservation. Furthermore, variation among PRF preparation protocols may have contributed to the heterogeneity of the estimated effect (6, 7, 56–58).

### Methodological implications of CNMA

Standard NMA allows comparison of multiple treatment strategies but treats each intervention as a complete package (8, 15). In contrast, CNMA decomposes complex interventions into shared components and estimates their individual contributions (9, 10). This distinction is particularly relevant for ARP, where clinical protocols are constructed through combinations of graft materials, membranes and biologic adjuncts. The present analysis demonstrates that treatment rankings from conventional NMA should be interpreted cautiously. A highly ranked protocol may reflect the combined presence of several components rather than the effect of a single intervention. By estimating component-level effects, CNMA provides additional information that may be more directly relevant to clinical decision-making. However, CNMA relies on several assumptions. The additive model assumes that combined interventions can be approximated by the sum of individual component effects. Although the additive model showed better fit and greater parsimony than alternative models, statistical performance does not necessarily indicate biological independence. Component effects may vary according to material properties, surgical technique, timing and unmeasured interactions (9, 10, 15). Similarly, the absence of statistically significant scaffold–PRF interactions should be interpreted as insufficient evidence rather than evidence of no interaction. Future factorial or adequately powered multi-arm trials will be required to determine whether specific scaffold–PRF combinations produce synergistic effects.

### Limitations, certainty and future directions

Several limitations should be considered. First, some treatment nodes and components were supported by limited numbers of trials, resulting in imprecise estimates. Second, component categories included heterogeneous materials and techniques, particularly for membranes, scaffolds and PRF preparations. Therefore, the estimated effects represent average effects across broader component classes rather than specific products or protocols. Third, clinical heterogeneity and potential violations of transitivity remain important considerations. Differences in socket morphology, buccal-wall integrity, surgical procedures, measurement methods and follow-up periods may have influenced relative treatment effects. Global inconsistency was detected in the primary networks and may reduce confidence in some treatment- and component-level estimates, even though local node-splitting analyses did not identify statistically significant disagreement for individual comparisons. In addition, several components were supported by sparse or unevenly distributed evidence, and uneven component co-occurrence may limit the ability of the model to separate individual component effects from the treatment combinations in which they were evaluated. Although sensitivity analyses did not materially alter the overall interpretation of the component effects, these limitations reduce confidence in some component-level estimates. Accordingly, the CNMA estimates should be interpreted as evidence-informed average component effects within the observed network rather than as definitive evidence of superiority of individual components. Fourth, several split-mouth trials did not report paired effect estimates or within-participant correlation coefficients; therefore, residual unit-of-analysis issues arising from within-participant dependence cannot be fully excluded. Fifth, publication bias and small-study effects could not be reliably excluded because several treatment nodes and comparisons were supported by relatively few studies, limiting the interpretability of funnel-plot asymmetry. Sixth, the available evidence was insufficient for robust component-level analysis of several clinically relevant outcomes, including histomorphometric parameters, implant-related outcomes, patient-reported outcomes and adverse events. Future ARP trials should adopt standardized outcome definitions, report baseline socket characteristics and describe scaffold, membrane and biologic adjunct components separately. Factorial designs comparing identical scaffolds with and without PRF or membrane use would be particularly valuable for estimating component interactions. Beyond ARP, this study illustrates the potential application of CNMA to other regenerative interventions composed of multiple therapeutic elements. However, meaningful component inference requires transparent reporting, adequate network connectivity and careful evaluation of additivity and transitivity assumptions (9, 10, 15).

## Conclusions

This component network meta-analysis indicates that the effects of alveolar ridge preservation are likely driven by the combined contribution of individual treatment components rather than by any single intervention protocol. This CNMA suggests that barrier membranes and allograft components were associated with relatively favorable component-level estimates for horizontal ridge-width preservation, whereas PRF showed a smaller incremental effect. However, these findings do not constitute definitive evidence of superiority over other regenerative components and should be considered cautiously given network inconsistency, sparse evidence for some components, and the low or very low certainty of several estimates. Available evidence was insufficient to determine whether PRF provides scaffold-specific synergistic effects. These findings support an evidence-informed, component-based interpretation of complex ARP strategies, rather than a definitive clinical treatment hierarchy.

TITLE: Evaluation of bone apposition on surface modified titanium implant in experimental animal model: A systematic review and meta-analysis
DOI: 10.4103/jisp.jisp_222_22

INTRODUCTION

The advent of newer dental implant materials, surface structures, and designs has markedly improved their clinical application over the years. A long-term success and survival rate has been reported with the most commonly available endosseous dental implant systems.[] Adequate osseointegration appears to be a prerequisite in terms of measuring the success of a dental implant.[] As defined by Albrektsson, osseointegration is a “Direct structural and functional connection between living bone and the surface of a load-bearing titanium implant.” The implant material, implant design, implant surface characteristics, the position of the bone, surgical technique and the implant loading conditions were considered essential features for implant to osseointegrate. In order to promote tissue integration and better mechanical stability, a number of modifications have been introduced regarding implant material, design and surface characteristics.[] Titanium and its compounds have been found to be the most reliable implant material in terms of their inertness, mechanical strength, mechanical and biological stability, biocompatibility, and bone apposition characteristics.[] Numerous methods of implant surface modifications have been adopted to optimize roughness and topography of endosseous implant surfaces to maximize osseointegration. Such implants possess various surface characteristics ranging from machined/turned surfaces to surface roughness created by sandblasting, acid etching, coatings of osseoinductive materials such as hydroxyapatite (HA), calcium phosphate, ceramics or deposition of osseoinductive materials such as bone morphogenetic proteins (BMP’s), growth factors, and collagen chitosan polymers.[] Osseointegration proceeds as two events, first is primary stability or mechanical stability which usually rely on compactness and thickness of bone and implant designs, whereas the secondary stability or biological stability implies to interrelation between the exterior aspect of the implant and haemorrhagic site. Thus, a successful implant integration is dependent on the factors such as bone density and quality, implant type, design surface modifications, and various surgical procedures.[] Preclinical animal studies are carried out to analyze the biocompatibility and tissue response of newer implant materials. Pellegrini. discussed the relevance of preclinical implant animal studies to human situation and described the prediction of human trials through these studies. Prior to human clinical exposure, animal testing is conducted on a range of species from experimental mice to larger animals, for example, dogs or nonhuman primates. Preclinical trials involving smaller species are conducted to establish disease-associated research whereas large species provide information regarding tissue healing.[]

The methods that were used for the experimental evaluation of the osseointegration of the implant surfaces to assess implant stability were the histomorphometric analysis of peri-implant bone. Further methods that were used for the evaluation of the bone apposition are removal torque value analysis (RTV) and the Implant stability Quotient (ISQ). ISQ is a noninvasive technique which quantifies implant stability where higher values suggest high stability, and lower values suggest implant instability.[]

Although, there have been vast number of animal studies mentioned in earlier literature but still data is inconclusive and unclear as to whether which surface modifications of implant provide successful osseointegration. “Does the surface modification of dental implants have an impact on bone apposition in peri-implant area?”

Hence, the objective of current systematic review and consecutive meta-analysis was to primarily appraise published evidence writings pertaining to the proposed research question conducted between 2000 and 2020 and to secondarily analyze whether surface modified dental implants show an enhanced peri-implant bone formation in comparison to machined surface in healthy experimental animal models.

PICO framework and focused question

This systematic review and meta-analysis was carried out on the basis of PRISMA recommendations (preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses). The study strategy was conducted using the PICO framework.[]

The focused question was, “To assess bone apposition on surface modified titanium implant surfaces in healthy experimental animal model?”

P (POPULATION): Healthy animal models with placement of titanium implant in intraoral and extraoral sites

I (INTERVENTION): Surface modified implant (either subtractive or additive)

C (COMPARISON): Machined (turned) implants

O (OUTCOMES): Bone implant contact percentage (BIC %), (RTV = N-cm), and ISQ.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Search strategy

Two authors (PS and SS) independently searched the data in the following databases: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and EMBASE including all studies published from 2001 to 2020 by framing a search strategy, manual search was also done for potentially relevant literature [].

Protocol registration

The registration number for protocol is CRD42020204907 as per PROSPERO (International prospective register of systematic reviews) database.

Eligibility criteria

Criteria for the inclusion and exclusion are mentioned in.

Table 1

Criteria for inclusion and exclusion

This systematic review was confined to the literature fulfilling following inclusion criteria

Language: Studies published in the English Language

Current developments published during year 2000–2020

Preclinical animal investigation

Number and location of the implants defined

Animal studies having healthy animal models with different implant surface modifications in healed sites presenting % BIC and results from biomechanical tests

Presence of a control group-Machined (turned or smooth surface)

Exclusion criteria

Other study designs (e.g., clinical investigations,studies, cell culture models, conference papers, case reports, reviews and studies in systemically compromised and disease induced animal model such as diabetes and osteoporosis), studies in compromised host bone intentional creation of peri-implantitis, nicotine, smoking, defects with application with GFR, loaded implants, mini-implants with diameter <2.5 mm, and investigations with an unclear outcome were excluded

Data extraction

Titles and abstracts of studies were initially screened by two authors (PS and SS) and checked for agreement to exclude the irrelevant articles and duplicates. This was independently verified by the 3author (GSC). Data were extracted using the standardized evaluation forms. A detailed description of the literature search has been depicted in the PRISMA Flowchart [].

The data extracted are:

Name of author and its publication year;

Type of animal model used and their number;

Location of implant;

Number of implants placed;

Type of surface modification of implant;

Follow-up time (in weeks);

Outcome assessed (BIC %, RTV = Ncm, ISQ value).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed by using Metapackage tool in R programming which provide standard statistical programming methods for meta-analysis supported by R is an open source Implementation of S programming language, developed by R core Team at the University of Auckland, New Zealand. Forest plot was used to determine the summary effect for the included studies as sum of mean difference standardized mean differences (SMD) of the studies at 95% confidence interval for each of the assessed parameters for different groups of implant surfaces. A subgroup analysis was carried out at different follow-up periods (<4 weeks, 48 weeks, and more than 8 weeks). Funnel plot was drawn to evaluate the publication bias in the selected studies using Begg’s test, which showed minimal asymmetry suggesting that there was no publication bias in our analysis. For analysing the heterogeneity, a random-effects model was used. Q statistic and itsvalue were used to test whether effect sizes departed from homogeneity. Tauwas used to quantify the amount of heterogeneity and Iexpressed the proportion of dispersion due to heterogeneity.[]

RESULTS

Study strategy

A total of 2720 articles were found by the electronic search of the databases and by the manual search. After the removal of duplicates, 2251 articles were screened for their titles and abstract. The number of articles was later curtailed to 409 for full text review. After overall assessment, 37 articles were included for qualitative synthesis and 20 studies for quantitative synthesis based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria

Description of the studies

Details of the included studies are tabulated inand. Included studies measured the effect on BIC percentage (BIC%) around implants, RTV, ISQ on implants with machined surface and implants with modified surfaces either additive or subtractive were contemplated at different follow up periods ranging between 1 week and 12 weeks. Altogether all included articles reported placement of 1891 implants in 513 animals. Rodents (Guinea pigs) and rabbits were preferably used for the experiments. Dogs were the second most common animal used. Other animals such as pigs, foxhound, and sheep were also used in the experiment. In addition, the sites of implant placement such as intraoral site (maxilla, mandible, or both) and extraoral sites (femur, tibia, humerus, and illiac crest) were also considered.

Implant surface characteristics

Titanium-machined implants were evaluated in all 37 studies and analyzed as control group, whereas implant with modified surfaces was considered as the test group. Most of included studies compared more than one implant surface treatment with control group.

Several methods have been used to enhance the implant surface roughness to increase surface area thereby, improving the process of osseointegration. Out of 37 included studies, 7 studies assessed sandblasted implant surfaces.[] Six studies evaluated acid etching.[] 15 studies considered sandblasted and acid-etched (SLA) implant surfaces.[] 8 studies tested anodized surfaces.[] Eight studies evaluated laser-induced alteration.[]

For the additive procedures, like coating with different materials, out of 37 included studies, 4 studies evaluated HA-coated implant surfaces.[] Faeda, 2012 implanted HA coated and turned implants into the tibia of rabbit and observed BIC% and bone area after 4,8,12 weeks. They reported greater BIC% with HA-coated implants (73%) compared to turned implants (46.3%).[] Another study by Faeda, 2009 reported that implants coated with HA exhibited greater removal torque force (64 Ncm) than machined implants (33.8 N-cm) after 3 months healing period in the tibia of rabbits.[] London, 2002 suggested no advantage in BIC% of HA-coated surface (22.5%) over machined titanium surface (31.0%).[] A study by Zechner, 2003 concluded that HA-coated surfaces had higher BIC% (39%) as compared to machined surface implants (19.3%).[]

Two studies evaluated calcium phosphate (CaP)-coated surface in which Ahn, 2009 suggested lower RT values for CaP coated over machined surface implants and Koh, 2009 showed no significant differences in ISQ values between 2 and 4 weeks after implant insertion. Ca-P immersed implants showed higher RTVs at 2 and 4 weeks during healing than the machined implants.[]

Two studies evaluated collagen surface coating, Schliephake, 2005 evaluated that all organic coatings had significantly higher BIC% than machined surface. Schliephake, 2006 suggested that there was no correlation between BIC and RFA values for different collagen coating at various time intervals.[] One study evaluated titanium plasma spray (TPS) coating by London, 2002 who concluded that there was no advantage in BIC% to TPS surface (24%) over machined titanium surface (31.0%).[] Thiem, evaluated different plasma treated surface coating and revealed that BIC values were significantly increased in O2 plasma spray coating (r-NB-C) group in comparison to other groups.[] Offermans, 2018 evaluated the effect of strontium functionalized titanium coating implant (Ti-Sr-O) on dental implants which accelerated bone apposition and revealed significant differences following implantation periods.[] Strnad, 2008 investigated that alkali treated surfaces enhance the stability in the early stages of healing as compared to machined surface.[] Sul, 2002 showed significant increase in mean RTV and BIC% to calcium-coating implants over machined implants.[] De Maeztu, 2008 suggested statistically significant higher BIC% in implants treated with carbon oxygen ion implantation (CO ion) and diamond-like carbon (DLC) coating over the machined surface in the dog mandible model.[] In a study by Sollazzo, 2007 evaluated Anatase coating in rabbit tibia and showed higher BIC percentage (55.5%) over machined surfaces (31.8%), whereas Yeo, 2008 investigated early bone response and analyzed BIC percentage, ISQ, RT Values at various time intervals, they showed that calcium metaphosphate coating exhibited faster osseointegration and bone healing around dental implants over the turned surface in the rabbit tibia model.[]

Studies were only included in meta-analysis if at least two studies per group were reported.

Outcome assessment

Out of 37, 28 studies measured the percentage of BIC.[] Fourteen studies examined RT values.[] Ten studies analyzed the ISQ.[] Other than these primary outcomes, two studies calculated torque to interface failure.[] Two studies evaluated the bone between threads percentage (BBT%).[] Two studies assessed the bone area percentage (BA%).[] Three studies assessed bone density (BD%).[] 1 study estimated pull-out test.[] Cumulatively, 263 figures were obtained to assess BIC%/RTV/ISQ values for the included implant surface at different follow-up intervals.

All the studies showed comparable outcomes of bone apposition around implants in healthy experimental animal models in terms of BIC%, RTV, and ISQ for both test and control groups.

DISCUSSION

Dental implant surface modification is not a new concept, as it has been well evaluated through various animal and clinical studies. Numerous developments have been accepted with a purpose of optimizing the roughness and topography of endosseous implant surfaces to achieve predictable osseointegration.[]

This review was conducted to assess whether implant surface modifications are capable of increasing peri-implant bone formation or not in comparison to machined surfaces in animal models?

Thirty-seven full-text publications were reviewed as per inclusion criteria, with the follow-up intervals ranging between 1 week-12 weeks following implant placement. Histomorphometric analysis, RTV, and ISQ were assessed for peri-implant bone apposition. To sum up, current meta-analysis postulated that all implant surface modifications substantially increased bone apposition.

Numerous diverse animal species were utilized in all 37 studies, indicating dissimilar dynamics of bone formation during the early period of healing.[] These well-known factors had a great impact on observed bone apposition values. Out of all included studies, 20 studies evaluated osseointegration in rabbits, 4 in pigs (included mini pigs), 9 in dogs, 2 in foxhound and 2 in sheep. A low/unclear risk of bias was established using the SYRCLE ROB tool for nearly all the studies. The prime assumption for unclear ROB was under-reporting of particular facts in relation to design of study and research methodology, which made it challenging to draw substantial conclusions. The quality of studies was determined using the ARRIVE guidelines, almost every criteria was applied on majority of the studies, though certain criteria such as randomization at the animal level and implant level were difficult to assess. Consideration of a particular animal model to human circumstances is of utmost importance for the clinical translation of an experiment, 95% of studies discussed this issue. Almost every methodological criteria was skilfully adhered to, randomization was executed in 35% at animal level and 38% at implant level in all included studies. Notably, the only criteria in which only four studies reported well were so-called “3R’s criteria (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement), wherein the focus was on a reduction in the number of animals used in research but sometimes it was not feasible due to sample size restrictions for statistical analysis.[] With this “3Rs” criteria, the aim was to develop new approaches for reduction in number of animals, like use of multiple sites in an isolated animal or using newer concepts without sacrificing the animal.

All 37 articles qualified for the analysis in terms of outcome of interest such as BIC%, RTV and ISQ, a positive outcome was attained for subtractive and additive procedures over machined implant surfaces when data of all animal species were pooled together. Considering the different groups of surface modifications, pooled result of SLA, laser modified, acid etched and anodized surface exhibited substantially higher BIC% over machined surface (< 0.01). BIC% as a measure of osseointegration in all surface procedures ranged from 20% to 80% at various follow up periods. Regarding SLA surface, result was statistically significant (< 0.01) at <4 weeks, 4-8 weeks but after follow-up of >12 weeks, no statistically significant difference was found. In case of laser modified surfaces, result was statistically significant for <4 weeks and >8 weeks (< 0.01) and nonsignificant result was found in 4-8 weeks of follow-up period. While evaluating acid etched and anodized surface, statistically significant difference was found only during 4-8 weeks’ follow-up.

While analyzing RTV, only laser-modified implant surfaces exhibited significant RT values over machined surfaces and subgroup analysis suggested statistically significant result at 4-8 weeks interval better than 8 weeks’ follow-up period. No statistically significant result was observed between sandblasted, acid etched, SLA, and anodized surfaces over the machined surface. Regarding ISQ, no significant differences between any of the surface modification and machined implant surfaces were observed.

Only descriptive analysis was possible for additive procedures and these studies were not included in the meta-analysis due of large heterogeneity of the variables and lack of availability of two studies per group. The finding of the current review also specified a significant higher BIC percentage, RTV and ISQ values with additive procedures when compared to the machined surfaces. The present study was somewhat in accordance with the systematic review carried out by Meng, 2016.[]

Limitations included the lack of homogeneity in observed outcomes due to variation in animal species used and the location of the implant at different sites.[] Hence, the above-mentioned outcomes might have been impacted thus affecting the osseointegration process. Implant installation in extraoral sites showed higher percentage of BIC for machined surface implants, whereas a decreased values were reported for intraoral sites for machined surfaces due to large heterogeneity observed in the dynamics of bone formation.[] A large variation was observed while assessing implant healing timespan which ranged between 1 week and 6 months. Although few studies reported a single time frame, while other studies assessed various time intervals.

Moreover, data also presented that studies with nonstandardized protocols, missing standard deviation characters in the existing comprehended literature, lack of randomization, and differences in criteria for assessing outcomes resulted in bias. The correlation between outcomes was not possible due to the large variation in animal models, nonuniform protocols, and time of healing. Above described contributing factors made the inter-study correlation more troublesome and further complicated the translation of results.

Although authors of included studies demonstrated a trend toward a better BIC for surface modified implants, accurate, consistent, and necessary attempts are required for the standardized conduction of future animal trials.

CONCLUSIONS

Animal models and enforcement of rough surface implants have shown amazing impact on the osseointegration process. Analysis of 37 animal studies found a higher degree of osseointegration in relation to BIC interface, RTV, and ISQ with surface modified implant surfaces, as they seem to promote bone formation around peri-implant tissue during the early stages of healing. It would be inspiring to peep into more advanced research work on healthy animal models exhibiting an impact of newer surface modifications on bone apposition around dental implants.

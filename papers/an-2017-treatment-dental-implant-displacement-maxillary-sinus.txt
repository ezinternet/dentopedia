Treatment of dental implant displacement into the maxillary sinus
An JH, Park SH, Han JJ, Jung S, Kook MS, Park HJ, Oh HK
Maxillofacial Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery 2017;39(1):35
DOI: 10.1186/s40902-017-0133-1  PMID: 29204419  PMCID: PMC5701899
Chonnam National University, School of Dentistry, Gwangju, Korea
Source: PubMed Central full text (JATS), retrieved via PubMed MCP.

=== ABSTRACT ===
Background: Displacement of dental implants into the maxillary sinus is rare, but it primarily
occurs in patients with severe pneumatization of the maxillary sinus and/or deficiency of the
alveolar process. Complications such as infection of the paranasal sinuses and oroantral fistula can
follow. The displaced implant has to be removed immediately with surgical intervention and another
rehabilitation plan considered.
Main body: The conventional procedure for removal of a displaced implant from the maxillary sinus
involves sinus bone grafting and new implant placement performed in two or more steps with a
significant time gap. Simplification of these surgical procedures can decrease treatment duration and
patient discomfort.
Conclusions: This review discusses the anatomical characteristics of the maxillary sinus and the
complications associated with implant displacement into the sinus.

=== INTRODUCTION / ETIOLOGY ===
Severe maxillary sinus pneumatization + thin residual alveolar bone can cause displacement of
implants into the sinus during placement or after prosthetic restoration. Biting forces can also
displace. Risk higher with simultaneous implant + bone graft after sinus elevation, and with
residual bone height <4 mm plus simultaneous grafting. Galindo: air-pressure differences between
sinus and nasal cavity + autoimmune reaction (peri-implantitis bone resorption) also contribute.
Other factors: lack of primary stability, surgeon inexperience, temporary denture without relief,
overdrilling, inappropriate force during removal of non-integrated implants.
Immediate removal of displaced implants is recommended. When delayed, control sinus infection with
antibiotics + nasal decongestants before removal via Caldwell-Luc approach or endoscopic technique.
After removal, staged implant placement + bone grafting can follow, but the conventional staged
approach is long and delays rehabilitation.

=== ANATOMY OF THE MAXILLARY SINUS ===
Pyramid-shaped cavity lined with ciliated mucoperiosteum (Schneiderian membrane ~1.0 mm thick).
Connected to nasal cavity via the maxillary ostium (relatively far from the sinus floor). Posterior
superior alveolar nerve/vessels pass through; average nerve-to-crestal-ridge distance 16.9 mm.
Adult sinus: 25-35 mm wide, 36-45 mm high, 38-45 mm long. After tooth loss the sinus pneumatizes
(osteoclastic basal bone loss) → membrane perforation risk. Septa incidence 31.7% in premolar region
is another perforation contributor.

=== COMPLICATIONS OF DISPLACEMENT ===
May be intra- or postoperative. Displaced implant disturbs sinus anatomy, inhibits mucociliary
clearance; mucosal thickening + scattered graft can obstruct the ostium → maxillary sinusitis and
congestion. Ostium blockage → oroantral fistula. Implants can migrate further (paranasal sinuses,
orbital floor, cranial fossa) via mucociliary clearance, air-pressure change, foreign-body reaction,
local tissue necrosis. Some report displaced implants may NOT cause sinusitis (Galindo-Moreno: 2
antral migration cases, one left in situ on patient request, no complications at 4 yr). Others
(Regev, Raghoebar) argue displaced implants cause chronic sinusitis via foreign-body reaction and
must be removed even if asymptomatic.

=== TREATMENT MODALITIES ===
Basic principle: immediate removal. When delayed, control infection first: amoxicillin-clavulanate +
NSAID + pseudoephedrine hydrochloride for 1 week before surgery.
- Historically: Caldwell-Luc procedure; conservative observation if asymptomatic.
- Now preferred: minimally invasive — functional endoscopic sinus surgery (FESS); conservative
  intraoral surgery with a bony window in the lateral maxillary wall.
- Tsodoulos: general anesthesia, small rectangular lateral bony window under direct vision; after
  removal the bony window repositioned and stabilized with absorbable sutures.
- Nogami: local anesthesia, four holes + piezoelectric osteotomy; displaced implant identified with
  rigid endoscope, removed by dental suction; bony fragment repositioned, absorbable sutures.
- Subsequently, shorter/wider implants (by 1-2 mm) can be placed with/without sinus grafting after
  CBCT confirmation of residual bone height/width 4-6 months later.
Most reported studies split removal, grafting, and new placement into 2-3 procedures. Delayed
placement indicated when primary stability unattainable, extensive membrane perforation, or severe
infection — but staging delays rehabilitation.

=== SIMPLIFIED SURGICAL STRATEGY (extended bony window, simultaneous) — case ===
63-year-old man, left-cheek pain, referred for removal of an implant displaced 3 years earlier.
Panoramic + CBCT: implant in left maxillary sinus with mucosal thickening (sinusitis). Under local
anesthesia: full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap; lateral wall exposed; bony window ditched with round
bur, made VERTICALLY WIDER than a usual sinus-elevation window — UPPER portion for implant removal,
LOWER portion for sinus membrane lifting. After exposing the membrane and removing the bony window, a
horizontal incision at the upper portion; implant removed via dental suction through this opening.
The membrane is then lifted from the lower edge; perforated membrane covered with absorbable collagen
membrane; a new implant placed simultaneously with sinus bone grafting (maxillary tuberosity autograft
+ allograft mixture). Postop: avoid nose-blowing 2 weeks, sneeze mouth-open, routine meds 1 week. CBCT
at 4 months: no haziness. Second-stage surgery at 6 months; prosthetic restoration 2 months later; no
complications through 14-month follow-up.

=== CONCLUSIONS ===
Displaced implants should be removed immediately. When immediate removal is not possible (patient
condition or clinician inexperience), control sinusitis first (antibiotics + decongestants), then
perform implant removal + simultaneous new implant placement with sinus bone grafting through an
extended bony window. This single-stage approach reduces effort, time, and patient discomfort and
accelerates rehabilitation.

Title: Advances in 3D-printed scaffold technologies for bone defect repair: materials, biomechanics, and clinical prospects
Authors: Sun J, Chen C, Zhang B, et al.
Journal: Biomedical Engineering Online (Biomed Eng Online), 2025
DOI: 10.1186/s12938-025-01381-w | PMID: 40301861 | PMCID: PMC12042599
Source: PubMed Central full text, retrieved via PubMed MCP get_full_text_article

ABSTRACT

The treatment of large bone defects remains a significant clinical challenge due to the limitations of current grafting techniques, including donor site morbidity, restricted availability, and suboptimal integration. Recent advances in 3D bioprinting technology have enabled the fabrication of structurally and functionally optimized scaffolds that closely mimic native bone tissue architecture. This review comprehensively examines the latest developments in 3D-printed scaffolds for bone regeneration, focusing on three critical aspects: (1) material selection and composite design encompassing metallic; (2) structural optimization with hierarchical porosity (macro/micro/nano-scale) and biomechanical properties tailored; (3) biological functionalization through growth factor delivery, cell seeding strategies and surface modifications. We critically analyze scaffold performance metrics from different research applications, while discussing current translational barriers, including vascular network establishment, mechanical stability under load-bearing conditions, and manufacturing scalability. The review concludes with a forward-looking perspective on innovative approaches such as 4D dynamic scaffolds, smart biomaterials with stimuli-responsive properties, and the integration of artificial intelligence for patient-specific design optimization. These technological advancements collectively offer unprecedented opportunities to address unmet clinical needs in complex bone reconstruction.

Introduction

Bone possesses remarkable regenerative capabilities; however, under certain pathological conditions, regeneration may be compromised. Bone defects, a prevalent clinical orthopedic issue, present significant treatment challenges. Trauma, infection, and pathological fractures are common causes of critical bone defects. Currently, bone transplantation is the predominant treatment and ranks as one of the most widely performed types of tissue transplantation globally. Autografts, derived from the same individual, exhibit superior histocompatibility, osteoconduction, and osteoinduction, essential for bone regeneration. These grafts can rapidly and completely integrate with the defect site and exhibit a low probability of immune rejection, making them a preferred method for treating bone defects.

Despite their advantages, the availability of natural bone grafts does not meet the demand, and this shortage leads to challenges such as prolonged surgical times and a high incidence of donor sites. Allograft transplantation, another common approach, offers a relatively broader source availability and is not constrained by shape and size. Although allografts address some limitations of autografts, their osteoinductive properties can be diminished due to cryopreservation. Moreover, allografts often display inadequate regenerative capabilities and are associated with various challenges, including infection and immune response issues. In treating bone defects, the demand for innovative grafts that can replace traditional methods and foster new treatment approaches is growing. Recent advances in bone tissue engineering have seen the flourishing of bone tissue scaffolds, particularly those created using 3D printing technology.

With their exogenous structures, scaffolds are designed to enhance cell viability and proliferation, forming desired tissue architectures. Recent developments in scaffold manufacturing techniques include phase separation, freeze-drying, gas foaming, and electrospinning. However, these methods generally provide only an approximate control over scaffold pore size and cannot precisely adjust parameters such as porosity, thus failing to accurately emulate physiological bone structure and meet clinical needs. In response, 3D printing technology has emerged, offering unprecedented precision in structure control and design compared to earlier scaffold technologies. This method manufactures three-dimensional, multi-layered structures that allow precise control over factors such as pore morphology, closely mimicking physiological bone tissue. Currently, it has shown promising preliminary clinical outcomes, particularly in dentistry — 3D-printed materials used for temporary restorations can provide sufficient mechanical properties for intraoral applications in crown and bridge restorations.

This review explores scaffold material selection and composite design (metallic, polymer, bioceramic), structural optimization with hierarchical porosity (macro/micro/nano-scale) and tailored biomechanical properties, and biological functionalization through growth factor delivery, cell seeding strategies, and surface modifications, while discussing translational barriers including vascular network establishment, mechanical stability under load-bearing conditions, and manufacturing scalability, concluding with 4D dynamic scaffolds, stimuli-responsive smart biomaterials, and AI-driven patient-specific design optimization.

Overview of scaffold properties

Implant heterogeneity (biocompatibility): Biocompatibility is the ability of materials to elicit appropriate host responses in specific applications, crucial to prevent adverse immune reactions during cell adhesion, migration, and proliferation. Natural materials (e.g., collagen, elastin) theoretically exhibit superior biocompatibility versus synthetic polymers; incorporating natural materials into composites enhances biocompatibility of synthetics. Mainstream synthetic materials such as PCL and beta-TCP are recognized for excellent biocompatibility, enhancing cell growth and osteogenic marker expression.

Structural characteristics: Biomimetic characterization is paramount — biomaterials with high porosity, interconnected pores, and specific surface modifications emulate the extracellular matrix (ECM), providing a bone-like microenvironment for vascularization, stem cell recruitment, and cellular behavior regulation. Compact bone porosity ranges 5-30%; cancellous bone 50-90%. Multi-level (macro+micro) pore structures improve bone/cell growth over single-level structures by increasing surface area for cell adhesion and improving nutrient/oxygen supply via interconnected pores. Pore size ~100 micrometers favors cell ingrowth/osseointegration; pore size >300 micrometers better supports bone growth/angiogenesis; fibrocartilage needs 200-300 micrometers. No consensus exists on ideal pore size/porosity. Larger pores/porosity enhance cell ingrowth/osteogenesis but compromise mechanical strength and extend osseointegration time — this is a central design tension.

Mechanical strength: Key properties are Young's modulus, compressive strength, tensile strength. Ideal scaffold should approximate cortical bone: Young's modulus 7-30 GPa (long axis), compressive strength ~50-200 MPa, tensile strength ~150 MPa.

Biodegradability: Degradation rate must align with bone regeneration rate. Example: Miao et al. tuned degradation using strontium-doped calcium sulfate (SrCSH, fast-degrading) combined with strontium-doped tricalcium phosphate (Sr-TCP, slow-degrading), adjustable via porosity/structure. Common biodegradable polymers: PCL, PLA, PLGA. Degradation products must be non-toxic; adequate porosity facilitates removal of degradation products/metabolic waste.

Swelling ratio: Influenced by hydrophilicity/permeability. Injectable hydrogel scaffolds for small defects need higher swelling; scaffolds for large segmental defects needing long-term mechanical support need lower swelling (structural stability). Hydration-mediated expansion can accelerate degradation (e.g., water ingress disrupts polymer chain interactions).

Angiogenic capacity: Large porosity enables endothelial cell ingrowth/blood vessel formation; failure of many bone graft scaffolds attributed to inadequate vascularization. HIF-1alpha and VEGF are key angiogenesis regulators; sustained VEGF release from scaffolds promotes angiogenesis and (directly) osteogenesis. Example: magnesium-coated Ti6Al4V scaffolds enhanced HIF-1alpha/VEGF expression, improving endothelial cell proliferation/migration. Synergy between osteogenic factors (BMP-2) and angiogenic factors is emphasized. Novel approaches: PLLA/MnO2 nanoparticle microenvironment-responsive scaffold scavenging reactive oxygen species (Lai et al.); bioinspired double-network hydrogel with tissue-specific dECM + adipose-MSC-derived exosomes for concurrent cartilage/subchondral bone regeneration (Li et al.).

Material selection (mainstream classification)

Materials are categorized into metals, polymers, and bioceramics (further subdivided natural vs synthetic).

Metals: Stainless steel and titanium alloys used >a century in bone replacement for mechanical strength/compatibility. Metal ions promote osteoblastogenesis, inhibit osteoclastogenesis, enhance angiogenesis, provide antibacterial properties. Limitations: non-biodegradable, high elastic modulus causing stress-shielding effect, potential metal ion toxicity, poor tissue adhesion. Titanium alloys (esp. Ti6Al4V) are common in orthopedics/dentistry — good biocompatibility, high strength, corrosion resistance, relatively lower elastic modulus/stress-shielding vs other metals. Example: bilayer scaffold with 3D-printed titanium subchondral compartment + freeze-dried collagen cartilage compartment (Tao Yang team) accelerated osteochondral formation in rabbit femoral trochlear defect model. Surface coatings (e.g., silicon-substituted hydroxyapatite [SiHA] + VEGF) enhance titanium surface activity/osseointegration — combined SiHA+VEGF significantly boosted regeneration in sheep vs either alone. Titanium fiber plate (Takizawa et al.) achieved 0.2 mm thickness, Young's modulus ~30 GPa (near cortical bone 10-30 GPa), avoiding stress shielding. For infected bone defects: MAO (micro-arc oxidation)-modified 3D-printed porous Ti6Al4V + vancomycin-hydrogel coating achieved effective antibacterial + osteointegration outcomes in infected rabbit bone defects (Teng Zhang et al.). Magnesium alloys/ions: elasticity modulus closer to bone, reduces stress shielding, promotes bone metabolism (osteoblast/osteoclast function) but corrodes rapidly in physiological solution, risking premature loss of mechanical support. Example: Mg-ion-crosslinked double network hydrogel via Mg-S coordinate bonds (Xintao Zhang et al.) enhanced mechanical strength/cell adhesion. Tantalum: high strength, low stiffness, high porosity, self-passivating oxide layer promoting bone-to-bone interface; elastic modulus ~2-8 GPa. Example: selective-laser-melted porous tantalum with micro-gradient nanostructure surface (Zhiyi Zhang et al.) enhanced early bone integration in rabbit femur model.

Polymers: Natural biopolymers (collagen, gelatin, albumin, cellulose, hyaluronic acid, chitosan, alginate) closely match natural ECM, generally low immunogenicity. Type I collagen most widely used (bone); type II from cartilage. Pure collagen scaffolds lack mechanical strength; crosslinking improves mechanics but may impair cellular response. Example: high-strength mineralized collagen scaffold (calcium/phosphate ions + type I collagen + PCL) for large calvarial defects in sheep (Li et al.) — dense, small pore size compensating mechanical deficiency of collagen. Synthetic polymers (PCL, PLA, PLGA — aliphatic polyesters) offer broader sourcing, higher strength, adaptable functionalization, slower degradation, strong structural plasticity vs natural polymers. PCL: semi-crystalline, excellent toughness/biocompatibility, non-toxic degradation, but slow degradation and insufficient mechanical properties alone; hydrophobic, lacks cell-recognition sites, requiring surface modification (e.g., apatite/hydroxyapatite deposition). Example: PCL + GelMA-loaded dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) hybrid scaffold (Buyuksungur et al.) improved mechanical strength, cell activity, osteogenic differentiation, mineralization. Example: PCL-PEG-PCL + zinc oxide nanomineral (n-BPC) bioactive scaffold (Hou et al.) with interconnected large pores, improved water absorption/degradation/mechanical strength via PEG. Example: 3D-printed PCL/beta-TCP scaffold coated with polydopamine (PDA) + alginate microspheres for sustained BMP-2 delivery improved cortical bone connectivity/induction in rabbit femoral segmental defect model.

Bioceramics: Dominate the bone scaffold market — high strength, excellent biocompatibility, significant bioactivity, robust osteoinduction/conduction, can load stem cells/growth factors/drugs. Limitations: insufficient degradability, mechanical strength, high brittleness (sintered powders). Classified as resorbable calcium phosphates (hydroxyapatite [HA], tricalcium phosphate [TCP], biphasic calcium phosphate [BCP]), bioactive types (bioactive glass), near-inert types (zirconia). HA/TCP show promise regenerating alveolar bone (dental applications). HA is highly biocompatible/non-toxic, excels in osteoconductivity/osteoinductivity, but slow degradation and relatively weak mechanical properties limit use as sole load-bearing material — requires composites. Example: chitosan + L-arginine + hydroxyapatite nanocrystal composite reinforced with genipin (K. Zafeiris et al.). Bioactive glass: calcium/phosphorus/silicon-rich, historic 1969 origin, promotes osteoblast proliferation, minimal adverse tissue effects, antibacterial via silicon ion release. Example: sponge-replication bioactive glass scaffolds (pore ranges 1-1.5 mm and 170-700 micrometers) coated with silver nanoparticles for antibacterial effect (Rodrigo L.M.S. Oliveira et al.). Bioactive glass is brittle with limited degradation, less ideal for load-bearing; combining with PLGA (porous, acidic degradation neutralizing bioactive glass's alkaline degradation products) accelerated degradation/bone healing in rat cranial defect model (Angela Maria Paiva Magri et al.). Example: borosilicate bioactive glass (BSG) + Fe3O4 (iron tetroxide) scaffold enhanced antibacterial efficacy and bone repair, promoting M2 macrophage polarization and osteogenic factor (RUNX2, ALP, OCN) expression (Ying Jin et al.).

Special processing methods

Surface-modified coatings compensate for material deficiencies (e.g., typical metals suffer high stress-shielding/low tissue compatibility). Hydroxyapatite coating on metals enhances metal-bone integration, reduces corrosion/metal ion toxicity. Example: sol-gel HA-coated metal mesh (Armaghan Naderi et al.) for cranioplasty — matched elastic modulus to human skull, supported adipose-derived stem cell (ASC) activity, improved corrosion resistance. Growth factor incorporation (BMP most widely used, among other cytokines) into scaffolds is a key modification strategy to stimulate bone repair.

Bioprinting

Three main bioprinting methods: inkjet-based (including binder jetting — low equipment cost, fast, but low precision; and selective laser melting [SLM] — high-energy laser melting powders in vacuum, high dimensional accuracy/design freedom for complex shapes, but high complexity/cost/slow speed, potential laser damage to cells and photoinitiator toxicity), and extrusion-based (fused deposition modeling [FDM] — continuous extrusion via syringe/micro-nozzle, high sedimentation/printing speed, no heating required, cell-loading-friendly, versatile bioink options, but limited shapes/regular structures and limited printing resolution).

Bioink/scaffold-material compatibility is critical: hydrogels are common (high water content, biocompatibility, mimic ECM) but often have poor mechanical properties/low printability — addressed via dual-crosslinking (physical+chemical) to improve mechanical stability/printability. Bioinks require shear-thinning behavior for smooth extrusion; photo-crosslinkable bioinks enable precise gelation control for complex high-fidelity structures. Ceramic-containing bioinks offer high mechanical strength/osteoconductivity for bone tissue engineering but pose extrusion challenges due to high viscosity, requiring novel rheology modifiers/crosslinking strategies.

Clinical applications, challenges, and prospects

3D printing combined with CT/medical imaging enables patient-specific implants. Examples: 3D-printed artificial vertebral body for multi-segment spinal resection (Hu et al.) with good outcomes; porous Ti6Al4V scaffold via 3D printing repairing lower-extremity defects without additional bone grafting (Liu et al.); porous tantalum bone plate for tibial fracture nonunion, improving therapeutic outcomes given poor tibial blood supply (Zhao et al.).

Key translational challenges and strategies: (1) Print-detail control — large tolerance compensation for low-precision regions vs high-precision compensation only where needed, to save time/material costs. (2) Comparison with other bone-repair strategies (stem cell therapy, gene therapy) is useful context alongside 3D printing. (3) 4D printing — dynamic, stimuli-responsive (light/temperature) scaffolds that change structure/mechanical properties over time to match the phases of bone healing (high mechanical strength/low porosity during load-bearing phases; higher porosity/altered architecture during vascularization phases); shape-memory materials can improve fit to irregular defect geometries. Example: hollow self-folding hydrogel tube via 4D printing with precise dynamic mechanical control (Alina Kirillova et al.).

Conclusion

Bone defects, especially large critical-sized defects, remain a formidable orthopedic/reconstructive challenge; autografts remain the clinical gold standard but their limitations have driven innovation via 3D printing. This review highlights the transformative potential of 3D-printed scaffolds across material innovation (osteoconductive bioceramics, mechanically resilient titanium alloys, bioactive polymers), structural precision (tunable porosity/stiffness/degradation via additive manufacturing to mimic native bone architecture), and biological functionalization (growth factor delivery, stem cell integration, immunomodulatory coatings). Persistent challenges include rapid vascularization, infection prevention, and seamless osseointegration. Future progress depends on merging 4D-printed dynamic scaffolds, AI-driven design optimization, and advanced bioreactor conditioning with clinical insights, moving 3D-printed scaffolds from structural replacement toward active tissue regeneration.

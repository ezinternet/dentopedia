Title: Bone Grafts and Substitutes in Dentistry: A Review of Current Trends and Developments
Authors: Zhao R, Yang R, Cooper PR, et al.
Journal: Molecules, 2021
PMID: 34070157
PMCID: PMC8158510
DOI: 10.3390/molecules26103007

ABSTRACT

After tooth loss, bone resorption is irreversible, leaving the area without adequate bone volume for successful implant treatment. Bone grafting is the only solution to reverse dental bone loss and is a well-accepted procedure required in one in every four dental implants. Research and development in materials, design and fabrication technologies have expanded over the years to achieve successful and long-lasting dental implants for tooth substitution. This review will critically present the various dental bone graft and substitute materials that have been used to achieve a successful dental implant. The article also reviews the properties of dental bone grafts and various dental bone substitutes that have been studied or are currently available commercially. The various classifications of bone grafts and substitutes, including natural and synthetic materials, are critically presented, and available commercial products in each category are discussed. Different bone substitute materials, including metals, ceramics, polymers, or their combinations, and their chemical, physical, and biocompatibility properties are explored. Limitations of the available materials are presented, and areas which require further research and development are highlighted. Tissue engineering hybrid constructions with enhanced bone regeneration ability, such as cell-based or growth factor-based bone substitutes, are discussed as an emerging area of development.

1. Introduction

A bone graft is defined as a living tissue capable of promoting bone healing, transplanted into a bony defect, either alone or in combination with other materials. A bone substitute is a natural or synthetic material, often containing only a mineralized bone matrix with no viable cells, that is able to achieve the same purpose. Bone grafts and substitutes have been used in the medical field for centuries, with the first recorded use of bone grafts in 1682, where a cranial defect was successfully restored using a cranial bone graft from a deceased dog. According to the US Food and Drug Administration (USFDA), bone grafts are classified as Class II devices (bone grafts filling the bony voids and defects) and Class III devices (bone graft containing drugs). The use of bone grafts and substitutes in dentistry have markedly increased in recent years due to advancements in dental implantology and the growing need for repair of craniofacial bony defects. These bony or skeletal defects may arise from trauma, periodontal disease, surgical excision, cranioplasty, infection or congenital malformations, and oral cancer. The most common observation of insufficient quantity of bone in dentistry is following tooth loss, where rapid resorption of alveolar bone occurs due to an absence of intraosseous stimulation that would typically occur via the periodontal ligament fibers. Successful placement of dental implants requires sufficient alveolar bone dimensions, that is, at least 10 mm in height and 3 mm to 4 mm in diameter. It has been estimated that up to 50% of all dental implant procedures currently performed will involve the use of bone grafts. Globally, current statistics indicate that approximately 2.2 million bone graft procedures, costing an estimated US$664 million by 2021, are being performed each year, with the number of operative procedures for repairing bony defects estimated to grow by approximately 13% annually. As of 2018, the market value for dental bone substitutes has been estimated to be worth approximately US$493 million and is projected to grow to approximately US$931 million by 2025, at a combined annual growth rate of 9.5%. Despite this widespread use of bone graft and substitute materials globally, there are still limitations that remain associated with currently used materials. These primarily involve the use of allografts, the transfer of grafting materials between two genetically unrelated subjects; and autografts, the transfer of grafting material from one body site to another within the same subject. None of the products in the market currently possesses all the ideal properties for a bone substitute material including low patient morbidity, ease of handling, low immunogenicity, low cost and angiogenic potential. The disadvantages of autografts include the lack of availability of graft tissue, associated pain, morbidity at the donor site and the need for two operative procedures; whereas disadvantages of allografts include rejection of the donor tissue by the recipient's immune system and concerns with transmission of diseases, such as HIV and hepatitis. In recent years, there has been an increased drive in the market to use newer bone grafting materials, such as bone substitute products, despite little evidence-based research for indications and safety. Thus, these matters of concern, along with continued marked increases in demand for bone graft materials and the global ageing population strongly indicate a need for further research into the development of novel materials used for bone grafting procedures.

This literature review reports on the dental bone graft and substitute materials that are currently available commercially, their limitations, and the potential development of promising alternatives brought about by the emergence of synthetic bone substitutes in recent decades.

2. Characteristics of an Ideal Bone Grafting Material

The main function of bone grafts is to provide mechanical support and stimulate osteo-regeneration, with the ultimate goal of bone replacement. The four fundamental biological properties of osseointegration, osteogenesis, osteoconduction, and osteoinduction, are paramount in performing this role effectively. Osseointegration is the ability of a grafting material to chemically bond to the surface of the bone in the absence of an intervening fibrous tissue layer. Osteogenesis refers to the formation of new bone via osteoblasts or progenitor cells present within the grafting material, and osteoconduction refers to the ability of a bone grafting material to generate a bioactive scaffold on which host cells can grow. Osteoinduction is the recruitment of host stem cells into the grafting site, where local proteins and other factors (PDGFs, FGFs, TGF-beta) induce the differentiation of stem cells into osteoblasts. Other important properties include biocompatibility, bioresorbability, sterility, structural integrity, adequate porosity for vascular ingrowth, plasticity, ease of handling, cost, and compressive strength. Almost all current bone graft and substitute materials primarily serve as a structural framework for osteo-regenerative processes to occur, thus they only satisfy the osteoconductivity component of the ideal characteristics.

3. Classification of Dental Bone Graft and Substitute Materials

3.1. Natural Bone Graft and Substitute Materials
Subdivided into autografts, allografts (including demineralized bone matrix, DBM), xenografts, and phytogenic materials. Up to 90% of all bone grafting procedures worldwide use a natural bone graft or substitute material.

3.1.1. Autografts
Commonly obtained from mandibular symphysis, mandibular ramus, external oblique ridge, iliac crest, proximal ulna, or distal radius. No histocompatibility/immunogenicity issues — highest biological safety — but require a secondary surgical site, donor site injury, higher costs and surgical risks. Cancellous bone has osteogenic potential (osteoblasts/progenitor cells, large trabecular surface); cortical bone provides structural-mechanical integrity via osteoconduction but integrates more slowly. Autografts remain the gold standard as the only material possessing all four fundamental biological properties, and are still routinely used for complex augmentation (e.g., posterior mandibular edentulous reconstruction).

3.1.2. Allografts
Sourced from compatible living donors or cadaveric bone; prepared fresh, frozen, or freeze-dried. Freeze-drying increases shelf life and decreases immunogenicity but decreases osteoinductive potential and structural strength. ~8% prevalence of unknown diseases reported in osteoarthritic femoral heads used for allografts; risk mitigated via sterilization, mechanical debridement, ultrasonic washing, gamma irradiation. Cancellous allografts have poor mechanical strength and can trigger fibrous tissue formation; cortical allografts provide mechanical scaffolding. Used to fill periodontal, maxillary, mandibular defects and to restore alveolar ridge height deficiencies. Declining use due to tissue supply shortage, high long-term failure rates, and increased European regulatory restriction favoring synthetic materials.

Demineralized Bone Matrix (DBM): acid-treated allograft derivative exposing BMP/TGF-beta/FGF-rich matrix, conferring greater osteoinductive capacity than cancellous/cortical allografts, though preparation technique (alcohol/lactic/acetic/nitric acid) reduces osteoinductivity. Available as blocks, particulates, powders, and putty with excipients (glycerol, starch, hyaluronic acid, collagen, saline) for improved handling. Poor mechanical properties — limited to filling defects, usually combined with other allografts, BMPs, or composites. Human DBM putty has restored alveolar bone height/thickness post-extraction, forming mineralized/mature bone at 6 months.

Collagen-based allograft materials (extracellular bone matrix) favor new bone formation via mineral deposition/vascularization/growth factor adhesion but present high adverse immune reaction potential and poor structural integrity — generally poor as a standalone graft, better combined with BMPs or HA carriers.

3.1.3. Xenografts
Most common source: deproteinized bovine bone (e.g., Bio-Oss), treated by stepwise annealing + NaOH to produce porous hydroxyapatite (HA) resembling human bone structurally, supporting osteoconduction and angiogenesis. Extensively used in maxillary sinus lifting and implant procedures. Maxillary sinus grafting with bovine xenograft achieved 39% new bone formation at 6 months (comparable to 40% with autograft), with 31% of xenograft material remaining at the site vs 18% of autograft. A 14-year longitudinal study (Ozkan et al. 2011-type reference / Ewers) reported 95% implant survival in atrophic maxillae after sinus grafting. Bio-Oss shown to provide good long-term survival.

Other bovine-derived products (high-temperature treated, eliminating organic components) exhibit similarly low immunogenicity and osteoconductive properties.

Chitosan: naturally occurring polymer from crustacean exoskeletons (glucosamine/N-acetylglucosamine); stimulates bone regeneration via structural scaffold supporting osteoblastic activity and MSC differentiation. Poor mechanical properties alone — combined with gelatin, calcium phosphates, bioglass. Used as GBR membrane, guided tissue regeneration, implant coating, periodontal regeneration, alveolar bone height restoration.

Silk fibroin (SF): biopolymer from silkworm, used as bone scaffold (sponge/fiber/film/hydrogel). Good biocompatibility, degradability, tissue integration, permeability. Clinical trials (2016) of silk mat membranes after impacted mandibular third-molar extraction showed ~4mm new bone gain at 6 months. Indicated for GBR after extraction, cyst/tumor excision, alveolar bone deficiency for implants. Can be blended with HA and chitosan for improved mechanical strength.

3.1.4. Phytogenic Materials
Gusuibu: traditional Chinese herbal medicine (dried rhizome of a pteridophyte) used for bone fracture/osteoarthritis; possesses osteoinductive properties and increases alkaline phosphatase activity. Combined with collagen carrier, increased new bone formation by 24% vs Gusuibu alone and by 90% vs absorbable collagen sponge alone (Wong and Rabie). Accelerates bone remodeling following orthodontic tooth movement.

Coral-based bone substitutes: composed of calcium carbonate, processed by heat treatment with ammonium phosphate into crystalline HA. Naturally occurring coralline HA is brittle and highly resorbable; used as granules/blocks resembling trabecular bone. Improves vascularization vs non-coralline HA; performs better than freeze-dried allograft for cell attachment. Recent modifications: fluorine/zirconia doping (mechanical strength), strontium ion incorporation (bone formation stimulation, resorption inhibition), VEGF coating (enhanced vascularization/mineralization in alveolar defect animal models). Used in maxillary sinus lifting, periodontal osseous defects, alveolar reconstruction.

Marine-algae-derived HA (e.g., Algipore-type material): naturally occurring HA used clinically since 1988. Good resorbability, large surface area for protein adhesion, low immunogenicity; can act as carrier for growth factors and MSCs. Combined with beta-TCP to decrease resorption time while maintaining volume support. A 14-year longitudinal study by Ewers found 95% implant survival in atrophic maxillae following sinus grafting with this material. Mostly used as a space filler to prevent ridge deformities after extraction.

3.2. Synthetic Bone Substitute Materials
Designed to mimic biological properties of natural bone while avoiding donor-site morbidity/immunogenicity; but currently only display osteointegrative and osteoconductive properties (not osteogenic/osteoinductive). Categories: calcium phosphate ceramics (HA, TCP, bioglass), metals (nickel-titanium), polymers (PMMA, polyglycolides), calcium phosphate cements.

3.2.1. Hydroxyapatite (HA)
Chemical composition resembles inorganic bone component. Synthetic HA lacks trace Na/Mg/K/Sr found in natural HA (e.g., bovine bone) and lacks microporous structure. Delayed resorption due to high Ca/P ratio and crystallinity; relatively low mechanical strength — unsuitable for high load-bearing sites. Insufficient for preserving alveolar ridge height alone for implant placement/sinus lifting/periodontal defect management; generally limited to implant coatings, external fixator pins, low-loading sites. Nano-sized HA shows enhanced biomechanical properties and larger surface-to-volume ratio promoting osteogenic progenitor cell adhesion/proliferation/differentiation, though evidence for widespread nanocrystalline HA use remains insufficient.

3.2.2. Tricalcium Phosphate Ceramics (beta-TCP)
Two crystallographic forms (alpha-TCP, beta-TCP). Faster biodegradation/absorption than HA due to lower Ca/P ratio. Advantages: ease of handling, radiopacity, good osteoconductivity (macroporosity), good resorbability vs bovine bone, low immunogenicity. Poor mechanical strength under compression limits use as standalone graft — suitable as filler in bony defects, marginal periodontal/periapical defect repair, partially resorbable filler in alveolar defects. Bone regenerative potential comparable to freeze-dried bone allograft, deproteinized freeze-dried bone allograft, and autograft (Nakajima et al.).

3.2.3. Biphasic Calcium Phosphate Ceramics (HA + beta-TCP)
Combines beta-TCP resorbability with HA osteoconductive potential — more rapid/higher bone regeneration than HA alone, greater mechanical properties than beta-TCP alone. Resorption/osteoconductivity controllable via HA:beta-TCP ratio. Still lower compressive strength than cortical bone. Indicated in periapical surgery with predictable outcomes and complete alveolar healing over 2 years.

3.2.4. Bioactive Glass (BAG)
Synthetic silicate-based ceramics (SiO2, Na2O, CaO, P2O5; modified with K2O, MgO, B2O3). Upon implantation, silicon ions leach to form surface HA layer promoting osteogenic cell adherence. Good biocompatibility, osteoconductivity, antimicrobial activity, porous structure. Zinc-doped BAG reduces periodontal biofilm formation (Esfahanizadeh et al.); silver-doped BAG shows controlled antibacterial ion release (Lovelace et al.) against periodontal pathogens. Commercial products (PerioGlas, UniGraft-type, 90-710 micron particles) used in periodontal surgery; reductions in pocket depth and clinical attachment gain reported in infrabony defects. Brittle, low mechanical strength/fracture resistance — limited to low-stress environments or combined with other materials. Used for unilateral cleft alveolar bone augmentation, periodontal osseous defects, alveolar preservation after extraction in orthodontic patients.

3.2.5. Calcium Phosphate Cements (CPCs)
Two/three-component systems (aqueous + powder, e.g., alpha-TCP/HA) forming a self-setting paste hardening into HA nanocrystals at room temperature. Advantages: self-setting, shapeable, replicate bone structure, high biocompatibility, osteoconductive. Lacks macroporous structure limiting cell adhesion/fluid exchange/resorbability; incomplete setting reaction risk of inflammatory reaction. Recent strategies: 3D-printed CPC scaffolds, improved injectability (chitosan/gelatin/hyaluronic acid binders), ion doping (silicon, strontium), bioactive glass incorporation, growth factor/stem cell infusion. Lyu et al. found CPC + collagen membrane delayed new alveolar bone formation at extraction sites. Brittle under tensile/shear forces — non-load-bearing sites only; risk of extrusion into surrounding tissue. Used as filler for bony defects, fracture reconstruction, dental implantology.

3.2.6. Calcium Sulfates
Heated gypsum powder forming alphahemihydrate crystalline structure; rehydrated to a self-setting moldable paste. Extensively used as osteoconductive scaffold; also shown to have osteoinductive properties via release of osteoinductive molecules. Advantages: low cost, high availability, high biocompatibility, short setting time, osteoconductivity. Major drawback: rapid resorption exceeding new bone formation rate, causing loss of mechanical properties at defect site; increased inflammation/infection risk — often mixed with antibiotics. Biphasic form with ~33% HA developed to allow hardening in presence of saliva/blood. Used for surgical defects, alveolar ridge height maintenance, furcation defects, bone void filler.

3.2.7. Polymers
Degradable (polylactic acid, polyglycolic acid, poly-epsilon-caprolactone, copolymers — aliphatic polyesters) and non-degradable subtypes. Advantages: customizable forms, low immunogenicity, controllable resorbability/porosity/physiochemical structure. Concerns: acidic degradation products altering local pH, poor osteoconductivity/cell adhesion, restricting dental use. Animal studies show variable results (no complications to occasional inflammation). Modifications (HA/TCP addition) may improve bone regeneration potential. VEGF-loaded silk fibroin coating on polymer scaffold achieved controlled release with enhanced angiogenic properties/osseointegration. 3D-printed polylactic acid biopolymer with 200 micron pore diameters showed improved cell proliferation/differentiation. Commercial polymer-based product (PMMA + polyhydroxylethylmethacrylate + calcium hydroxide) used for periodontal intrabony/furcation defects.

3.2.8. Metals
Metallic ions (Mg, Sr, Zn, Si) play roles in bone maintenance/osteogenesis stimulation. Nickel-titanium materials explored for bone regeneration — good mechanical strength, biocompatibility, corrosion resistance, elastic modulus. Ni-Ti membranes (50-125 micron pore size) promote vascularization/bone healing, acting as physical barrier preventing epithelial/fibroblast migration while allowing osteogenic progenitor cell migration. Drawbacks: second surgical procedure needed, soft tissue dehiscence/membrane exposure risk. Used in alveolar bone reconstruction, stabilizing autograft placement, adjunct grafting, GBR membrane barrier.

Magnesium-based bone substitute (Liu et al.): pure Mg (99.9%) + Mg-30wt%Sr alloy fabricated in high-purity graphite crucible. Combined degradability, mechanical properties, biocompatibility of Mg/Mg-Sr alloys. Improved tensile/compressive strength, biocompatibility, antibacterial properties vs conventional bone grafts (calcium sulfates, HA, TCP) — potential for load-bearing site use.

3.3. Composite Bone Substitute Materials
Combine materials (e.g., bioglass + polymers) to improve mechanical properties via combined osteoconductive properties; often expand autograft utility or act as BMP carriers.

Example: nanocrystalline HA (76% w/w) + silicon dioxide (24% w/w) composite — silicon dioxide mediates bone remodeling via autologous protein surface adhesion; highly porous with high fracture toughness and mechanical strength; rapid host tissue integration. Animal studies show new trabecular bone formation followed by resorption after 8 months. In humans, preserves alveolar bone height at extraction sites; combined with platelet-rich fibrin accelerates bone regeneration after mandibular cyst excision.

Example: biphasic alloplastic composite of beta-TCP within calcium sulphate matrix — forms self-setting workable paste; negative surface charge attracts positively charged host BMPs/interstitial fluid, recruiting osteoblasts; sets to form a barrier membrane preventing unwanted cell infiltration while allowing osteogenic cell migration. Used in alveolar bone augmentation, implant rehabilitation, socket preservation, with profound bone regeneration reported. Composite bone substitutes generally perform well clinically and are regarded as promising autograft alternatives.

3.4. Growth Factor-Based Bone Substitutes (GFBSs)
Growth factors (BMPs, PDGFs, IGFs) possess osteoinductive properties accelerating bone regeneration. First dental use of bioactivated materials: PRGF, PRP, PRF for accelerating bone healing in BRONJ (bisphosphonate-related osteonecrosis of the jaw) patients. Recent studies show varying/doubtful benefit of adding PRP to grafting materials for infrabony defects/sinus augmentation. BMP-2 and BMP-7 have been the most commonly used USFDA-approved growth factors since the early 2000s as active components of two major commercial products; production of one has since been halted, and use of the other has been associated with a large number of life-threatening complications. Products using recombinant human PDGF-BB (rhPDGF-BB) and rhBMPs have progressed beyond animal studies, generally combined with structural scaffold/carriers (allograft, collagen sponges, titanium mesh, beta-TCP/HA). Indicated for bilateral maxillary sinus augmentation, ridge augmentation, implant coating, bony/ridge defects. Key challenge: need for structural scaffold (GFBSs lack osteoconductive capability) plus need for GFs to reach target tissue while retaining bioactivity during therapeutic timeframe — considered extremely challenging to satisfy simultaneously. Strategies: GF entrapment within scaffold, covalent/non-covalent GF-scaffold binding, micro/nanoparticle GF reservoirs for prolonged controlled release.

Sticky bone: bone graft matrix enriched with growth factors using autologous fibrin glue. Stabilizes graft material in defects, accelerates regeneration, minimizes bone loss. Advantages: good moldability, structural stability, osteogenic progenitor selectivity (prevents soft tissue cell migration via fibrin interconnections), rapid cell adhesion/accelerated healing. Combined with CGF membrane or titanium mesh, achieved favorable 3D ridge augmentation over 4 months in atrophic alveolar ridge.

3.5. Bone Substitutes with Infused Living Osteogenic Cells
Viable osteogenic progenitor cells (MSCs — non-hematopoietic multipotent cells from bone marrow) used alone or combined with cytokines/GFs/scaffolding carriers/DBM. Can differentiate into osteogenic cells and regenerate large defects with scaffolding carrier. Bioengineering with MSCs markedly improves bone healing/reconstruction vs MSCs alone or scaffold alone, improving successful implant placement rates.

Preclinical periodontal regeneration studies: heterologous MSCs from dental pulp of extracted third molars (cell sheets or injections) significantly increased alveolar bone height regeneration by 52.7mm and 32.4mm respectively in experimental pig models (Cao et al., Hu et al.) — difference attributed to 3D cell-sheet structure mimicking structural scaffold function. MSCs from heterologous periodontal ligament tissue increased alveolar bone regeneration more than dental-pulp-derived MSCs in experimental dog models (Park et al.). Clinically approved commercial products utilize autologous MSC sources with appropriate scaffolds, indicated for sinus augmentation of severely atrophic maxilla for predictable implant placement.

Limitations: low stem cell survival rates post-transplantation, high cost, procedural complexity, manufacturing difficulty with autogenous cells, need for special storage (below -80C), long wait/processing times, legal regulation. Currently not routinely applied — restricted to specific indications.

4. Future of Bone Substitute Materials in Dentistry

Autografts remain the gold standard and only material possessing all four fundamental biological properties despite limited availability. All currently available materials on the market demonstrate a shortcoming in biomechanical performance. Producing a mechanically strong, interconnected porous structure allowing ideal osseointegration and vascularization is the major difficulty in material development — synthetic bone substitutes only possess osteoconductive properties, restricting bone regeneration to the outer surface layer. Careful structural design (pore size, density, morphology, interconnectivity, resorbability) is needed. Recent trend: incorporating osteoinductive growth factors and/or MSCs with structural scaffolds to increase bone regenerative potential and inhibit inflammatory responses, plus controlled time-release delivery of growth factors to maintain bioactivity over the therapeutic window. Major challenge: lack of research investigating safety/efficacy of newer materials — most evidence derived from case reports or experimental animal models, questionable reliability; more standardized preclinical/clinical studies needed.

5. Conclusions

Bone graft and substitute materials (particulate or block form) are mostly used in dentistry to regenerate missing hard tissue. High and growing demand for new, more efficient grafting materials exists. Current materials primarily serve as a structural framework satisfying only osteoconductivity; graft-vs-host response issues persist for non-autograft-derived materials. Tissue engineering progress (diverse ceramic/polymeric substitutes integrated with growth factors or modified with living osteogenic progenitor cells) is expanding understanding at the molecular level, enabling better control/modification of structure and surface properties. Natural bone grafts have gradually been replaced by synthetic bone substitutes due to continual technological advancement. Development of hybrid grafts utilizing growth factors and living osteogenic cells capable of inducing bone regeneration represents the future of dental bone grafting and dental implants (e.g., substitutes releasing BMPs or PDGFs in a controlled manner). More work is needed to develop dental biomaterials with porous structure, mechanical stability, controlled degradation, and remodeling ability comparable to the rate of new bone formation.

Introduction

Over time, tremendous progress in orthodontics has been made as a result of both evolutionary and revolutionary innovations. Revolutionary changes bring in extreme, transformative adjustments as opposed to evolutionary changes, which include gradual advancements and modifications through time. Fixed orthodontic appliances have developed in an evolutionary manner over the past century, with progressively better bracket and wire frameworks. But transparent aligner technology has undergone a dramatic change that has drastically changed the orthodontic treatment scene.

A significant and disruptive change in orthodontics is clear aligner treatment. Clear aligners give patients a more discrete and cozier alternative to conventional permanent equipment for tooth straightening. Because of their simplicity and beauty, these almost invisible aligners have grown in popularity. This makes orthodontic treatment less noticeable and more patient-friendly. Attachments of various shapes and sizes are used, along with other procedures and components, to increase the success of clear aligner therapy.

The goal of the current study is to determine how different attachment sizes and shapes affect tooth movement during clear aligner treatment, with a particular emphasis on rotation, tipping, and torquing motions. Orthodontists who want to provide their patients with the best clear aligner therapy must comprehend how these attachments affect treatment outcomes.

The accessible writing offers supportive experiences into the biomechanics of orthodontic tooth development. A PC-demonstrating technique called finite element analysis (FEA) has been utilized to look at how tensions and stresses are dispersed across the dental and periodontal designs during different sorts of tooth movements. For instance, William utilized FEA to decide the instantaneous center of rotation (ICR) of a maxillary focal incisor, giving data on the effect of stacking areas and the versatile qualities of the periodontal film on tooth development.[]

Comparable examinations, such as those by Puente[] and Rudolph,[] zeroed in on the distinctions in weight on dental and periodontal designs as they examined pressure appropriation during tipping and force movements. These discoveries stress that it is so pivotal to fathom the mechanics of tooth development to further develop treatment results.

The improvement of innovation has likewise affected orthodontic exploration. To better understand the stress patterns in dental restorations, Verdonschot[] created three-dimensional (3D) finite element models of recovered human teeth using micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) methods. In order to better understand the complicated nature of tooth movement during orthodontic treatment, Hayashi[] presented a unique approach for the three-dimensional study of tooth movement. More recently, Thai[] used eye-tracking technology to investigate the esthetic assessment of clear aligner treatment attachments. This study investigated how patients’ visual preferences were influenced by attachment design, and it concluded that attachments had a substantial impact on patients’ perceptions of esthetics throughout treatment.

This study intends to add to the expanding body of information in the area of orthodontics given the transformational nature of clear aligner therapy and the improvements in orthodontic research. In order to help orthodontic practitioners maximize treatment outcomes and raise patient satisfaction, the research examines the impact of various attachment sizes and shapes on tooth movement during clear aligner therapy. Investigating attachment-related characteristics that may have an influence on treatment acceptability and compliance is crucial as patients value esthetics in their orthodontic treatment decisions. Understanding these subtleties can help orthodontists customize treatment programs to meet the specific needs of each patient, leading to clear aligner therapy outcomes that are more predictable and satisfying. This study fills in the gap between orthodontics’ technological development and its contemporary therapeutic ramifications.

Materials and Methods

This analytical study used FEA to examine the impact of various composite attachment sizes and shapes on tooth movement during clear aligner treatment. The work was carried out in a personal lab with FEA software. In the study, replicas of the upper right human canine from the permanent dentition were created using computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM). These CAD models were created using an STL-formatted 3D digital model that was scanned.[] The models portrayed the periodontal ligament (PDL), attachments, and aligners as well as the upper right human canine. Based on previously published research, the characteristics of alveolar bone, teeth, composite attachments, and plastic aligners were allocated.

STL files were transformed into Initial Graphics Exchange Specification (IGES) format before being used to build the CAD models. Then, Catia version 5 release 19 was used to create the CAD geometry for the canine, PDL, attachments, and aligners. The PDL was modeled with an average thickness of 0.2 mm, whereas the Aligner section was modeled with an average thickness of 0.5 mm. The CAD assembly was then imported into the ANSYS version 15 program. Tetrahedral elements were used in ANSYS 15 to mesh the CAD models, guaranteeing adequate discretization for FEA. Interfaces between the ligament and the bone, the tooth and the ligament, and the tooth’s attachments were all bound, indicating solid unions without relative movement. The friction coefficient of the contact surfaces between the aligner and the tooth surface and attachment was determined to be 0.2, which is consistent with earlier research.

The attachment was subjected to a constant force of 2.942 N, as measured by earlier studies, straight along all three axes. On the attachment’s active side, the displacement was adjusted at 0.3 mm. The greatest displacement was measured after monitoring the movement of three separate sites. After applying force to composite attachments of various shapes and sizes, stress was measured at a number of locations, including the canine root, PDL, alveolar bone, and cortical bone.[] For rotating (spheroidal and rectangular beveled), tipping (triangular beveled and vertical rectangular beveled), and torquing (rectangular power ridge) motions, the research looked at five alternative attachment shapes. Given the numerical nature of the data, computerized mathematical research was undertaken using ANSYS version 15 software, eliminating the need for statistical testing. Three models were generated for each attachment shape, resulting in a total of 15 models.a-d shows changes at the spherical model.

Results

Using ANSYS version 15, a finite element study was performed to better understand how different attachment shapes and sizes affect rotational, tilting, and torque motions during clear aligner treatment. The investigation concentrated on the distributions of displacement and stress within dental structures.

The study contrasted spheroidal and rectangular beveled attachments for rotational movement. According to the findings, spheroidal attachments had displacements as low as 0.000954 mm (2S model) and as high as 0.001173 mm (1S model). A minimum displacement of 0.000902 mm (3R model) and a maximum displacement of 0.001217 mm (1R model) were seen in comparison for rectangular beveled attachments. Rectangular beveled attachments showed more rotation as a force was applied [].

Triangular beveled attachments and vertical rectangular beveled attachments were contrasted for tipping movement. A maximum displacement of 0.002236 mm (1T model) and a minimum displacement of 0.002041 mm (2T model) were seen with triangular beveled attachments. The maximum displacement for vertical rectangular beveled attachments was 0.00203 mm (2V model), while the minimum displacement was 0.001158 mm (1V model).[] Triangular beveled attachments, in contrast, showed larger displacement, showing that they permitted more tipping movement under the same applied force [].

Torquing movement was also analyzed and measured utilizing power ridge attachments with rectangular shapes. The findings showed that the 3P model had the smallest displacement, at 0.000163 mm, while the 1P model had the most displacement, at 0.000239 mm. This result suggested that the 1P model produced torquing movement more successfully.[]

The study also assessed displacement in the x-, y-, and z-axes at the incisal, cervical (CEJ), and root locations of three different tooth sites. In spheroidal attachments, the incisal area of the 1S model had the highest sum displacement (0.0010701 mm), whereas the root area of the same model had the lowest sum displacement (0.0000037711 mm). In rectangular beveled attachments, the incisal region of the 3R model showed the highest sum displacement (0.00074864 mm), whereas the root area of the same model showed the lowest sum displacement (0.0000033116 mm) [].

Similar sums of displacement were seen in all three axes for triangular attachment models, with the largest displacement measured at the incisal region of the 3T model (0.0019761 mm) and the least displacement measured at the root area of the 1T model (0.000013253 mm). The greatest cumulative displacement for the vertical rectangular beveled attachment models was 0.0018623 mm (2V model) at the incisal area, while the smallest sum displacement was 0.000014124 mm (1V model) at the root area.[] For rectangular power ridge attachments, the incisal region of the 1P model showed the highest sum displacement (0.00021572 mm), whereas the root area of the same model showed the lowest sum displacement (0.0000017795 mm). This pattern showed that across all attachment types and sizes, the total of displacement was the greatest in the incisal area and reduced toward the root area [].

The periodontal ligament’s (PDL) stress distribution was measured. The 2V model of vertical rectangular beveled attachments saw the highest stress (0.230955 MPa), whereas the 3R model with rectangular power ridge attachments experienced the lowest stress (0.031305 MPa). The 2T model of triangular beveled attachments had the highest root area stress (0.02304 MPa), whereas the 2P model with rectangular power ridge attachments had the lowest root area stress (0.003211 MPa). Similar stress patterns were seen in the alveolar bone and cortical bone, with the 2V model experiencing the highest stress (0.507355 MPa) and the 2P model experiencing the lowest stress (0.045163 MPa).

In conclusion, our thorough FEA gave important information on how attachment functions during clear aligner therapy.[] It was discovered that attachment size and shape have a significant impact on how stress is distributed and how teeth move, providing orthodontic practitioners with the knowledge they need to choose the most effective treatment options for their patients.

Discussion

The ramifications of the findings in relation to clear aligner treatment and the biomechanics of tooth movement are discussed in this paper, along with the study’s limitations and practical consequences.

In recent years, clear aligner therapy has become extremely popular, mostly as a result of the rising desire for attractive orthodontic treatments among adult patients. This study emphasizes how important clear aligners are as a preferred treatment option for people seeking orthodontic treatment. Clear aligners have a number of benefits over conventional fixed appliances, including improved esthetics, increased comfort, better dental hygiene maintenance, lower pain, fewer appointments, and fewer emergency visits. These advantages complement the changing esthetic expectations and patient preferences in contemporary orthodontics, making clear aligners a quickly expanding orthodontic treatment option.

The study also acknowledges the clear aligner therapy’s drawbacks, including its relatively high cost, need on patient compliance, and inability to effectively cure complicated malocclusions without the use of additional auxiliaries.[] Clear aligners depend on a planned mismatch between the aligner and the teeth as opposed to fixed appliances, which precisely direct force to a specific place on the tooth through the interaction of metal wires and brackets. This disparity may make it difficult to manage the direction, amount, and application point of force during tooth movement.

The study digs into the biomechanics of tooth movement with clear aligners to solve these issues. It divides tooth movement into the force-driven system and the displacement-driven system. The displacement-driven system is largely responsible for controlling easy motions like tipping or tiny rotations. The tooth moves until it is lined up with the aligner, which is made to match the position of the tooth in the following staged position. However, this process has several drawbacks, especially when it comes to causing root motions and intricate tooth rotations.

In contrast, the force-driven system, which is based on biomechanical principles, is in charge of complicated movements, such as torque and axial root motions. The forms of aligners are altered to generate pressure spots or power ridges in order to put precise stresses on the teeth. The successful execution of targeted tooth motions depends heavily on these power ridges. Conventional and optimized attachments improve the force system even more and aid in directing the required actions.[] In contrast to optimal attachments, which are automatically positioned by the software to personalize the location, direction, and amount of force delivered to each tooth, conventional attachments serve as handles for aligners to move teeth.

Using the finite element approach, the research conducted a thorough investigation of various types and sizes of composite attachments and power ridges to understand their impacts on rotation, tipping, and torquing movements. It was discovered that the degree of tooth displacement was greatly impacted by the attachment’s size and shape. Particularly, displacement increased with smaller attachments, whereas displacement decreased with bigger attachments. This finding is consistent with the idea that tooth movement is more pronounced when force is applied to attachments with smaller surfaces.

The results of this study help with accurate patient selection and treatment planning by advancing our knowledge of the biomechanics of clear aligner therapy. It emphasizes how crucial attachment size and design are to improving treatment results and cutting down on treatment time.

It is important to recognize the study’s limitations, though. Further studies investigating the impacts of various force levels on various attachment designs and sizes are necessary because the research depended on a constant force. The study also concentrated on certain attachment types, and future research might broaden the scope to cover a wider variety of attachment styles. Clinical investigations are required to confirm the results and shed light on the practical efficacy of various attachments. This study investigates the impact of attachment types and sizes on tooth movement, which increases our understanding of the biomechanics of clear aligner treatment.[] It emphasizes the benefits of transparent aligners in contemporary orthodontic practice while also recognizing their drawbacks. Orthodontists can improve treatment precision and efficiency by maximizing attachment design and size. In order to close the gap between theoretical understanding and practical application in clear aligner treatment, future research should continue to investigate these issues.

Conclusion

The effects of various composite attachment sizes and shapes on tooth movement during clear aligner treatment were examined in the current study using FEA. In order to improve treatment planning and results, the study concentrated on rotation, tipping, and torqueing actions.

Rotational Movement: In terms of rotational movement, it was discovered that rectangular beveled attachments with measurements of 3.5 mm × 1.2 mm × 3.5 mm (1R model) produced the most rotation. During clear aligner treatment, these attachments were successful in reducing and improving tooth rotation. The significance of attachment design in obtaining precise and desirable tooth motions is highlighted by this research.

Tipping Movement: The triangle beveled attachment with dimensions of 5 mm × 0.8 mm × 2 mm (1T model) surpassed other attachments by producing the most displacement, according to the study’s assessment of tipping motions. This suggests that the size and form of the attachments are crucial in regulating tilting motions during therapy.[] When organizing situations that call for these motions, orthodontic practitioners should take these aspects into account.

Torquing Movement: The rectangular power ridge with dimensions of 0.5 mm × 0.5 mm × 5 mm (1P model) showed the most displacement during torquing. This emphasizes how important attachment choice is for attaining efficient tooth torquing in clear aligner treatment.

Displacement in the X-, Y-, and Z-Axes: For various attachment models, the study evaluated displacement in the X-, Y-, and Z-axes. It was determined that tooth displacement in all three dimensions is greatly influenced by the type of attachment used. The findings showed that varied attachment shapes and sizes might be deliberately used to maximize tooth movement in particular directions, allowing orthodontists to customize treatment programs to suit the demands of specific patients.

Stress Distribution: Differences in stress distribution were found according on the size and kind of attachments in the alveolar bone, cortical bone, root surface, and periodontal ligament (PDL). Understanding these stress patterns is essential for attaining desirable tooth motions while limiting any negative effects on supporting structures.

Force Dissipation and Attachment Size: An interesting finding was that as the attachment size grew, less force was delivered to the tooth, which reduced tooth displacement. The requirement for a balanced approach when choosing attachment dimensions is shown by the inverse association between attachment size and tooth displacement. When selecting attachment sizes, practitioners must take the intended tooth movement and the biomechanics of force delivery into account.

This research sheds important light on the biomechanics of clear aligner treatment, particularly with regard to the influence of attachment size and shape on tooth movement.[] The research highlights how important attachment design is in producing precise and controlled motions, which can eventually result in better treatment outcomes and shorter treatment times.

This research can help orthodontic professionals by emphasizing attachment selection as a crucial component of treatment planning. Practitioners can maximize the effectiveness of their treatments and the pleasure of their patients by intelligently selecting attachment types and sizes based on the necessary tooth motions. To further improve our comprehension of the biomechanics of clear aligner treatment and its therapeutic use, future study should investigate the effects of various force levels and attachment materials.

Comparative evaluation of the anesthetic efficacy of 4% buffered articaine versus 2% un-buffered lidocaine for dental injections in children: a double-blind randomized parallel group control trial

PMID: 42558346
PMCID: PMC13437181
DOI: 10.17245/jdapm.2026.26.075

INTRODUCTION

Management of procedural pain and anxiety during dental treatment is challenging in pediatric dental practice. Children's heightened emotional sensitivity, limited coping strategies, and tendency toward avoidance behavior make effective local anesthesia not merely a comfort measure but also a prerequisite for successful atraumatic care. Inadequate analgesia during this critical developmental window can precipitate dental fear, future avoidance, and oral health consequences. Despite decades of research on optimizing local anesthetic delivery, injection-related pain remains one of the most frequently cited deterrents to dental cooperation in children.

Lidocaine hydrochloride 2% with epinephrine has served as the reference standard for local dental anesthesia in pediatric populations for over six decades. Its predictable efficacy, established safety record, and widespread availability have ensured its primacy in clinical guidelines worldwide. However, the inherently acidic pH (3.5–4.5) of commercially formulated lidocaine, necessitated by epinephrine stabilization, imposes physiological disadvantages particularly consequential in children. The protonated, ionized form predominates at low pH, reducing membrane permeability, slowing onset, and intensifying the burning discomfort at the injection site.

Articaine, a hybrid amide ester local anesthetic introduced in dentistry in the 1970s, has attracted interest for pediatric applications. Its unique thiophene ring and additional ester side chain confer exceptional lipid solubility and tissue diffusion capacity approximately 1.5 times that of lidocaine. These pharmacokinetic properties facilitate transcortical diffusion in the mandible, potentially enabling infiltration anesthesia in anatomical regions that traditionally require inferior alveolar nerve blocks. The 4% concentration delivered a higher molecular dose per unit volume, and the ester moiety allows for partial plasma esterase metabolism, potentially reducing systemic accumulation.

pH buffering through addition of sodium bicarbonate to a local anesthetic cartridge immediately before use raises solution pH from approximately 3.5 toward physiological neutrality (7.0–7.4). At physiological pH, the equilibrium shifts decisively toward the non-ionized, lipid-soluble base form of the anesthetic, which traverses neuronal membranes more easily. The clinical corollaries include an accelerated onset of action, reduced injection-related stinging from acidic pH, and potentially more profound analgesia. This concept was first systematically explored by McKay et al. in 1987 for intradermal lidocaine and has been progressively adapted for intraoral dental use over the past three decades.

However, the existing evidence is limited. First, an overwhelming majority of buffering trials involve adult participants, in whom mandibular cortical bone density, pain threshold variation, and cooperative capacity differ fundamentally from those in children. Second, most published pediatric trials comparing buffered and non-buffered agents have employed the same drug across arms, leaving the clinically relevant question of inter-agent buffered comparison underexplored. Third, previous studies in children have principally reported injection pain as the sole outcome, neglecting operator experience, physiological stress markers, behavioral cooperation, and postoperative safety parameters. Fourth, the impact of buffered articaine on the duration of soft tissue anesthesia and incidence of postoperative lip and cheek trauma has not been systematically characterized.

The pilot phase of this investigation, conducted using a parallel group design in 40 children requiring dental injections, demonstrated that buffered 4% articaine produced significantly lower visual analog scale (VAS) and Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability pain scores and faster onset than non-buffered 2% lidocaine at both the maxillary and mandibular sites. Building upon these pilot findings, the present study was designed as a fully powered, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial (RCT) to rigorously characterize the comparative efficacy of buffered 4% articaine versus non-buffered 2% lidocaine across a comprehensive battery of intraoperative and postoperative outcomes in children aged 6–12 years. This study also extends previous work by incorporating operator difficulty assessment, cardiovascular monitoring, behavioral scoring, and a structured one-week adverse event follow-up. We hypothesized that buffered 4% articaine would demonstrate superior performance compared to non-buffered 2% lidocaine with respect to injection pain, onset time, operator-rated procedural difficulty, and behavioral cooperation, with a non-inferior safety profile.

METHODS

1. Study design and registration

This study was designed as a prospective, double-blind, two-arm, parallel-group, RCT. The trial was registered with the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI/2025/03/083198) before recruitment. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Human Ethics Committee (IHEC/SDC/PEDO-2401/25/010). All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision), Indian Council of Medical Research Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research, and CONSORT 2010 Reporting Standards for Randomized Trials.

2. Participants

Children aged 6–12 years, classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status (ASA I), who presented to the outpatient clinic for single-tooth dental procedures were considered for enrolment. Body weight was required to fall within the interquartile range for age and sex as defined by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention growth charts. Eligible participants were first-time dental attendees demonstrating moderate baseline anxiety, defined by a Frankl Behavior Rating Scale score of 2–4 and a Children's Dental Anxiety Scale score within the moderate range.

Inclusion criteria required written informed consent from the parent or legal guardian, the child's own assent, willingness to attend two follow-up appointments (at procedure completion and at one week), and the absence of a prior history of adverse reactions to any amide local anesthetic. Children were excluded if they had systemic medical conditions; were receiving medications affecting pain perception or behavior (including sedatives, anticonvulsants, or analgesics within 24 hours); had active infection or localized pathology at the planned injection site; possessed highly symptomatic teeth with irreversible pulpitis; had any documented developmental, cognitive, linguistic, or communication disorder; had prior dental treatment experience; or had received local anesthesia within the previous seven days. Participant recruitment will be conducted between March 2025 and October 2025.

3. Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined using G*Power version 3.1.9.7. Assuming a two-tailed independent samples t-test, a significance level of alpha = 0.05, statistical power of 0.80, and a Cohen's d effect size of 0.8 based on pilot data demonstrating a mean difference in VAS injection pain of approximately 1.7 units with a pooled standard deviation of approximately 1.0, a minimum of 26 participants per group was required. To account for a 20% anticipated dropout rate and permit subgroup analyses, the final sample size was set at 50 participants per group (N = 100). This sample size also provided more than 90% power to detect the observed onset time difference.

4. Randomization and blinding

After confirming eligibility and obtaining consent, each participant was allocated in a 1:1 ratio to one of the two groups using a computer-generated random allocation sequence produced by an independent biostatistician using block randomization with variable block sizes (4, 6, and 8). Allocation concealment was maintained using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE method). A double-blind protocol was strictly maintained throughout the study. Anesthetic solutions were prepared in identically coded syringes by a trained preparation technician who was not involved in the clinical care or outcome assessment. The solutions were visually indistinguishable. Clinicians administering anesthesia, the child, the parent or guardian, and the outcome assessors were all blinded to the group allocation. Blinding integrity was formally assessed at study completion by asking the clinicians and assessors to guess the agent used. At study completion, 23 of the 50 clinician guesses (46%) and 24 of the 50 assessor guesses (48%) were correct and did not differ significantly from chance (exact binomial test, P > 0.05), confirming the effectiveness of blinding.

5. Intervention

Group A (Buffered Articaine): Each child received buffered 4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine (Septanest, Septogon, France). Buffering was performed immediately before injection by adding 0.18 mL of 8.4% sodium bicarbonate (SODAC, Neon Uttaranchal Biotech Ltd., India) to a 1.62 mL articaine cartridge using a sterile insulin syringe, maintaining a final cartridge volume of 1.8 mL. This resulted in an approximately 10% dilution of articaine, yielding a sodium bicarbonate-to-anesthetic volume ratio of approximately 1:10. The cartridge was inverted five–six times to achieve uniform mixing.

Group B (Non-buffered Lidocaine): Children received non-buffered 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine (Xicaine, ICPA Ltd., India), with a final cartridge volume of 1.8 mL, to ensure an identical final injection volume and blinding.

In both groups, the injection site was dried with sterile gauze, and topical anesthesia (10% lidocaine spray) was applied using a cotton-tip applicator for 60 s prior to injection. A 30-gauge short needle and a self-aspirating syringe were employed uniformly. The injection rate was standardized at 1 mL/min for all the participants using a pre-calibrated syringe driver. An intradermal sensitivity test using 0.1 mL of the allocated anesthetic solution was performed in all participants. Pulp therapy procedures commenced only after objective and subjective confirmation of profound anesthesia.

6. Outcome measures

Primary outcomes comprised three variables: (1) Injection pain intensity, measured on a validated 100-mm VAS immediately upon needle withdrawal; (2) Onset time of anesthesia in seconds, measured from needle withdrawal to the child's verbal report of complete lip and area numbness confirmed by painless probing of the gingival margin with a blunt explorer; and (3) Operator-rated procedural difficulty, assessed on a 100-mm VAS (0 = no difficulty; 100 = extreme difficulty) by the treating clinician immediately after the procedure. Secondary outcomes included: (4) Procedure pain during pulp therapy assessed by the child on a VAS immediately post-procedure; (5) Duration of soft tissue anesthesia in minutes from confirmed onset to the child's report of restored normal sensation; (6) Heart rate (bpm) during injection recorded by continuous pulse oximetry; (7) Behavior during injection rated on the Frankl Behavioral Rating Scale; (8) Pulpal anesthesia success (dichotomous: Yes/No); (9) Need for re-injection (Yes/No); (10) Incidence of lip or cheek biting within two hours post-procedure; and (11) Delayed adverse events at one week post-procedure.

7. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0) and Python 3.11 (SciPy 1.11.0). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and as frequencies and percentages (%) for categorical variables. Normality of continuous outcome variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were compared using an independent-samples t-test. The Frankl behavioral score was compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical outcomes were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. A two-tailed significance level of P < 0.05 was employed throughout. All the analyses followed the intention-to-treat principle.

RESULTS

1. Study flow and baseline characteristics

One hundred children were enrolled and randomly allocated to the buffered and non-buffered lidocaine groups (n = 50 each). All 100 participants completed the study and were included in the analysis (zero attrition). The groups were well-matched at baseline, with no significant differences in age, weight, sex distribution, baseline heart rate, baseline Frankl score, or procedure type (all P > 0.05). The mean age was 8.58 ± 1.90 years in the articaine group and 8.80 ± 1.96 years in the lidocaine group. The male-to-female ratio was comparable between the two groups (24:26 vs. 28:22). Procedure types (pulp therapy, extraction, and restoration) and tooth types were similarly distributed between groups, confirming effective randomization.

2. Primary outcomes: injection pain, onset time, and operator difficulty

Children in the buffered articaine group reported significantly lower injection pain compared with those in the non-buffered lidocaine group (2.25 ± 0.94 vs. 3.93 ± 0.90; mean difference 1.68, 95% CI 2.04–1.32; t = 9.17; P < 0.001). Anesthetic onset time was markedly faster in the buffered articaine group (71.98 ± 15.24 seconds vs. 115.28 ± 20.04 seconds; mean difference 43.30 seconds, 95% CI 50.36–36.24; t = 12.16; P < 0.001), representing a 37.6% reduction in onset time. Operator-rated procedural difficulty was substantially lower in the articaine group (2.27 ± 0.99 vs. 4.22 ± 0.98; mean difference 1.95, 95% CI 2.33–1.57; t = 9.91; P < 0.001), reflecting greater ease of procedure management when buffered articaine was employed.

3. Secondary outcomes: procedure pain, anesthesia duration, and cardiovascular response

Intraoperative procedure pain was significantly lower in the buffered articaine group (VAS: 2.15 ± 0.99 vs. 3.56 ± 1.05; mean difference 1.41, 95% CI 1.81–1.01; t = 6.89; P < 0.001). Soft tissue anesthesia provided significantly longer effect following buffered articaine administration (159.02 ± 19.10 minutes vs. 137.84 ± 17.54 minutes; mean difference +21.18 minutes, 95% CI 13.62–28.74; t = 5.78; P < 0.001). Heart rate during injection was significantly lower in the buffered articaine group (100.46 ± 8.10 bpm vs. 106.44 ± 7.65 bpm; mean difference 5.98 bpm, 95% CI 9.09–2.87; t = 3.80; P = 0.0003), suggesting attenuation of the physiological stress response with buffered anesthetic delivery. Behavioral cooperation during injection, rated on the Frankl scale, was significantly better in the articaine group (3.18 ± 0.60 vs. 2.48 ± 0.74; Mann–Whitney U = 1856.5; P < 0.001).

4. Categorical clinical outcomes

Pulpal anesthesia success rates were high and comparable between the groups (84.0% vs. 82.0%; chi-square = 0.00; P = 1.000), indicating equivalent clinical effectiveness in achieving adequate pulpal anesthesia. The need for reinjection was numerically lower in the buffered articaine group (16.0% vs. 28.0%; chi-square = 1.457; P = 0.227), although this difference was not significant. Lip or cheek biting within two hours post-procedure was infrequent in both groups (12.0% vs. 16.0%; chi-square = 0.083; P = 0.773). Delayed adverse events at one week were rare overall, occurring in 2.0% of the articaine group and 14.0% of the lidocaine group; this difference approached, but did not reach, conventional statistical significance (chi-square = 3.397; P = 0.065). No systemic adverse reactions, anaphylaxis, or serious adverse events were observed in either group.

DISCUSSION

This RCT provides comprehensive, multidimensional evidence that pH-buffered 4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine offers clinically meaningful advantages over non-buffered 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine for local dental anesthesia in children aged 6–12 years. The primary findings of significantly lower injection pain, faster anesthetic onset, and reduced operator difficulty were consistent across all continuous outcome domains and corroborated by objective physiological data showing attenuated heart rate response and independent behavioral observations, confirming superior cooperation.

The 37.6% reduction in anesthetic onset time observed with buffered articaine (71.98 ± 15.24 vs. 115.28 ± 20.04 seconds) has direct clinical consequences in pediatric practice. Children's tolerance for procedural anticipation is limited, and the waiting period between injection and commencement of treatment is a recognized source of behavioral deterioration. Faster onset not only reduces this anxiogenic interval but also allows clinicians to work more efficiently, maintaining the child's cooperation within a narrower window of engagement. The onset advantage observed in this study is consistent with pilot data from this investigation, where buffered articaine showed mean onset times of 83.4 ± 23.7 and 65.3 ± 15.6 seconds for mandibular and maxillary injections respectively. The shorter onset values in the present study may reflect the larger sample size, parallel-group design, and exclusion of participants with higher anxiety.

The basis for these findings is well established. Commercial local anesthetic solutions are formulated at acidic pH (3.5–4.5) to stabilize epinephrine against oxidative degradation. This acidic environment protonates the anesthetic molecule, shifting the Henderson–Hasselbalch equilibrium toward an ionized membrane-impermeable form. By buffering with sodium bicarbonate at a 1:10 ratio, the solution pH was raised toward physiological levels (6.5–7.0 for articaine), markedly increasing the proportion of non-ionized bases capable of rapid lipid membrane traversal. The comparatively low pKa (7.8 vs. 7.9 for lidocaine) and its thiophene ring confer superior lipid solubility, further amplifying this advantage, resulting in a pharmacokinetically optimized molecule at physiological pH.

The significantly lower heart rate during injection in the buffered articaine group (100.46 vs. 106.44 bpm; P = 0.0003) provides objective physiological corroboration for the subjective pain and behavioral findings. Injection-related pain activates sympathetic autonomic pathways, producing tachycardia, and attenuation of this response in the articaine group suggests that reduced nociceptive input translates into measurable physiological benefits. Behavioral cooperation on the Frankl scale showed a significant advantage for buffered articaine (3.18 vs. 2.48; P < 0.001), reflecting that reduced injection discomfort meaningfully supports better child behavior, with direct implications for treatment success and clinical workflow.

The significantly longer duration of soft tissue anesthesia in the buffered articaine group (159.02 vs. 137.84 minutes; P < 0.001) is a nuanced finding. While prolonged anesthesia may seem advantageous from the perspective of procedural coverage, it also increases the theoretical risk of inadvertent soft tissue self-injury. However, the observed rate of lip or cheek biting was not significantly higher in the articaine group (12.0% vs. 16.0%; P = 0.773), suggesting that prolonged numbness did not translate into increased trauma, possibly because the parents were counseled to monitor the children during the post-treatment period.

The comparable pulpal success rates (84% vs. 82%; P = 1.000) indicated that both agents achieved similarly high rates of adequate pulpal anesthesia in this pediatric sample. The 16% reinjection rate in the articaine group versus 28% in the lidocaine group (P = 0.227) did not reach significance in this study, although the absolute difference of 12 percentage points was clinically meaningful.

Compared with adult studies in similar domains, pediatric findings show more pronounced advantages for buffered articaine. This disparity likely reflects the thinner cortical plates, higher bone porosity, and shorter diffusion distances in the pediatric mandible, which amplify the tissue penetration advantage of buffered articaine in children relative to adults. These anatomical differences fundamentally support pediatric-specific evidence for local anesthetic optimization.

The novelty of the present study lies in several dimensions absent from previous literature: (1) it is among the first to compare a buffered agent (articaine) against the unbuffered clinical gold standard (lidocaine) in children, rather than comparing buffered versus non-buffered formulations of the same drug; (2) it incorporates operator difficulty as a formal, quantified outcome; (3) it employs objective physiological monitoring (heart rate) as a stress surrogate alongside subjective and behavioral measures; (4) it includes structured postoperative follow-up at one week; and (5) it recruits from a first-time dental attendee cohort, minimizing carryover learning effects from prior dental experience.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. (1), while a sample size of 100 was adequate for primary continuous outcomes, the statistical power was insufficient to detect differences in categorical outcomes, including the reinjection rate and delayed adverse events with confidence. (2) the study was conducted at a single academic center; therefore, the results may not be generalizable to community dental settings with different patient demographics or operator experience levels. (3) Despite the blinding validation, the possibility of subtle differences in injection burns between the two agents could not be entirely excluded. (4) the study did not distinguish between maxillary and mandibular injection outcomes in the primary analysis. A stratified analysis by arch may reveal differential performance. (5) follow-up was limited to one week; longer-term outcomes were not captured. (6) A cost-effectiveness analysis was not performed. (7) The use of a 0.1 mL intradermal sensitivity test prior to dental injection may have influenced anticipatory anxiety, pain perception, and total anesthetic exposure.

Conclusions

Buffered 4% articaine with epinephrine demonstrates superior clinical performance compared with non-buffered 2% lidocaine in children aged 6–12 years, producing lower injection and procedural pain, faster anesthetic onset, improved behavior and operator experience, and prolonged soft-tissue anesthesia while maintaining a comparable safety profile. Despite these advantages, the pulpal anesthetic success was similarly high in both groups. Because this comparison involved differences in the anesthetic agent, concentration, and buffering status, the observed benefits could not be attributed to buffering alone. Further multicenter studies are required to confirm these findings and inform clinical practice guidelines.

Removal of failed dental implants revisited: Questions and answers
Solderer A, Al-Jazrawi A, Sahrmann P, Jung R, Attin T, Schmidlin PR
Clinical and Experimental Dental Research 2019;5(6):712-724
DOI: 10.1002/cre2.234  PMID: 31890309  PMCID: PMC6934347
Source: PubMed Central full text (JATS), retrieved via PubMed MCP.

=== ABSTRACT ===
Objectives: This narrative review is aiming on showing reasons for implant failure, removal
techniques, and respective clinical considerations; further, the survival rate of implants in
previous failed sites is examined.
Materials and methods: Questions have been formulated, answered, and discussed through a
literature search including studies assessing implant failure and removal up to 2018.
Results: Studies describing reasons for implant failure, implant removal techniques, and the
reinsertion of implants in a previous failed site were included. To date, peri-implantitis is the
main reason for late implant failure (81.9%). Trephine burs seem to be the best-known method for
implant removal. Nevertheless, the counter-torque-ratchet-technique, because of the low
invasiveness, should be the first choice for the clinician. Regarding zirconia implant removal, only
scarce data are available. Implantation in previously failed sites irrespective of an early or late
failure results in 71% to 100% survival over 5 years.
Conclusion: If removal is required, interventions should be based on considerations regarding
minimally invasive access and management as well as predictable healing. (Post)Operative
considerations should primarily depend on the defect type and the consecutive implantation plans.

=== INTRODUCTION ===
Dental implants report high success rates of 97% over 10 years (Buser) and 75% over 20 years
(Chappuis). Biological complications may lead to complete implant failure and removal. Failures
are early (before functional loading; inadequate healing/osseointegration — overheating,
overpreparation, lack of primary stability, overload, parafunction; implants clinically mobile,
easy to remove) or late (after loading; mainly biological — peri-implantitis, implant fractures).
Rarely a healthy osseointegrated implant is a "failure" due to extreme malpositioning. Most
late-failing implants are NOT mobile and remain at least partially osseointegrated apically, so
explantation can be challenging and invasive, potentially harming neighbouring teeth/structures.
Removal methods described: counter-torque ratchet, piezo surgery, high-speed burs, elevators,
forceps, trephine burs, laser surgery. This is the first review to also address ailing/failing
zirconia implants.

=== METHODS / SCREENING ===
Narrative review; databases PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane. Two authors screened by
title/abstract; Cohen's kappa 0.65. Out of 3,997 screened articles, 34 studies were included.
Inclusion: RCTs, case series, retrospective, case reports, systematic reviews, narrative reviews.
Exclusion: not osseointegrated implants, no screw-shaped implants, orthodontic implants, animal
studies.

=== Q1: WHEN AND WHY DO IMPLANTS NEED REMOVAL? ===
Chrcanovic analyzed 10,096 implants; 642 failures (6.36%); 49% of failures/removals in the first
year. Etiology categorized as biological, mechanical, iatrogenic, functional.
- Biological: Peri-implantitis = major biological complication; main reason for late failure.
  Derks meta-analysis: 22% of implants develop peri-implantitis. Anitua: of 158 non-mobile implants
  scheduled for explantation, main reason peri-implantitis 82.9%, malpositioning 13.9%. Implants do
  not become mobile until osseointegration is nearly completely lost. Failure thresholds debated:
  >50% bone loss (Misch) vs >=75% bone loss / <3 mm remaining bone contact.
- Mechanical: Implant fractures — causes bruxism, large occlusal forces, trauma, reduced diameter,
  material fatigue, advanced bone loss. Prevalence ~1% (Goodacre); Lee: 70/19,087 implants (0.4%)
  fractured. Higher risk in molar region. Zirconia fracture rate ~1.95% overall (0.2% for
  market-available), similar to titanium.
- Surgical malpositioning: ~10% of implants prosthetically inadequate position. Sendyk
  meta-analysis: experienced surgeons (>50 implants/yr) 2.4% failure vs inexperienced 12.2%
  (OR 2.18, 95% CI 1.40-3.39).

=== Q2: HOW SHOULD IMPLANTS BE REMOVED? ===
Selected technique should be fast, minimally traumatic, cost-effective. Only case reports/series
available.
- Tooth extraction set: for mobile implants or little residual BIC — levers, elevators, forceps.
- Trephine burs: cylindrical blades, standard/common. Choose diameter only slightly larger than
  implant to conserve bone. 1,200-1,500 rpm with maximal water cooling to avoid thermal necrosis.
  Use only if no less invasive alternative. Complications reported: mandible fracture, osteomyelitis
  — assess local anatomy incl. CBCT. Deeb: CAD/CAM surgical guides for 3D-guided, less invasive.
- Piezo surgery: less traumatic than trephine; 24,000-29,500 Hz, precise/selective cutting spares
  vessels/nerves. Circumferential osteotomy with diamond insert, intermittent mode + saline
  cooling. Improved postoperative bone healing vs trephine. Caution with pacemakers.
- Laser surgery: Er,Cr:YSGG — circumferential destruction of bone-implant interface via
  hydrokinetic effect; clean cuts, no thermal damage, good hemostasis. In vitro vs trephine: laser
  removed ~half the bone (0.302 vs 0.519 cm) but was >2x slower (44.1 vs 17.2 s); well-defined bone
  edges without thermal alteration vs microcracks with trephine. Less invasive but more time
  consuming; more research needed.
- Counter-torque ratchet technique (CTRT): LEAST TRAUMATIC; keeps surrounding bone ~undamaged.
  Two modalities:
  (1) Intact-connection: fitting abutment/engaging tool, counterclockwise torque. Internal
      connection easier than external (leverage). Thread shape matters — square threads highest BIC,
      hardest to remove; tapered bodies easier than parallel; apical antirotational design hampers.
  (2) Reverse screw technique (RST): for fractured/damaged implants — screw driven counterclockwise
      to grip, then counter-torque removes unit; cool with saline (friction heat).
  Simon & Caputo: 31 orthodontic mini-implants — 26 removed intact at 11-23 Ncm; remainder
  fractured at bone level at 27-35 Ncm. Anitua (BTI kit): 42 patients/91 implants — 78 removed by
  CTRT alone, 13 needed trephine+BTI. Anitua 81 patients/158 non-mobile implants — 139 removed at
  146 Ncm without burs; 19 needed trephine + higher torque 161 Ncm. Indication for trephine:
  removal torque >200 Ncm, fractured implants/prosthetic components. Removal torque lowest in
  plasma-sprayed, highest in acid-etched+sandblasted surfaces. **4 mm remaining osseointegration =
  critical threshold for CTRT-alone**; more osseointegration -> combine CTRT + bone-cutting
  (trephine/piezo). Beware fractures in narrow implants.
- Electrosurgery (thermo-explantation): monopolar unit induces thermo-necrosis / osseodisintegration
  at bone-implant interface, then low counterclockwise torque removes implant. Cunliffe & Barclay:
  15 s application; implant removed 1 week later with CTRT at 30 N. Concern: mucosal/extended
  osteonecrosis. Bone thermal-necrosis thresholds: 44-47 C for >1 min (Eriksson & Albrektsson);
  47 C critical; 56-70 C harmful. More research needed.

=== Q3: HOW DOES IMPLANT MATERIAL INFLUENCE REMOVAL? ===
Zirconia: osseointegration similar to titanium; BIC 25-88% (Zr) vs 24-85% (Ti); removal torque
9-78 N (Zr) vs 7-74 N (Ti). Bone-resection methods (extraction set, trephine, piezo, laser) apply
comparably. But CTRT more risky for zirconia due to lower fracture toughness (Zr 4-18 MPa/m vs Ti
77 MPa/m) — likely to fracture; CTRT only feasible with extensive surrounding bone resorption. No
data on electrosurgical removal of zirconia; zirconia acts as a thermal insulator at elevated
temperatures, suggesting thermal method may not be appropriate.

=== Q4: FATE OF SURROUNDING BONE / SOCKET PREPARATION ===
Implants lack bundle bone, so post-explantation defect behaves differently from post-extraction
socket (no accentuated bundle-bone resorption). No literature quantifying bone remodeling after
removal of osseointegrated failing implants. Schwarz peri-implantitis defect classification:
Class I intrabony (circumferential Class Ie 55.3% most common — behaves like self-containing
three-wall defect, best for GBR) and Class II horizontal. Expected defect morphology should guide
removal method choice. Immediate/early re-placement often requires bone augmentation. Extra care
for bisphosphonate/bone-metabolism-drug patients.

=== Q5: CONSIDERATIONS WHEN REINSERTING IMPLANTS ===
Gomes meta-analysis (11 articles) + 2 more: 704 replaced implants in 579 patients. Survival 88.7%
in previously failed sites; 85% in twice-failed sites. Second implantation survival 71-100% (7-180
months); third attempt 50-100%; success 90-100% (few reported). Wang: replacement after early
failure — survival 94.6%, success 90.6% at avg 69.4 months (no significant difference vs late
failure). "Cluster effect" — multiple simultaneous failures suggest systemic/genetic factors to
control before re-surgery. Replacement implants should be LARGER diameter and/or rough-surfaced for
primary stability; shorter/similar-diameter -> lower survival. Sinus-grafted sites: second surgery
100% (grafted) / 92% (nongrafted) survival — grafting no obstacle. No statistical difference between
immediate vs delayed reimplantation given sufficient bone quality/quantity and absence of infection;
extended defects need buccal plate correction with GBR first.

=== CONCLUSIONS ===
Limited data (mainly case reports / small series). CTRT is least invasive and should be first
choice; trephine burs most common but remove bone; choose technique by defect type to preserve bone
for reimplantation; zirconia removal data scarce; reinsertion in failed sites is predictable
(71-100% survival). More research (RCTs, standardized outcomes) needed.

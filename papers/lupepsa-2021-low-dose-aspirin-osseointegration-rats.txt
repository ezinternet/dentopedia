Title: Effects of low-dose aspirin on the osseointegration process in rats.
Authors: Lupepsa AC, Vargas-Sanchez P, Moro MG, Mecca LEA, Claudino M, Soares PBF,
Fischborn AR, Andreis JD, Franco GCN.
Journal: International Journal of Implant Dentistry. 2021 Jan 13;7(1):3.

BACKGROUND
Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) is one of the most widely consumed drugs worldwide.
At low doses (roughly 75-100 mg/day in humans) it is prescribed chronically for
cardiovascular prophylaxis because of its irreversible inhibition of platelet
cyclooxygenase (COX). A growing number of dental-implant candidates are therefore
long-term low-dose aspirin users. Because COX-derived prostaglandins participate in
the bone-healing cascade, the authors asked whether chronic low-dose aspirin
interferes with the biological process of osseointegration - the direct
bone-to-implant apposition on which implant success depends. Earlier work had shown
that higher, anti-inflammatory/analgesic aspirin doses can delay bone and fracture
healing, but the specifically cardioprotective LOW dose had not been characterized
around a titanium implant.

AIM
To evaluate, histomorphometrically, the influence of continuous low-dose aspirin on
early and late osseointegration of machined-surface titanium implants placed in the
rat tibia.

METHODS
The study followed the ARRIVE guidelines for animal research. Thirty-two male Wistar
rats were randomized into four groups of eight. The dose of aspirin was 6.75 mg/kg,
derived from the human cardioprotective dose of ~75-80 mg via the FDA body-surface-area
animal-to-human conversion factor. Drug (or saline vehicle) was delivered by daily oral
gavage. Critically, dosing began 42 days BEFORE implant surgery, so that a steady-state
antiplatelet/COX-inhibited condition was established before the bone was challenged,
and continued through the post-operative period.
Groups:
  - CG-7  : control (saline), euthanized 7 days after implant placement.
  - AG-7  : aspirin 6.75 mg/kg, euthanized 7 days after implant placement.
  - CG-28 : control (saline), euthanized 28 days after implant placement.
  - AG-28 : aspirin 6.75 mg/kg, euthanized 28 days after implant placement.
A machined (turned) commercially pure titanium implant, 1.5 mm diameter x 8.0 mm
length, was installed in the tibia of each animal. After euthanasia the tibiae were
processed for undecalcified histology and histomorphometric analysis. Two parameters
were quantified around the implant: bone-to-implant contact (BIC, the percentage of
the implant perimeter in direct contact with bone) and bone area between threads
(BABT, the fraction of the inter-thread area occupied by bone). These index the
quality and quantity of peri-implant bone formation respectively.

RESULTS
At the EARLY 7-day time point, aspirin significantly reduced bone apposition. BIC in
AG-7 was 62.8% +/- 17.1 versus 82.7% +/- 15.2 in CG-7 (p < 0.05). The bone area
between threads followed the same direction: AG-7 showed 70.9% +/- 15.2, trending lower
than the saline controls, indicating less bone had formed between the threads in the
first week.
At the LATE 28-day time point the difference had disappeared. BIC in AG-28 was
91.9% +/- 5.4 versus 89.9% +/- 9.7 in CG-28 - essentially identical and not
statistically different. BABT in AG-28 reached 95.4% +/- 3.7, likewise recovered to
control levels. Within every group, both BIC and BABT were higher at 28 days than at
7 days, reflecting the normal maturation of peri-implant bone over time. Thus the
inhibitory effect of low-dose aspirin was transient: present in the first week of
healing and no longer detectable by four weeks.

DISCUSSION / MECHANISM
The authors attribute the early deficit to COX inhibition and the consequent drop in
prostaglandin E2 (PGE2). PGE2 promotes the vasodilation and angiogenesis that a
freshly placed implant site needs for early new-bone formation; suppressing it slows
the initial deposition phase. Aspirin exposure has also been linked to reduced
expression of osteogenic signaling molecules - bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2),
the osteoblast master transcription factor CBFA-1/Runx2, and vascular endothelial
growth factor (VEGF) - all of which support the early wave of bone formation. At the
same time, aspirin suppresses osteoclast differentiation through the RANKL pathway,
which reduces resorption; the net remodeling balance over four weeks allows the bone
to catch up, explaining the full recovery by day 28. The magnitude of the low-dose
effect was modest ("discrete") and self-limiting, in contrast to prior reports where
higher aspirin doses (around a 325 mg human equivalent, in a rabbit model) more clearly
delayed bone healing, and where the NSAID ketorolac delayed fracture healing though all
fractures still united by day 35.

CONCLUSION
Chronic low-dose (cardioprotective) aspirin produced only a discrete, transient
inhibitory effect confined to the EARLY (7-day) stage of peri-implant bone deposition,
with no measurable effect at the late (28-day) stage. The finding does not
contraindicate implant placement in low-dose aspirin users, but it suggests prudence
about aggressive EARLY loading, since the very first week of osseointegration may be
slightly slower in these patients.

LIMITATIONS
Only a machined (turned) implant surface was tested, so results may not transfer to
modern moderately-rough surfaces that osseointegrate faster. Only two healing intervals
were sampled, and the rat tibia model differs from human jaw bone in loading, density
and remodeling rate. The study measured histomorphometry, not biomechanical
removal-torque or molecular markers directly.

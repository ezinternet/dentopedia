## ABSTRACT
Introduction and aimsHealth-related emergencies, from minor incidents to life-threatening situations, can occur unexpectedly in dental clinics. Ensuring that dentists and their teams are well-prepared with adequate training and essential equipment is crucial. Proper preparedness can lead to effective management of emergencies and reduce potential complications. This cross-sectional national study aimed at assessing the preparedness of Lebanese dentists in managing medical emergencies.
MethodsData was collected between August and October 2024 using an online questionnaire which was distributed to all registered Lebanese dentists through the Lebanese Dental Associations located in Beirut and Tripoli.
ResultsThe study found that 38.2% of dentists had an emergency kit in their clinics, and 88.0% had some emergency equipment; yet only 5.9% had a defibrillator. While 71.3% had emergency training in university, 28.5% never received it. Only 18.8% reported staff training in medical emergencies. Most dentists (74.2%) encountered minor medical issues, and 22.4% faced major emergencies, with only 1.8% reporting cardiac arrest cases. Vasovagal reactions, hypoglycemia, and orthostatic hypotension were common emergencies. While 69.0% felt capable of managing minor issues, confidence dropped for major emergencies and specific procedures. An overwhelming 87.8% expressed a need for more emergency training. Post-graduation training, prior defibrillator use, and having an emergency kit were linked to better emergency management capabilities in the logistic regressions.
ConclusionThe findings of this study indicate significant gaps in emergency preparedness among Lebanese dentists. Many lack adequate training and resources, highlighting the urgent need for further training and better-equipped clinics. Strengthening policy frameworks and resource allocation is crucial.
Clinical relevanceStakeholders should prioritize implementing mandatory training programs and developing clear guidelines to ensure that dental clinics in Lebanon are adequately prepared to manage health-related emergencies effectively.

## Introduction

Various health-related emergencies, primarily triggered by underlying medical conditions and complications associated with dental procedures,1 as well as patient apprehension and anxiety,2 may arise within dental clinics. These emergencies can range from simple to complex situations, including vasovagal syndrome, hypoglycemia, allergic reactions, syncope, seizures, myocardial infarctions, sudden cardiac arrest, and more.1,3 Furthermore, these situations can manifest in a variety of clinical presentations, each exhibiting distinct signs and symptoms that require immediate and appropriate intervention. Therefore, it's imperative for dentists, who serve as the primary healthcare providers in their dental clinics, along with any clinical staff they may have, to be thoroughly prepared in identifying and handling these emergencies effectively to prevent potential serious complications.

The preparedness of the dentist and clinical staff for health-related emergencies should encompass several components. Firstly, it involves preventive measures, such as gathering comprehensive medical data from the patient, including medication history,4,5 as well as documenting any prior incidents within a dental clinic. Additionally, conducting vital sign assessments and physical examinations can help identify any hidden conditions that may put the patient at risk.5 The second component of preparedness involves developing a medical emergency response plan, commonly referred to as an emergency code, which involves the clinic team and clearly defines roles and responsibilities of the clinic staff.4 The third component focuses on the expertise of the dentists and clinic staff in managing emergency situations.4 It is essential for both dentists and the clinic staff to possess the necessary skills and knowledge to recognize and handle medical emergencies. Therefore, dentists and staff need to undergo primary emergency care training, including simulations and hands-on practice. The fourth element of preparedness involves appropriately equipping dental clinics to provide first-aid interventions ensuring the availability of essential medications and equipment, such as epinephrine, nitroglycerin, benzodiazepines, oxygen tanks, defibrillators, and more.4

Various studies identified in the literature assessed the occurrence of medical emergencies in dental clinics, as well as dentists' confidence in managing such emergencies. Studies conducted in the eastern Mediterranean region indicate that a considerable proportion of dentists have encountered such emergencies, ranging from 67.0% in Saudi Arabia6 to 87.8% in Jordan.7 The studies also underscored dentists' limited confidence in managing medical emergencies and their requirement for further training. In fact, evidence from the study conducted in Saudi Arabia suggests that one-third of dentists lacked confidence in using emergency drugs, and only 45.0% were proficient in performing cardio-pulmonary resuscitation.6 As for the Jordanian study, 86.7% of dentists expressed the need for further training to handle medical emergencies, and most participants lacked confidence in managing such situations.7 Another study, conducted by Varoni et al. in Italy, also revealed an average level of satisfaction among dentists regarding their capability to identify and manage medical emergencies.2

In the Lebanese context, no previous studies have been conducted to assess dentists' preparedness in managing medical emergencies within the clinic setting. Additionally, it is worth noting that there are currently no regulations mandating first aid training for dentists or dental clinic staff in Lebanon. Nevertheless, it is important to highlight that first aid training is integrated into the academic curriculum for dentists at Lebanese universities. In fact, the three universities teaching dentistry in Lebanon offer basic life support training to their students as follows:1 The training department at the Lebanese Red Cross provides a 7-hour basic life support training to 4th-year students at the Faculty of Dental Medicine at the Lebanese University;2 a certified first aid trainer—who is also an instructor at the faculty- provides a 14-hour course on basic life support for Faculty of Dental Medicine students at Saint-Joseph University of Beirut; and3 The Department of Nursing at the Health Sciences Faculty provides a 14-hour course on basic life support training to the Faculty of Dentistry students at Beirut Arab University. However, the training provided by these universities is limited to basic life support with no advanced training available. This pioneering study was therefore conducted to assess the preparedness of Lebanese dentists in handling medical emergencies, marking the first of its kind in the field. The study also investigated dentists' past experiences with medical emergencies, the previous training they have received as well as the preparedness of dental clinics in terms of equipment and medications for the management of such situations. Lastly, the study explored factors affecting dentists' capabilities in handling different types of medical emergencies.

## Methods

### Study design

This study is a cross-sectional national survey conducted among Lebanese dentists using self-administered questionnaires. The study commenced in June 2023, with data collection occurring from August to October 2024.

### Recruitment of participants

The targeted population for this study comprised Lebanese dentists actively practicing in Lebanon and registered with either of the two Lebanese Dental Associations (LDA) located in Beirut or Tripoli. The minimum required sample size was determined using StatCalc followed by population survey options in the Epi Info software. Assuming a population size of 7,000 (as per the LDA), an expected frequency of 61.1%,7 confidence limits of 95.0%, and a type I error (α) of 5.0%, the final calculated sample size was 347.

A total of 442 dentists (n = 442) completed the survey, and their demographics in terms of gender and age distribution were compared to the gender and age distribution of Lebanese dentists as per the figures shared by the Lebanese Dental Associations of Beirut and Tripoli (see supplementary material) to check for response bias and verify the representativeness of the sample.

### Data collection tool

The data was gathered via an online self-administered questionnaire developed based on a tool that was originally used in a study evaluating the readiness of dentists and dental clinics for medical emergencies in Jordan.7 The tool encompassed several sections including:1.Participant characteristics: This section includes variables such as gender (qualitative dichotomous) and age (qualitative ordinal) of the participants.2.Professional characteristics: This section covers variables related to specialization (qualitative nominal), years of experience (qualitative ordinal), governorate(s) of practice (qualitative nominal), and practice setting(s) (qualitative nominal).3.Medical emergency experience: This section encompasses variables regarding experiences with medical emergencies, such as minor medical problems faced in clinic including vasovagal syncope, hypoglycemia, and orthostatic hypotension (qualitative dichotomous), major medical emergencies faced in clinic including asthma attack, seizures, allergies, inhalation of foreign bodies, and acute coronary syndrome (qualitative dichotomous), cardiac arrest situations faced in clinic (qualitative dichotomous), use of oxygen supply/source (qualitative dichotomous), use of defibrillator (qualitative dichotomous), use of blood pressure monitor (qualitative dichotomous), use of emergency drugs (qualitative nominal), whether a medical emergency was encountered in the clinic in the last twelve months (qualitative dichotomous), and type(s) of medical emergency faced (qualitative nominal).4.Availability of emergency equipment: This section includes variables such as emergency kit availability (qualitative dichotomous), contents of emergency kit (qualitative nominal), duration since the last oxygen tank control (qualitative ordinal), type of defibrillator, if any (qualitative nominal), and emergency drugs available in the clinic (qualitative nominal).5.Training: This section focuses on training-related variables, including whether training in medical emergencies was part of the university curriculum (qualitative dichotomous), training in medical emergencies after graduation (qualitative dichotomous), type of training (qualitative nominal), recency of the most recent training in medical emergencies (qualitative ordinal), and the need for training in medical emergencies (qualitative dichotomous).6.Capabilities: This section assesses the participants' capabilities in handling various medical conditions through seven Likert scale questions.

Additional questions were added to the questionnaire to evaluate the presence of an emergency response plan and the training received by clinic staff other than the dentist, if applicable: training of additional staff in medical emergencies (qualitative dichotomous), presence of an emergency medical response plan involving clinic staff (qualitative dichotomous), and frequency of emergency medical response plan simulation (qualitative ordinal).

The questionnaire, originally developed in English, was translated into French by the researchers to ensure understanding among all practicing dentists in Lebanon. It was then back translated into English to ensure accuracy. After translation, the questionnaire was converted into a Google Form, providing both the English and French versions along with a brief introductory paragraph stating the study's aim, the anonymity of collected data, the confidentiality of data storage and handling processes, as well as the reference to ethical approval obtained. The tool was pilot tested with five dentists to ensure proper data collection procedures and comprehensiveness of all questions. After pilot testing, the Likert scale questions were converted into single questions rather than being presented in a table format for better visualization.

### Data collection process

The link providing access to the questionnaire was sent via WhatsApp to all dentists by the Lebanese Dental Associations of Beirut and Tripoli, utilizing their databases of phone numbers. Four reminders were subsequently sent, each with a two-week interval, to achieve the targeted sample size. Data collection was conducted anonymously, as received responses did not contain any identifiers revealing the respondent's identity (such as phone number or email address). Furthermore, the data was treated confidentially, with only researchers having access to it. The data was securely stored on a password-protected Excel sheet saved on a drive. Upon publication of the manuscript, the dataset will be permanently deleted.

### Ethical considerations

The study received approval from the Saint-Joseph University of Beirut ethics’ committee (reference number: USJ-2023-229).

### Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 software was utilized for statistical analysis. All variables, being categorical, were described using frequencies and percentages. Subsequently, variables pertaining to dentists' perceptions of their capacities in treating minor or major medical problems, as well as managing a cardiac arrest, were dichotomized as follow: “Fully capable” and “Capable enough” were considered as “capable,” while “Not capable enough” and “Not capable at all” were considered as “Not Capable.” Following this step, researchers explored potential associations using the Chi-square or Fisher tests between capabilities in treating medical emergencies (including minor medical emergencies, major medical emergencies and cardiac arrests) and variables reflecting the level of preparedness of dentists, their experience with medical emergencies, and their training status. A P-value of less than .05 was considered statistically significant.

Variables that exhibited a statistically significant association with the outcome variables (capabilities to handle minor medical emergencies, major medical emergencies and cardiac arrests) in the bivariate analysis were subsequently included in bivariate logistic regressions. Considering the sample size, which did not permit the inclusion of all covariates showing significant associations with the outcomes of interest in one regression, multiple logistic regressions were conducted for each outcome. Each regression incorporated covariates exhibiting significant association with the outcome and related to one of the three dimensions believed to impact the capacity of dentists (i.e., the level of preparedness of dentists, their experience with medical emergencies, and their training status). It was ensured that each variable included in the logistic regression had a minimum of 20 individuals for robust analysis. Following the execution of various logistic regressions, variables that significantly influenced the capacity of dentists were integrated into the final model, as discussed in the article.

### Medical emergency preparedness

Among the participating dentists, 38.2% (n = 169) reported possessing an emergency kit in their clinic, 88.0% (n = 389) confirmed the availability of at least one emergency equipment item, and 42.8% mentioned having at least one emergency drug (n = 189). Notably, only 5.9% of participating dentists reported having a defibrillator in their clinics (n = 26). Regarding training in medical emergencies, 71.3% of dentists (n = 315) reported having received training on the management of medical emergencies as part of their university curriculum and 39.6% (n = 175) stated having been trained after graduation. As for the most recent training received, only 7.9% (n = 35) received training in the past year while 20.6% have been trained since more than a year and less than 4 years (n = 91) and 43.0% have been trained since more than 4 years (n = 190). However, 28.5% (n = 126) declared having never received a training on the management of medical emergencies. As for the previous use of a defibrillator, only 12.4% of dentists (n = 55) reported having already used a defibrillator (not necessarily in their clinic).

Also, with regards to preparedness, out of the 341 dentists working with other staff in their dental practice, 18.8% (n = 64) testified that the other staff members have been trained on the management of medical emergencies. Sixteen-point-one percent (16.1%; n = 55) informed that they have a medical emergency response plan in place and 50.0% of them (n = 28) simulate it at least once a year, 28.6% less than once a year (n = 15) whereas, 21.4% never simulate it (n = 12).

## Discussion

This pioneering cross-sectional study conducted in Lebanon to evaluate dentists' preparedness in managing medical emergencies within their clinics has uncovered compelling insights regarding the insufficient preparedness in dealing with such situations, both in terms of equipment and training. Furthermore, this study revealed dentists' low levels of confidence in their capabilities to manage major medical emergencies within their practices. Notably, over 80.0% of surveyed dentists expressed the urgent need for additional training to enhance their capability in managing medical emergencies.

Regarding the participating dentists' experience with medical emergencies, approximately 75.0% reported encountering minor medical problems, while only 22.4% had experienced major medical emergencies. These findings align with the frequency of emergencies reported in dental clinics as observed by Abu Al Ghanam et al. in Jordan,7 suggesting that medical emergencies in dental settings are predominantly minor in nature. The most reported medical emergencies included vasovagal reactions, hypoglycemia, and orthostatic hypotension. These results are consistent with evidence from other countries such as Italy2 and Poland.8

Any medical emergency, even a minor one, requests an immediate response. Therefore, dentists must be adequately equipped to manage such situations by emphasizing the importance of possessing emergency kits and drugs, as well as undergoing corresponding training to enhance their overall preparedness. Despite the importance of dentist preparedness, the situation in Lebanon highlights a limited preparedness among dentists. In fact, this study revealed that only 38.2% of participating dentists have an emergency kit in their clinic with the blood pressure meter and the pulse oximeter being the most commonly available equipment. In addition, 42.8% have at least one emergency drug in their clinic with epinephrine and steroids being respectively the most abundant these drugs. On the other hand, the current study indicates a higher level of dentist preparedness in terms of training, with 71.3% of respondents confirmed undergoing an emergency response training as part of their dental education. This finding surpasses training prevalence in Jordan, where only 27.2% of dentists received an emergency response training during their education period.7 Nevertheless, due to the absence of mandatory postgraduate training requirement in Lebanon, there is a decline in the proportion of dentist who have received an emergency response training in recent years; where only 7.9% of dentist reported receiving a training in the last year and 20.6% confirmed receiving a training within the period ranging from one to four years ago. This outcome deviates from the recommendations of the American Heart Association (AHA) and the European Resuscitation Council (ERC) which advocate for an optimal yearly Basic Life Support (BLS) training for dentists.7,8 Preparedness figures in this study falls below those observed in countries such as Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates (UAE), where the possession of a medical emergency kit in a dental clinic and successfully completing a BLS is mandatory and regulated. For instance, these regulations in Saudi Arabia resulted in 78.0% of Saudi dentist possessing a medical emergency kit and 95.0% completion rate of the BLS course.9 Similarly, a pilot study in the UAE revealed that 74.0% of the dental clinics are equipped with emergency kits, in addition, within the last two years, 75.0% of general dental practitioners and 91.0% of specialists have undergone a BLS training.10

The lack of a regular BLS training is translated into the dentists’ insufficient capabilities to manage certain medical emergencies and perform specific procedures. While nearly 25.0% of surveyed dentists exhibit full capability in handling minor medical conditions such as vasovagal reaction, orthostatic hypotension, and hypoglycemia in their clinics, this proportion decreases when confronted with a major medical emergency such as asthma, seizure allergies, with only around 5.0% confirmed capability in handling such situation. This percentage further drops to nearly 1.0% when dentists are confronted with a cardiac arrest. These findings are consistent with studies conducted in various countries.2,7,8,9,10 Low capabilities were also noted in managing complicated procedures such as applying an oxygen mask, performing intravascular injections, and inserting a pharyngeal tube.

As for the factors influencing dentists' capabilities to manage medical emergencies in their clinics, the presence of an emergency kit, prior experience with medical conditions, and prior use of a defibrillator were found to significantly enhance dentists' capabilities to manage various types of medical situations, including minor medical problems, major emergencies, and cardiac arrests. Additionally, receiving postgraduate training in managing medical emergencies was associated with increased capabilities in handling both minor and major medical emergencies. Importantly, a significant association emerged between dentists' perceived requirement for training in medical emergency management and their capabilities in dealing with such situations. Dentists who demonstrated lower capabilities in managing major medical emergencies and cardiac arrests clearly articulated the necessity for further training in managing medical emergencies.

The preparedness to address medical emergencies in dental clinic settings extends beyond dentists alone and includes the preparedness of the entire clinic staff. Of the surveyed dentists, 77.1% declared collaborating with non-dentist staff members. Among those collaborating with non-dentist staff, only 18.8% confirmed that their staff had undergone training in medical emergencies management and, 16.1% declared the implementation of a medical emergency response plan, with half of them stimulating the plan within the past year. These proportion are lower compared to the findings of a study in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, in which 77.0% of Saudi dentists have reported that all their staff hold a valid BLS certification.5 The higher proportion in Saudi Arabia is mainly attributed to the country's mandate.

These findings underscore the importance of informing relevant stakeholders, including the Ministry of Public Health, LDAs and faculties of dentistry about the imperative for swift action to improve preparedness through enhanced policies. Furthermore, the researchers outline several key recommendations to improve emergency preparedness and management in dental clinics. Firstly, we propose enhancing the design and implementation of medical emergency management courses and training within dental programs offered by faculties of dental medicine. Secondly, we suggest making the provision of essential emergency equipment and drugs mandatory in all dental clinics. Thirdly, we recommend implementing compulsory annual training programs through the LDA and in collaboration with the training department at the Lebanese Red Cross (LRC). To ensure dentists' compliance with these training requirements, we suggest integrating them into the process of renewing registration. This approach would incentivize dentists to participate in training sessions to continue benefiting from the association's resources. Lastly, the researchers suggest that beyond implementing these recommendations, endorsing them through legal frameworks—including policies and laws at the level of the Ministry of Public Health—making regular training of Lebanese practicing dentists and their staff mandatory, as well as clearly delineating guidelines for optimum preparedness of dental clinics to properly manage health-related emergencies that could occur in the clinic. The researchers believe these steps are crucial for improving morbidity and mortality rates associated with health-related emergencies in dental settings.

This study, being the first of its kind to delve into the field of preparedness for medical emergencies among Lebanese dentists, also has a notable strength in providing representative results, as participating dentists span across all age categories and thus encompass varying levels of experience. Moreover, the active involvement of the Lebanese dental associations from the study's inception, including validation of protocols and dissemination of surveys, was anticipated to enhance stakeholders' ownership and interest in the findings. However, the study also comes with certain limitations. For instance, while it evaluates dentists' confidence in their capabilities to handle medical emergencies, it does not assess their knowledge levels regarding the proper management of such emergencies. Additionally, the assessment of equipment and drug availability, as well as training, relies on dentists' self-reporting, which may be influenced by social desirability bias, potentially leading to an overestimation of preparedness. Finally, a response bias has been identified, with significantly different gender and age distributions in the sample compared to the population of Lebanese dentists, as reflected by the figures shared by the LDAs of Beirut and Tripoli. The sample shows an overrepresentation of females and an underrepresentation of dentists aged above 60 years. This age-related difference could be explained by the nature of the data collection process using online Google forms (see supplementary material).

## Conclusion

The national cross-sectional study assessed the preparedness of dental clinics in Lebanon to manage medical emergencies, focusing on human resources training, capabilities and the availability of essential equipment and drugs. The findings revealed worrisome levels of inadequate preparedness, as most dentists reported limited capabilities in managing emergencies and expressed a clear need for further training. The study underscores the importance of mandating dentists to be adequately prepared to handle medical emergencies while awaiting emergency medical services. The researchers stress that stakeholders should utilize these findings to reinforce policy frameworks and allocate the necessary resources to enhance preparedness. This would not only reduce morbidity and mortality rates within dental clinics but also in wider contexts.

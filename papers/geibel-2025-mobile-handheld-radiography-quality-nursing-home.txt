1 Introduction

Worldwide, 30% of people in need of care require support for their oral health; 60% of people in need of care are no longer able to visit a dental practice. Demographic developments mean a further increase in the number of people requiring treatment at home or in retirement/nursing homes is expected. Oral health care for these patients could be improved using domiciliary dental care, where dental treatment is carried out on site. Radiography is required in mobile dentistry to ensure accurate diagnosis. In Germany, radiography is covered by dental care when clinical examination cannot provide a sufficient diagnosis, but it can be difficult or impossible to apply conventional dental radiography to immobile, seriously ill, or disabled patients. A mobile dental X-ray unit can enable comprehensive dental diagnostics in difficult environments such as multi-bed retirement/nursing homes.

Currently available handheld X-ray devices developed for intraoral applications include the Nomad Pro2 (Dexis/KaVo), widely used in the US, Netherlands, and Sweden. Regarding radiation exposure, a statistically significant increase in cancer rates has not been detected in populations exposed to doses below 0.05 Sv. International studies conclude effective dose in mobile radiology is comparable to (or lower than) wall-mounted devices, and operator exposure with CE-certified portable devices, rectangular collimation, and backscatter radiation shields stays within dose limits when technique keeps the beam parallel to the ground (Hoogeveen & Berkhout, 2022).

Authorisation requirements for mobile X-ray devices differ significantly between countries; available devices are not yet approved in Germany, partly due to a lack of quality studies. The objective of this study was to evaluate image quality and dental benefit achievable with a portable mobile dental X-ray machine for non-mobile patients in routine clinical practice.

2 Methods

127 dental single-tooth radiographs taken with the Nomad Pro 2 were evaluated. All images were taken by one experienced dentist familiar with the device, in a retirement/nursing home setting during routine dental care. Written informed consent was obtained (or from legal guardian/next of kin); images were anonymised, with a key file accessible only to the treating dentist. Images were assessed by three dentists with differing specialisations/experience (one oral and maxillofacial surgery specialist, one oral surgery specialist, one second-year conservative dentistry trainee) to investigate the influence of professional experience on findings.

Indications for imaging were root remnants (surgery), caries examination, periodontitis examination, and implantology. Assessment followed the standard approach and quality criteria catalogue of the German Federal Dental Association, evaluating: central beam centring, phosphor storage plate (PSP) quality defects, foreign bodies/overlaps in the beam path, and sufficiency of contrast/sharpness. Osseous structures assessed for treatment relevance included the alveolar border, interradicular septum, periodontal ligament (PDL) assessability, and periapical bone structure.

The Nomad Pro 2 operates at 60 kV and 2.5 mA. Per German Dental Association specifications, standard dental X-ray diagnostics require imaging voltage >=60 kV, resolution >=5 line pairs/mm, and focus-to-tube-end distance >=200 mm. Average effective dose is ~0.005 mSv (analogue) / ~0.003 mSv (digital) for dental radiographs. Results were compared descriptively against a published 2012 study of wall-mounted X-ray devices at a university hospital.

3 Results

Of 127 dental radiographs, 101 (80%) showed no qualitative limitations. The periradicular/periapical bone structure was diagnostic in 109 (86%) radiographs; in fewer than 4%, bony structures (alveolar ridge, dentin-enamel junction, pulp chamber, etc.) could not be assessed with sufficient accuracy. Field of view sufficiently covered the justifying indication in 107 (84%); in 20 (16%), the image section was not completely adequate. Secondary/incidental findings were noted in 78 (61%) of radiographs.

Recorded defect types (% of found defects) included: periradicular/periapical bone structure assessability issues (14%), image section incompleteness (20%), image section non-compliance with justifying indication (16%), and overall "quality defects relevant for assessment" (84% of the subset of flagged images), secondary/incidental findings (39%). Among specific defects across all 127 images: overexposed radiograph 9% (n=11), superimposition 3% (n=4), PSP not aligned occlusally 43% (n=54), PSP quality (scratches/folds/wear) 47% (n=60), X-ray beam center not centred 28% (n=36), motion artefacts 4% (n=5), foreign-body superimposition 0%, and radiograph angulation too steep 1% (n=1).

4 Discussion

107 of 127 radiographs (83%, anterior/premolar/molar regions combined) showed no diagnostically relevant quality defects - matching quality standards published by other authors under inpatient conditions. Quality criteria per German Federal Dental Association guidelines (crown-to-apex visibility, periodontal bone structure, interradicular septum) were met in over 96% of radiographs, better than the 83% reported in normal (non-mobile) environments. Field of view corresponded to the justifying indication in 84% of radiographs, comparable to 86% in normal environments. Overall, the mobile handheld unit achieved comparable results to a wall-mounted intraoral X-ray unit, consistent with other work groups' findings on handheld device advantages/disadvantages.

16% of images showed qualitative defects under mobile conditions - the same range as stationary conditions (17%) reported elsewhere; these could be further reduced via direct digital detectors or stricter PSP quality control. Motion artefacts occurred in only 4% of cases, an acceptable result given the anticipated difficulty of radiography in domiciliary care with limited-compliance patients; no evidence of additional motion artefacts from handheld use was found compared to wall-mounted units, and the handheld device's shorter exposure time (0.12 s anterior / 0.16 s molar vs. 0.25 s / 0.32 s for a stationary unit) may even reduce motion-artefact risk.

This was not a direct head-to-head comparison between handheld and wall-mounted units on the same patients; a comparator study (Lommen et al.) reported significantly better perpendicularity for the handheld Nomad Pro 2, while other studies of bitewing/molar-premolar images found no significant quality differences between Nomad Pro 2 and a Heliodent Plus wall-mounted unit. Operator training/experience is noted (per Nietschke et al.) as crucial for image quality; all 127 radiographs here were taken by the same experienced dentist to control this variable, while a multi-rater grading system controlled for evaluator-experience bias.

Treatment-relevant unexpected secondary/incidental findings (e.g., apical osteolysis, root remnants) were observed in 61% of cases, highlighting the diagnostic value of enabling radiography at the bedside for patients with limited compliance in demanding environments such as nursing homes. Surgical procedures are legally contestable without dental radiology in this setting, raising professional and ethical concerns that mobile radiology addresses.

The study concludes that portable X-ray machines can meet quality requirements for intraoral X-ray images and are comparable to conventional wall-mounted dental X-ray machines in terms of image quality, supporting their role in domiciliary/mobile dental care.

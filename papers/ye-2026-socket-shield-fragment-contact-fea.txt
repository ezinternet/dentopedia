Title: Biomechanical Effects of Implant-Root Fragment Contact in the Socket Shield Technique: A Finite Element Analysis

Introduction

Tooth extraction inevitably leads to dimensional changes in the alveolar ridge, particularly on the buccal aspect, which poses a significant challenge for aesthetic rehabilitation in the anterior maxilla. The socket shield technique (SST) involves preserving a portion of the root – typically the buccal segment, termed the ‘shield’ – following tooth extraction, with the goal of minimizing post-extraction remodelling of the alveolar ridge and supporting soft tissue contour.

In recent years, SST has demonstrated promising clinical outcomes in preserving ridge dimensions and significantly improved the pink aesthetic score compared to traditional immediate implant placement.,,,However, the biomechanical stability of direct contact between the shield fragment and the implant remains insufficiently studied. The ‘jumping gap’ in SST refers to the distance between the implant and the buccal root fragment. Clinically, the criteria for this gap vary, and there is no consensus exists on its optimal size.Dumitra and Nicolae found that a jumping gap of 0.5 to 1 mm allowed for direct implantation, while gaps larger than 1 mm required bone grafting.Ideally, maintaining minimal gaps is optimal. However, in the aesthetic anterior tooth region, where the bone plate is extremely thin, or during immediate implant cavity preparation, local direct contact between the implant threads and the retained tooth root fragments is an unavoidable clinical reality due to anatomical constraints or procedural errors. The original proof-of-principle by Hürzeler et aldemonstrated that implants placed in direct contact with the root fragment could achieve osseointegration, with new cementum formation observed on the implant surface. However, clinical reports by Nevins et alhighlight the long-term risks, showing that retained root fragments can become infected, leading to peri-implantitis and implant failure, and suggesting that retained fragments can harbour calculus and bacteria, implying that shield thickness and morphology directly influence long-term hygiene. This contradiction necessitates a deeper investigation into the specific conditions under which direct contact is biomechanically favourable.

SST is technique-sensitive. Improper fragment preparation or excessive stress may lead to fragment displacement, fracture, or infection, and cause failure ultimately. The preparation method and morphology of the shield fragment require ongoing exploration. Currently, no unified standards exist for fragment preparation in cases of direct implant contact. While fragment thickness (1-1.5 mm) has been extensively studied, research on fragment size classification is scarce.,,Finite element analysis (FEA), as a numerical simulation method, has been widely used in dental implantology to evaluate stress distribution and deformation in peri-implant bone and soft tissues under different designs, surgical techniques, and materials. By constructing three-dimensional models, FEA can accurately simulate biomechanical conditions in clinical settings, predicting the potential effects of various designs.

This study utilized FEA to investigate how the labial arc angle of the shield influences stress distribution in the fragment, surrounding bone, and adjacent periodontal ligament (PDL) under simulated insertion and occlusal loading. We hypothesized that an intermediate arc angle would minimize stress concentrations in both the shield and cortical bone.

Material and methods

Maxillary bone and implant models were constructed: Based on a previously established finite element model of maxillary SST implants,cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) data from a periodontally healthy adult with no alveolar bone resorption were used. The scan was acquired using a Kava PP3-1 CBCT unit (Kava Dental GmbH). The slice thickness was 0.125 mm, and the voxel resolution (isotropic voxel size) was 0.125 mm × 0.125 mm × 0.125 mm. The Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine files were imported into Mimics (version 21.0; Materialise). Bone and tooth structures were segmented using greyscale thresholding, followed by region growing and manual refinement. Specifically, the initial threshold for the overall osseous structure was set greyscale values of 400 to 5000, and the tooth was segmented at greyscale values of 1500 to 5000. The cortical and cancellous bone were further separated by applying different greyscale thresholds and region-growing operations: cortical bone was defined using higher greyscale values of 1000 to 5000, whereas cancellous bone was defined using lower greyscale values of 400 to 1000. After segmentation, the masks were smoothed using smoothing method and iterations and exported as STL files for subsequent model processing. A Nobel implant (3.5 mm in diameter × 11.5 mm in body length) with a threaded tapered design was modelled. The thread geometry was defined with a pitch of 1.25 mm, a thread profile (cross-section) angle of 60°, and a helix (lead) angle of 6.5°. The abutment length and diameter were set to 6.0 and 4.8 mm, respectively. A natural tooth-shaped crown was generated based on the anatomical morphology of the maxillary central incisor. A natural tooth-shaped crown was generated from the maxillary central incisor anatomy. The coronal portion of the tooth model was extracted, and a Boolean operation with the implant/abutment geometry was performed to create the definitive restoration with an internal fitting surface. The crown dimensions were standardized (crown height 10.96 mm, mesio-distal width 7.43 mm, labio-palatal thickness 6.67 mm).

Residual tooth fragment models were set: The patient’s maxillary central incisor root was modelled in Mimics and imported into 3-Matic for fan-shaped cutting. According to the standardized parameters used in recent clinical literature, the C-shaped shield measures 1.0 to 1.5 mm thickness in size.Meanwhile, it is generally considered that a root fragment within the 6 to 8 mm length provides better predictability in postoperative outcomes.Given that this study employed a root shield design with direct contact, a 1.5 mm thickness and 6 mm length C-shaped shield was selected. Fragment models with labial arc angles () of 60°, 90°, 120°, 150°, and 180° were created. The fragments were in direct contact with the implant, and the contact surface matched the implant thread morphology. The boundary constraints and the location/direction of load application are shown in. For both loading scenarios, the superior region of the maxillary bone was fixed as the boundary constraint. During simulated implant placement, the root fragment (socket shield) was maintained in direct contact with the implant surface. An insertion torque of 30 N·cm was applied to replicate the clinical screw-in phase, which is widely considered adequate to achieve primary stability for immediate loading protocols.In this torque-loading scenario, the implant thread–bone and thread–fragment interfaces were defined as frictional contact with a coefficient of friction= 0.3.To simulate functional occlusion after osseointegration, a 100-N static load was applied at the lingual fossa of the maxillary central incisor crown, oriented along the long axis of the opposing mandibular incisor. The load was implemented as a concentrated force applied to a reference point and distributed to the lingual fossa surface region via coupling (coupled occlusal surface) to avoid a single-node point-load artefact. Although physiological occlusal forces on a single anterior tooth typically range from 20 to 50 N during normal function, a 100-N load represents a conservative yet clinically accepted upper-bound condition commonly employed in finite element analyses to evaluate biomechanical behaviour under worst-case functional scenarios.In this post-osseointegration occlusal-loading scenario, complete osseointegration was assumed, and the implant, root fragment, PDL, and surrounding bone were bonded using shared nodes.

All materials were assumed homogeneous, continuous, and linearly elastic.The material properties and element/node numbers for each component are listed in. Finite element meshing was performed using HyperMesh 11.0 (Altair Company) with 4-node linear tetrahedral elements (C3D4), using 4-node linear tetrahedral elements (C3D4). A non-uniform mesh strategy was adopted. The cortical bone in regions distant from the implant was meshed with an element size of 0.45 mm, whereas the implant head, crown, cancellous bone, Bio-Oss, and other non-critical regions were meshed with an element size of 0.3 mm. The implant beneath the crown, the bone adjacent to the implant, the root fragment, and the PDL were locally refined to 0.125 mm.

Mesh convergence analysis was performed using the 60° fragment model under both oblique loading and insertion torque conditions in Abaqus 6.14 (Dassault Systèmes). Three mesh sizes (0.25, 0.125, and 0.065 mm) were tested. Convergence was assessed based on the maximum von Mises stress in the implant and the consistency of the peak-stress location, which was located at or near the implant–bone interface. A difference of less than 5% between two consecutive mesh refinements was considered acceptable. Under oblique loading, the total numbers of elements/nodes were 691,132/122,066, 816,743/143,144, and 1011,223/176,061 for the 0.25, 0.125, and 0.065 mm meshes, respectively, with corresponding maximum von Mises stresses of 110.8, 117.5, and 115.8 MPa. Under torque loading, the corresponding element/node numbers were 552,554/101,534, 638,134/117,096, and 756,683/137,950, with corresponding maximum von Mises stresses of 92.50, 98.50, and 98.87 MPa. Compared with the 0.125 mm mesh, the difference for the 0.065 mm mesh was 1.45% under oblique loading and 0.37% under torque loading, while the location of peak stress remained unchanged. Therefore, the 0.125 mm mesh was selected for subsequent analyses as it provided an appropriate balance between accuracy and computational efficiency.

Outcome measures included stress distribution, peak von Mises stress, and displacement in the PDL, root fragment, implant, cortical bone, and cancellous bone, as evaluated under the relevant loading conditions (insertion torque and occlusal loading).

Results

The biomechanical analysis under torque loading condition was shown in: The high-stress regions in PDL were predominantly concentrated in the mid-upper and marginal areas, corresponding to the stress distribution in the root fragment. The root fragment exhibited peak stress in its mid-upper region, particularly at the contact zone with the implant neck’s maximum diameter. Similarly, the implant’s high-stress areas were localized at the buccal neck region where it interfaced with the fragment.

For the PDL, the 60° fragment displayed the highest peak stress (4.073 MPa), occurring at the upper marginal zone of the buccal PDL. In contrast, the 180° fragment showed the lowest peak stress (1.067 MPa), also located in the upper buccal PDL, representing a 73.8% reduction relative to the 60° model. The stress trends in the fragment mirrored those in the PDL: the 60° fragment experienced the highest peak stress (101.9 MPa), which decreased significantly to 75.48 MPa for the 90° fragment (25.9% lower than 60°), and further declined to 69.62 MPa (31.7% lower), 67.14 MPa (34.1% lower), and 42.43 MPa (58.4% lower) for the 120°,150°, and 180° fragments, respectively. All stress peaks were observed at the fragment region opposing the implant neck’s widest threaded portion.

Across all models, the implant neck sustained relatively consistent peak stresses, ranging from 98.5 to 112.6 MPa. Under torque loading, fragment displacement decreased as the arc angle increased. The root fragment itself showed high stress concentrations in its mid-upper portion (the area adjacent to the implant neck). The implant demonstrated maximum stress at the cervical region, with greater stress on the buccal side than the lingual side. At the implant-bone interface, stress was mainly distributed in the cortical bone around the implant neck and the cancellous bone near the implant apex. For the PDL, the fragment with a 60° showed the lowest peak stress (1.143 MPa) at the marginal area of the upper buccal PDL. As the fragment arc increased, the stress peak location remained consistent, but the magnitude increased, reaching 1.996 MPa for the 180° arc fragment, which was a 74.6% increase relative to 60°. In contrast to the PDL, the fragment’s stress pattern showed an opposite trend. The 60° fragment experienced the highest peak stress (87.40 MPa), which decreased significantly to 72.05 MPa for the 90° fragment (17.6% lower than 60°). The stress reduction became more gradual for larger arcs: 63.55 MPa (27.3% lower), 62.20 MPa (28.8% lower), and 61.57 MPa (29.6% lower) for the 120°, 150°, and 180° fragments. All stress peaks were located at the lingual cervical margin of the fragments. As the fragment arc increased, the peak stress at the implant neck decreased from 117.5 to 106.6 MPa, corresponding to a 9.3% reduction. The maximum stress in the peri-implant cancellous bone showed minimal variation across models (6.695-8.811 MPa), with the peak located in the cancellous bone near the implant apex, representing a 31.6% difference between the lowest and highest values. For cortical bone, the 60° fragment showed a peak stress of 49.74 MPa. As the fragment arc increased to 90° and 120°, the peak stress decreased to 47.77 MPa (4.0% lower than 60°) and 47.62 MPa (4.3% lower), respectively. However, when the arc exceeded 120°, the peak stress increased to 50.83 MPa at 150° (2.2% higher than 60°) and rose further to 64.51 MPa at 180° (29.7% higher). These findings indicate a non-linear trend in cortical bone stress, with stress increasing markedly once the fragment arc exceeded 120°.

Discussion

The results showed that the stress concentration areas of the implant were mainly distributed at the implant neck. This pattern is consistent with the FEA by Zheng et al,who reported that stress concentration was mainly observed in the cortical bone at the crest of the alveolar ridge, with principal stresses centred around the implant neck and implant von Mises stresses primarily located near the crestal cortical bone. Similarly, Elsheikh et alfound that peak stresses at the implant–bone interface were concentrated in the coronal cortical region under static loading. In the present study, the stress concentration at the implant–bone interface was primarily located in the crestal cortical bone corresponding to the implant neck, and was also observed in the directly contacting residual root fragment. The stress in the PDL of the residual tooth fragment was concentrated at the buccal cervical region of the corresponding area, which was consistent with previous research findings.When the implant was in direct contact with the retained root fragment, the stress on the PDL decreased compared to that in natural teeth, while the stress on the root fragment increased significantly.Compared with our previous finite element model of the SST without direct implant–fragment contact (ie, a grafted gap was present), in which a 100-N load produced peak von Mises stresses of 100.5 MPa in the implant, 23.11 MPa in the cortical bone, 29.42 MPa in the retained fragment, and 1.131 MPa in the fragment PDL,the current study demonstrated markedly increased stress at the peri-implant bone interface, tooth fragment, PDL, and implant body. These findings indicate that direct implant-fragment contact may lead to a higher risk of postoperative complications, including potential implant loosening, fragment displacement, fracture, or detachment, thereby increasing the likelihood of implant failure.

Under a torque load of 30 N·cm, both the peak stress and displacement of the tooth fragment and PDL decreased as the fragment’s arc angle increased. This demonstrates that fragments with larger arc angles provide greater resistance during implant placement and exhibit superior stability. Conversely, smaller fragments showed higher susceptibility to displacement and potential detachment. Specifically, fragments with arc angles of 60° and 90° exhibited significantly greater displacements of 0.01651 and 0.01362 mm. Mechanistically, increasing the fragment arc enlarges the implant–fragment contact surface, which distributes the insertion torque more evenly across the fragment and PDL, thereby reducing local stress and limiting displacement. Under static occlusal loading conditions, tooth fragments with 60° and 90° arcs exhibited peak stresses of 101.9 and 75.48 MPa, respectively. Furthermore, under torque loading, these two groups demonstrated significantly higher stress values compared to the other three test groups. In the cortical bone surrounding the implant, when the tooth fragment arc was 60°, the peak stress reached 49.74 MPa. As the fragment arc increased, the peak stress initially decreased to 47.77 MPa at 90° and 47.62 MPa at 120°. However, with further increases in arc angle, the stress began to rise significantly, reaching 50.83 MPa at 150° and 64.51 MPa at 180°. Mechanistically, increasing the arc angle enlarges the implant–fragment contact region and promotes more uniform load sharing within the fragment, consistent with the monotonic reduction in fragment stress. However, at very large arcs (150°-180°), the interposed Bio-Oss/cancellous region between the fragment and the crestal cortical bone is reduced; under this geometry, circumferential constraint may increase, and the neck-region construct may become stiffer, which may contribute to higher stress concentration in the crestal cortical bone. Although the 180° model showed the highest cortical bone stress, this value should be interpreted as a theoretical mechanical reference under the present model assumptions, rather than a definitive prognostic threshold for bone fatigue or resorption. This stress increase may indicate a less favourable biomechanical trend in the cortical bone under the present static modelling conditions.In summary, a small grafted gap without direct implant–fragment contact is preferable under ideal conditions. However, limited local thread–fragment contact may be unavoidable in thin-buccal anterior sockets during immediate placement. In such cases, may represent a relatively favourable biomechanical range for both the implantation process and occlusal loading conditions.

Bäumer et alalso employed a direct contact approach between the implant and socket shield, observing new bone formation within the concavity prepared at the centre of the socket shield fragment. The actual challenge in clinical practice lies in the technique sensitivity of the socket shield procedure, which requires highly skilled surgical techniques and meticulous operation. This may increase surgical uncertainties and risks, potentially leading to early implant complications. The more common complications include bone resorption, exposure or displacement of the root shield fragment, and inadequate primary stability.Although the SST aims to reduce bone resorption, improper execution – such as suboptimal shield preparation or poor management of the gap between the shield and implant – may still cause bone resorption issues, particularly in the buccal bone plate area.In certain cases, the socket shield may not be completely covered by soft tissues, resulting in partial exposure. This not only compromises aesthetic outcomes but may also trigger inflammatory responses or other biological complications. Furthermore, improper management of the contact or gap between the socket shield and implant may lead to inadequate primary stability of the implant, thereby adversely affecting the healing process and ultimate success rate – ultimately negating the technique’s biomechanical advantages.

The preparation of residual root fragments presents a significant clinical challenge. Both in vitro and clinical studies have demonstrated that freehand implant placement achieves significantly lower precision compared to static guide-assisted surgical procedures.,,With the advent of three-dimensional imaging and implant design software, precision implantology has become achievable in clinical practice. The accurate transfer of virtual preoperative planning to actual implant positioning is critical for both protecting vital anatomical structures and achieving optimal aesthetic and functional restorative outcomes.The digital guide system utilizes CAD/CAM-fabricated surgical templates incorporating metal guide sleeves. These sleeves precisely control drill positioning and depth, enabling accurate root fragment preparation while minimizing manual errors.The system simultaneously restricts drill and implant movement, preventing buccal deviation that could generate excessive torque or pressure on the fragment and cause displacement. Furthermore, advances in biomaterials now allow application of matrix proteins and other osteogenic compounds to the fragment contact surface, effectively promoting bone formation.

This study has several limitations inherent to its FEA methodology. The models were constructed from a single maxillary CBCT dataset; therefore, anatomical variability (eg, sex, age, bone density, and ridge morphology) was not represented, which may limit the clinical generalizability of the conclusions. Simplifying bones into homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic materials fails to fully reflect their physiological anisotropy and heterogeneity. This simplification may affect absolute stress values and local stress distributions, making precise clinical predictions impossible. However, it can be scientifically interpreted as a comparative trend. In addition, the present findings should be interpreted as peak stress distributions under representative load magnitudes, rather than predictions of time-dependent responses or fatigue life. No definitive failure thresholds or long-term remodelling risk (eg, fracture, fatigue, or bone resorption) were evaluated in this study, and peak static stress should not be directly equated with clinical success or failure rates. Under cyclic loading, fatigue damage would depend on the stress amplitude, load ratio (mean stress), and number of cycles, and a dedicated fatigue assessment using material-specific S–N data with an appropriate mean-stress correction (eg, Goodman/Gerber/Soderberg) would be required for quantitative fatigue-risk evaluation. Nevertheless, since the boundary conditions and loading directions remain comparable, the locations of stress concentration and the relative trends of different implant-design scenarios are expected to be qualitatively consistent, although the absolute safety margins may differ. Finally, this study is purely computational and has not been validated by in vitro experiments, cadaver testing, or clinical imaging data.

Clinically, the procedure is more technically demanding than conventional implantation and has not yet gained widespread acceptance among practitioners. Therefore, additional research outcomes and technical refinements are required to determine whether root membrane-assisted immediate implantation can be widely adopted in clinical practice. To validate the simulation results presented in this study, future research should leverage digital medical technologies to construct precise tooth defect models, followed by clinical validation through longitudinal case follow-ups. In these subsequent investigations, soft tissue characteristics and pink and white aesthetics will be incorporated into the evaluation framework, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of peri-implant aesthetic outcomes.

Conclusion

Under direct implant-fragment contact, fragment size (particularly arc angle) significantly affects peri-implant tissues. The present static FEA suggests that an arc angle of 120° to 150° may represent a potentially favourable range in terms of peri-implant stress distribution, with reduced stress concentration, which may be beneficial for promoting tissue health under the modelled conditions. Further validation under dynamic/cyclic loading and clinical follow-up is needed.

Title: Regenerative Endodontic Procedures in Immature Permanent Teeth: Biological Mechanisms and Clinical Outcomes
Author: Abdullah Alqedairi (Division of Endodontics, Department of Restorative Dental Sciences, College of Dentistry, King Saud University, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia)
Journal: Cureus
DOI: 10.7759/cureus.109575
PMID: 42359182
PMCID: PMC13291709
Publication date: 2026-05-24

Abstract:
Necrotic immature permanent teeth with open apices pose a difficult management problem in clinical endodontics. Conventional apexification techniques resolve infection but do not support continued root development, leaving the tooth structurally vulnerable. Regenerative endodontic procedures (REPs) have emerged as a biologically oriented alternative that harnesses stem cells, scaffolds, and growth factors to form new tissue within the root canal space and encourage root maturation. This narrative review discusses the biological principles underlying REPs, the clinical protocols currently in use, the reported treatment outcomes, the key prognostic factors, and the limitations of current evidence. Overall, REPs demonstrate favorable rates of periapical healing and continued root development, with platelet-rich plasma and platelet-rich fibrin scaffolds showing advantages over blood clot alone for dentinal wall thickening. Pulp sensibility recovery is variable and does not reliably confirm true pulp regeneration. Current outcomes more closely reflect biologically supported repair than consistent, predictable pulp regeneration. Standardized protocols and longer-term follow-up studies are needed to fully establish the evidence base for these procedures.

Introduction and background

Immature permanent teeth with pulp necrosis and apical periodontitis are among the more demanding cases encountered in endodontic practice. An incompletely formed root has thin dentinal walls, a wide open apex, and a short root length, any of which can compromise structural integrity and make conventional endodontic treatment difficult or impossible. Without treatment, these teeth face a high probability of cervical fracture, failed apical seal, and eventual extraction. The challenge is compounded by the fact that these cases predominantly occur in children and adolescents, where tooth loss carries significant functional, esthetic, and psychological consequences over a lifetime.

For many years, apexification using calcium hydroxide or mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) was the accepted approach. Both methods can clear infection and create an apical stop, but neither supports further root development. The tooth remains non-vital with a structurally weak root. Long-term calcium hydroxide treatment also carries a recognized risk of increasing root fragility through its sustained high-pH effect on dentinal collagen. MTA apical plug techniques, while faster and more predictable, similarly leave the tooth unable to complete its development, maintaining thin dentinal walls susceptible to fracture and limiting the long-term prognosis of the tooth.

Regenerative endodontic procedures (REPs) developed from research into dental stem cell biology and tissue engineering. The procedure applies the principle that tissue formation depends on three components: a viable cell source, a suitable scaffold, and appropriate biological signals. Early case reports by Banchs and Trope in 2004 showed that thorough disinfection followed by induction of a blood clot into the canal could stimulate striking root development in young patients. Both the American Association of Endodontists (AAE) and the European Society of Endodontology (ESE) now recommend REPs as the treatment of choice for necrotic immature permanent teeth when patient factors allow.

Clinical evidence accumulated over two decades has been encouraging. Periapical healing rates of 85-94% have been consistently reported across diverse study populations and protocols. Continued root development (apical closure, root lengthening, dentinal wall thickening) is achieved in 74-81% of cases. Platelet-rich plasma and platelet-rich fibrin scaffolds have demonstrated superior dentinal wall thickening compared to blood clot alone in controlled trials. Pulp sensibility recovery is the least predictable outcome, reported in 25-70% of cases, and must be interpreted with caution as it does not reliably confirm true pulp tissue formation.

This narrative review was conducted through a literature review of PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science, covering publications from 2004 to 2025. Search terms included: regenerative endodontics, revascularization, immature permanent teeth, pulp regeneration, stem cells of the apical papilla, platelet-rich plasma, platelet-rich fibrin, and apexification. As this is a narrative rather than a systematic review, a formal PRISMA flowchart and risk-of-bias assessment were not conducted.

Review

Biological basis of regenerative endodontic procedures

Stem cell sources and the apical papilla: The apical papilla is a loose connective tissue at the apex of developing roots, carrying a high concentration of stem cells (SCAP - stem cells of the apical papilla). It tends to survive pulp necrosis because its location apical to the main foramen keeps it outside the main zone of bacterial contamination. SCAPs have a higher proliferative rate than dental pulp stem cells, express mesenchymal markers (STRO-1, CD73, CD90, CD105), and can give rise to odontoblast-like cells. Other contributing cell populations: dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs, may survive in apical canal portion with partial necrosis), periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs, migrate from periapical tissues especially with periapical bone loss), bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (drawn in when bleeding induced). The exact contribution of each population to regenerated tissue is not established.

Scaffolds and growth factors: The blood clot (from over-instrumentation causing controlled bleeding) is the most widely used scaffold, forming a fibrin network containing TGF-beta, PDGF, VEGF, and BMPs released from activated platelets. Its composition varies between patients, contributing to outcome inconsistency. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and platelet-rich fibrin (PRF), prepared by centrifugation of patient's blood, contain concentrated growth factors delivered more reproducibly. Several trials found PRP/PRF-treated teeth show greater dentinal wall thickening than blood-clot-treated teeth. PRF releases growth factors more slowly, possibly prolonging stimulatory effect. Injectable PRF (i-PRF) has improved handling properties. Final EDTA rinse (17%) at end of disinfection demineralizes canal wall surface, releasing bound growth factors (TGF-beta1, FGF-2, BMP-2).

Clinical protocols

Patient selection and first visit: REPs are best suited to patients under 18 years with necrotic immature permanent teeth, especially teeth with walls too thin for rotary instrumentation or apices too wide to obturate conventionally. Not appropriate for antibiotic paste allergy, irreparable structural damage, or poor patient cooperation. First visit priority: thorough disinfection while preserving dentinal structure. Sodium hypochlorite at 1.5-3% (lower than 5.25% conventional concentration, as higher concentrations reduce SCAP viability). Final 17% EDTA rinse. Intracanal medicament: double antibiotic paste (DAP, ciprofloxacin + metronidazole) generally preferred over original triple antibiotic paste (TAP) to avoid grey-brown crown discoloration from minocycline. Calcium hydroxide is an alternative avoiding antibiotics.

Second visit and coronal seal: Scheduled 2-4 weeks later once symptom-free. Re-irrigation with 17% EDTA, medicament removed. Sterile file passed 2mm beyond apex to induce periapical bleeding; blood clot forms up to ~3mm below cementoenamel junction. PRP or PRF may be placed instead of/alongside blood clot. 3-4mm layer of MTA or Biodentine placed over scaffold as cervical barrier; tooth restored with bonded composite. Biodentine widely used for faster setting, easier handling, no crown discoloration. Final restoration quality is one of strongest predictors of long-term success (gaps risk recontamination).

Clinical outcomes and evidence synthesis

Periapical healing is the most consistent finding, ranging 85-94% across individual studies, comparable to apexification healing rates. Root development outcomes (apical closure, root lengthening, dentinal wall thickening) seen in 74-81% of cases, with considerable inter-study variation. A systematic review/meta-analysis by Swaikat et al. synthesizing 32 studies comparing REPs with apexification found REPs produced greater root length and dentinal wall thickening, with comparable periapical healing between approaches. Studies using PRP/PRF generally report better wall thickening than blood clot.

Pulp sensibility recovery is least predictable (25-70%); positive sensibility test after REP should be interpreted cautiously as it may reflect innervation of periapical tissues rather than true intracanal nerve regeneration.

Prognostic factors: Younger age (≤12 years) at treatment is consistently favorable, likely due to more viable SCAPs and greater root growth potential. Preoperative root dimensions matter (longer/thicker roots at baseline → larger absolute gains). Long infection history before treatment associated with poorer outcomes (bacterial toxins reduce viable SCAP population). Poor coronal seal is a leading cause of failure (microleakage/recontamination). Scaffold choice (PRP/PRF vs blood clot) influences wall thickening outcomes.

Representative studies table (selected milestones, 2004-2025): Banchs & Trope (2004, case report, 2 teeth, ages 11-12, TAP+blood clot, 24mo follow-up): apical closure and root lengthening in both cases. Bose et al. (2009, retrospective cohort, 16 teeth, ages 7-14, TAP+blood clot, 12-48mo): root lengthening 81%, pulp sensibility recovery 56%. Jeeruphan et al. (2012, retrospective cohort, 45 teeth, ages 6-18, REP vs apexification, 24mo): REP superior for root development, comparable periapical healing. Galler et al. (2016, position statement): ESE protocol recommendations. Kahler et al. (2014, prospective cohort, 16 teeth, mean age 10, blood clot scaffold, ≥12mo): periapical healing 100%, root development in majority. Alagl et al. (2017, RCT, 40 teeth, ages 9-16, PRP vs blood clot, 36mo): PRP - greater root length and wall thickness increases. Song et al. (2025, retrospective cohort, 85 teeth, ages 6-18, AAE protocol, 36mo): periapical healing 91%, root development 76%, calcification 22%. Swaikat et al. (2023, SR+MA, 32 studies, REP vs apexification): REP superior for root length and wall thickening. Theekakul et al. (2024, prospective cohort, 68 teeth, ages 8-16, AAE protocol+blood clot, 24-48mo): root development 79%, periapical healing 94%, calcification 18%.

Limitations and challenges

Biological/histological limitations: Tissue forming inside treated canals is not true pulp. Histological examination of retrieved REP-treated teeth consistently shows a mixture of cementum-like, bone-like, and periodontal ligament-like tissue rather than organized pulp-dentin complex. Odontoblast-like cells lining canal walls are uncommon rather than expected. Continued root development/apical closure on radiograph may be driven by cementum/bone deposition from periapical cells rather than genuine dentinogenesis - clinicians should not assume radiographic improvement equals pulp regeneration.

Methodological/clinical challenges: Outcome definitions (apical closure, root development, success) are used inconsistently across studies; many report only 12-24 month follow-up, with long-term data beyond 5 years scarce. Most published work is retrospective; patient-reported outcomes rarely recorded. Crown discoloration from minocycline (TAP) was a recognized problem, reduced by shift to DAP and Biodentine. Intracanal calcification develops in ~18-22% of treated teeth over medium term, complicating retreatment if primary procedure fails. Antibiotic paste use in young patients warrants antimicrobial stewardship consideration; non-antibiotic alternatives (e.g., photodynamic therapy) under investigation. REPs require patient cooperation across two appointments, careful handling of fragile thin-walled roots, and high-quality coronal seal achievement - real obstacles in younger/anxious pediatric patients. PRP/PRF preparation requires centrifugation equipment/trained personnel not available everywhere.

Clinical implications

REPs should be first-line treatment for necrotic immature permanent teeth in patients meeting selection criteria (adequate infection control feasible, reliable attendance for both appointments, achievable durable coronal seal). MTA apexification remains a well-supported alternative when conditions cannot be met. Patients/parents should receive honest information: tissue forming inside canal may not be functional pulp, sensitivity testing may remain negative even after success, some calcification may develop, long-term follow-up needed.

Future directions

Most active translational research area: delivering defined stem cell populations (especially SCAPs) directly into disinfected canals rather than relying on passive recruitment through bleeding. Animal studies show SCAP delivery on appropriate scaffolds can produce tissue with odontoblastic cells lining canal walls, more closely resembling true pulp. Main barriers: regulatory requirements for cell-based therapies, practical challenges of harvesting/processing autologous cells. Dental stem cell banking from exfoliated deciduous teeth is a potential future strategy. Engineered scaffolds releasing growth factors at controlled rates (injectable hydrogels, peptide-based matrices, biodegradable polymers loaded with TGF-beta-1, VEGF, or BMP-2) show encouraging lab results directing stem cells toward pulpal rather than periodontal phenotype. Chitosan-based nanofibrous scaffolds show favorable biocompatibility/mechanical properties. Self-assembling peptide nanofiber scaffolds show promise for organized pulp-like tissue formation in preclinical models. CBCT-based measurement of root dimensions provides more accurate/reproducible data than periapical radiography and should be more widely adopted. Agreed-upon core outcome sets for REP trials (periapical healing, root development, pulp sensibility, patient-reported outcomes, histological characterization) would enable more meaningful pooled analyses. Multicenter prospective studies with minimum five-year follow-up remain the most pressing evidence gap.

Conclusions

REPs have changed management of necrotic immature permanent teeth, offering outcomes beyond what apexification achieves (root wall thickening, length gain), with consistently high periapical healing and root development rates. Limitations deserve honest acknowledgment: tissue forming inside treated canals is not reliably true pulp, sensibility recovery is unpredictable, and long-term follow-up data beyond two years remain limited. Intracanal calcification and its retreatment challenges should be incorporated into informed consent. Current REPs may be better described as biologically supported repair rather than true pulp regeneration. Careful case selection, meticulous technique, durable coronal seal, and long-term monitoring remain foundations of successful treatment until stem cell delivery, scaffold design, and growth factor control advances reach clinical practice.

Background Edentulism has significant functional and psychosocial consequences, and full-arch implant-supported fixed dental prostheses (FDPs) have become one of the most reliable rehabilitation options. However, long-term outcomes of prostheses due to prosthesis-related and patient-related risk factors remain limited. Thus, this study aimed to evaluate the long-term clinical performance, rates of complication types of full-arch implant-supported FDPs over a 4-10-year follow-up period. Additionally, the potential influence of patient- and prostheses- related risk factors—particularly bruxism, opposing arch type, retention system, and cantilever configuration—was assessed. Methods This retrospective study included 24 patients (13 males, 11 females; aged 39–77) treated between 2015 and 2020 with complete or single-arch edentulism received full-arch implant-supported FDPs. A total of 180 implants were placed in 34 edentulous arches, 24 of the implants were angled in posterior regions. Eighteen arches were restored with cement-retained prostheses, and 16 with screw-retained prostheses. Standardized clinical and radiographic examinations were performed at follow-up visits to record periimplant conditions, marginal bone levels and prosthetic complications. Outcomes were documented descriptively at the prosthesis and patient levels. Results However, the cumulative prosthesis survival probability at 5 and 10 years were 80% and 60%, respectively. The cumulative success probability (complication-free) was 61.8% at 1 year, 26.5% at 3 years, and 14.7% at 5 years. By the 6th year, all prostheses (100%) had experienced at least one minor complication, with a median complication-free time of 3 years. “Probable” bruxism was significantly associated with chipping ( p  = 0.049) and a higher rate of minor complications in the mandible ( p  = 0.015). However, no significant correlation was found between cantilever length or prosthetic material and marginal bone loss ( p  > 0.05). Conclusions Full-arch implant-supported FDPs demonstrated stable clinical performance over 4–10 years, with manageable technical complications. The present study contributes to the existing literature by providing long-term, single-center data with standardized follow-up and by presenting detailed implant-, prosthesis-, and patient-level complication profiles. Bruxism may increase the likelihood of certain technical complications, though further studies with larger samples are required to confirm. Trial registration Not applicable. Since removable dentures present issues, including occlusal wear, insufficient chewing forces, retention and stability loss during mastication and phonetics, patients and clinicians prefer fixed rehabilitation options [ 3 ,  4 ]. When there are adequate implants, conventional fixed bridge represents the main rehabilitation option. Nevertheless, the severe resorption of the maxilla and mandible makes a conventional fixed bridge an inadequate solution. Conversely, hybrid prostheses can easily replace the natural pink aesthetic, and they can reduce the mechanical and biological complications associated with their shock-absorbing characteristics [ 3 ,  4 ]. Although several retrospective studies with large cohorts, such as the 10-year evaluation by Chrcanovic et al. [ 5 ] have evaluated long-term outcomes of implant-supported FDPs, the extent to which patient-related factors—such as bruxism, smoking, or systemic conditions like diabetes mellitus (DM)—affect complication rates and maintenance requirements remains underreported. The present study differs from existing literature by specifically focusing on the interaction between patient-level risk factors and prosthetic design characteristics under standardized clinical protocols. Thus, the aim of this retrospective study was to evaluate implant survival, prosthetic complications, and success rates of full-arch implant-supported FDPs over a 4–10-year period. The study specifically focused on identifying potential risk indicators for mechanical and biological failures, with particular emphasis on the impact of parafunctional habits (bruxism), systemic conditions (DM, smoking), and prosthetic design characteristics (material type, retention mechanism, and cantilever presence). By analyzing these variables, the study sought to determine which factors most significantly contribute to the long-term maintenance requirements and complication patterns in this patient cohort. Materials and methods This retrospective study included 24 patients treated with full-arch/mouth implant supported FDP between 2015 and 2020. The study was based on data collection from dental records. The clinical and radiographic examination included medical and dental history review, clinical assessment of biological and technical complications encountered with implant-supported FDPs. Inclusion criteria were as follows: 
 
 Fully edentulous mouth or single edentulous maxilla/mandible restored with implant-supported FDP Follow-up period at least 1 year after stabilization of the prosthesis Presence of a periapical radiograph taken directly after implant placement Exclusion criteria were as follows: 
 
 Zygoma implants Implants with bone augmentation Patients required soft tissue managements Definitions The criteria for identifying the signs and symptoms of bruxism were based on the International Classification of Sleep Disorders [ 6 ], in alignment with the same guidelines applied in a study [ 7 ]. In accordance with the definition set out in an international consensus [ 8 ], the diagnosis of “possible” sleep or awake bruxism should be based on self-report, using questionnaires and/or the anamnesis. “Probable” sleep or awake bruxism should be based on self-report with a clinical inspection. “Definite” sleep bruxism should be based on self-report, clinical examination, and a polysomnographic record preferably with the audios/videos. In the present study, self-reports were obtained during the anamnesis. Patients were asked whether they experienced clenching or grinding during sleep or while awake, and when applicable, whether bed partners or family members had noticed grinding sounds. Clinical examination included the identification of wear marks / chipping on natural dentition or prosthetic materials, linea alba, cheek or tongue indentations, and palpation of the masseter and temporalis muscles for hypertrophy or tenderness. Patients presenting both self-reported symptoms and at least one clinical sign were categorized as “probable bruxers”, and all assessments were performed by the same clinician. Smoking 1 cigarette per day is enough to be classified as a smoker. Patients who quitted smoking before the implant surgery and remained as non-smoker until the last follow-up were determined as former smokers [ 5 ]. Any condition that led to the prosthetic removal and/or replacement was determined as prosthetic failure. Complications were classified as minor or major complications. Minor complications required only chairside treatments. Biological minor complications were classified as soft tissue recession; inflammation beneath the fixed prosthesis; peri-implant mucositis. Technical minor complications were classified as wear and/or chipping of the prosthetic material; loosening of abutment/occlusal screw; and decementation. Major complications, those necessitating further treatments, were classified as peri-implantitis and late implant failure in terms of biological. Major technical complications were classified as prosthetic material and/or framework fracture; abutment fracture; and abutment/occlusal screw fracture [ 5 ]. Implant survival was defined as “implant remaining in situ and supporting a functional prosthesis during the entire observation time” and a failure was defined as an event leading to the loss of the implant or the need to remove the implant [ 2 ,  9 ,  10 ]. Clinical and radiographic procedures The evaluated clinical parameters were implant and prosthetic survival; prosthetic materials; type of retention (cement or screw); cantilevers; presence/absence of bruxism and DM [ 7 ]. In accordance with The California Dental Association rating system for quality [ 9 ] and functional properties of the FDI criteria were selected and modified to evaluate the clinical situation of the prostheses at each follow-up examination. All surgical procedures were performed by experienced faculty surgeons using a standardized protocol. To standardize mechanical stability and minimize connection-related variables, implants with internal hexagonal connection designs were utilized. Primary stability was clinically verified for all implants prior to prosthetic loading. For the prosthetic phase, metal-ceramic, zirconia-ceramic, and metal-acrylic implant-supported FDPs were fabricated conventionally. The occlusal scheme was designed as group function for all patients. Regarding the veneering protocol, ceramic materials were produced using a traditional layering technique. The opposing dentition and the presence of cantilevers were recorded for each case. Following the stabilization of prostheses, patients attended periodic follow-up visits for radiological and clinical examinations. Standardized periapical radiographs were obtained using the parallel technique with a film holder (Rinn-plus, Dentsply Sirona), and panoramic radiographs were obtained on the day of implant placement (baseline), at the time of loading, and at 1-, 3-, 5-year and the following years’ follow-up visits. To compensate for any radiographic magnification or distortion, the known physical length and diameter of each implant were used as a reference for linear calibration in the software. Marginal Bone Level (MBL) was defined as the distance from the implant-abutment interface (reference line) to the first visible bone-to-implant contact. Measurements were performed on both the mesial and distal aspects of each implant. All assessments were carried out by a single, calibrated examiner. To ensure measurement reliability, a subset of 20 radiographs was re-measured after two weeks. Statistical analysis Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation and median (minimum-maximum) for quantitative variables, while frequency and percentages were used for categorical data. The normality of the data distribution was assessed, and due to the sample size being less than 30 in subgroups, non-parametric methods were preferred. The Mann-Whitney U test was used for comparing quantitative data across demographic groups, while the Pearson Chi-square test was employed for categorical variables. Multiple comparisons were examined using the Bonferroni-corrected Z-test. To account for the heterogeneous follow-up durations (ranging from 4 to 10 years), prosthesis survival was evaluated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Due to the limited sample size ( n  = 24) and the irregular distribution of data, inferential statistical analyses were primarily performed at the prosthesis level. While patient-level variables (e.g., smoking, DM, bruxism) were reported descriptively to avoid unit-of-analysis errors, more complex multivariable models could not be performed due to the study’s exploratory nature and limited cohort size. Statistical significance was set at  p  < 0.05. Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the limited number of available standardized radiographs for some follow-up intervals, intra-rater reliability for marginal bone level measurements (Intraclass Correlation Coefficient, ICC) could not be formally calculated. Results A total of 24 patients (11 females, 13 males; mean age 58.17 ± 9.73 years) were included in this retrospective study. At the patient level, 16.7% had diabetes mellitus ( n  = 4), 45.8% were active smokers ( n  = 11), 45.8% presented signs of bruxism ( n  = 11), and 54.2% had periodontal problems ( n  = 13). Only 2 patients were free of any medical condition, smoking, and parafunctional habits. The patients were followed for a mean period of approximately 5 years (Table  1 ). These patients were rehabilitated with 34 full-arch implant-supported fixed dental prostheses (FDPs) (Maxilla/Mandible = 20/14), supported by 180 implants (Straumann, Nobel Biocare, Nucleoss) (107 in the maxilla/73 in the mandible). A total 24 of the 180 implants were placed angled on the posterior regions (12 maxilla/12 mandible) according to the all-on-four concept. Implant diameters ranged from 3.3 to 4.5 mm, and lengths varied from 8 to 13 mm.

During the follow-up period, 4 prostheses were replaced due to major complications (prosthetic failure). Regarding implant stability, only one implant was lost due to peri-implantitis. However, the probability of major complication-free survival was 91.2% at 3 years, %91.2, at 5 years and 58.1% at 10 years. The median major complication-free survival time was 9 years (95% CI: 6.90–11.1) (Fig.  1 ). The replacement of the aforementioned prostheses did not involve or necessitate the removal of the underlying supporting implants. To account for the heterogeneous follow-up durations, a life-table analysis (Kaplan–Meier) was performed at the prosthesis level. The cumulative prosthesis survival probabilities were 95.8% at 3 years, 80% at 5 years, and 60% at 10 years (Fig.  2 ). The Kaplan–Meier analysis for prosthesis success—defined as the total absence of any technical or biological complications—demonstrated a steady decline over time. The cumulative success probability was 61.8% at 1 year, 26.5% at 3 years, and 14.7% at 5 years. Notably, by the 6th year of follow-up, the success probability dropped to 11.8%, and all prostheses (100%) experienced at least one minor complication during the entire observation period. The median complication-free survival time was 3 years (Fig.  3 ).

The mean follow-up period in this study was approximately 5 years (ranging from 4 to 10 years) which is consistent with several longitudinal evaluations in the literature [ 4 ,  5 ,  9 ]. While the 10-year prosthesis survival probability was high at 98.1%, the cumulative prosthesis survival probability determined by Kaplan–Meier analysis was 80% at 5 years and 60% at 10 years. Furthermore, when considering ‘success’ as a complication-free state, the cumulative probabilities were 61.8% at 1 year, 26.5% at 3 years, and 14.7% at 5 years, with a median complication-free survival time of 3 years. Notably, by the end of the follow-up period, all prostheses (100%) had experienced at least one minor complication. These findings align with Chrcanovic et al. [ 5 ], emphasizing that while implant stability is often maintained, technical and biological complications remain a frequent clinical challenge. In the literature, implant survival rates for conventional loading protocols range between 91.7% and 100% across various follow-up periods [ 10 – 13 ]. In the present study, only one implant failure occurred (in the mandible), which is consistent with high survival outcomes reported for full-arch rehabilitations and support that the implant-supported full-arch FDPs could be a beneficial treatment method for fully edentulous patients. Prosthetic design factors, such as cantilever length, are often cited as risks for marginal bone loss (MBL) [ 10 – 14 ]. In our cohort, cantilever lengths ranged from 5 to 7.5 mm. Unlike some reports suggesting that distal extensions jeopardize stability, our analysis identified no statistically significant correlation between cantilever length and MBL or technical complications ( p  > 0.05). This neutrality aligns with recent findings suggesting that when occlusal forces are well-controlled and cantilever lengths are kept within conservative limits, they do not necessarily jeopardize the peri-implant bone stability [ 15 ]. From a clinical perspective, we believe that the relatively short cantilever lengths maintained in our cohort may have contributed to the stable bone levels observed. Improperly positioned implants or prosthetic misalignments can contribute to mechanical complications that compromise the prosthetic maintenance over time, increasing failure possibility and necessity for intervention [ 16 ,  17 ]. There has been no major effect of sex and age on the complication rates. In regards of implant placement, the present study revealed no statistically significant difference in the survival and complication rates between axial and tilted implants in either the maxilla or the mandible. A recent systematic review [ 18 ] reported no significant difference in survival rates or marginal bone loss between axial and tilted implants placed according to the all-on-four concept. The advantage of tilted placement is the placement of a longer implant in comparison with an axial implant. Consequently, the contact area between the implant and the bone is increased, thereby enhancing the probability of achieving optimal primary stability [ 19 ]. The impact of systemic conditions remains a topic of debate. There are several reports that patients with poorly controlled DM [ 20 ,  21 ] have a high risk of developing peri-implantitis and failure. In this study, no significant differences were found in biological complication rates regarding the presence of DM. Although 4 of the 15 inflammation events and 2 of the 4 peri-implantitis cases occurred in patients with DM, the small subgroup size precludes definitive conclusions. Similarly, while smoking is known to exert detrimental effects on healing [ 22 ,  23 ], our analysis indicated it was not a significant factor for prosthetic complications ( p  > 0.05). Nevertheless, the multiple response analysis indicated that smoking did not constitute a significant factor in the complications of prostheses, but it is considered necessary for smokers undergoing implant treatment to receive long-term follow-up and smoking cessation guidance. Regarding prosthetic materials, zirconia-based prostheses showed high clinical success and patient satisfaction, though the material type did not statistically influence complication rates, aligning with previous reviews [ 24 ,  25 ]. Minor complications were the most common complication types in this study. Chipping was the most common technical complication, consistent with several reports [ 26 ,  27 ]. Notably, a significant correlation was identified between “probable” bruxism and the incidence of chipping ( p  = 0.049). Furthermore, the rate of minor complications in the mandible was significantly higher in bruxers ( p  = 0.015), and it isthought that this may be related to the rigid and compact bone structure of the mandible which transmits higher stresses to the prosthesis. Additionally, at least one of each major technical complication and one late implant failure was observed in bruxers. Despite the absence of statistical significance, the results reinforce the findings of earlier studies stating that parafunctional habits significantly impact prosthetic maintenance [ 7 ,  28 ]. Screw-retained implant-supported FDPs are appropriate treatment options for edentulous patients, as they are known to present lesser biological and mechanical complications [ 29 ]. In the current study, 16 arches were restored with screw-retained FDPs and 18 with cemented FDPs. While screw-retained designs offer easier retrievability [ 30 ], they are susceptible to mechanical issues [ 31 ]. In this study, two screw fractures were observed in two different FDPs, one occurring in a patient with bruxism. Thus, it is thought that the fracture was a consequence of the excessive force that led to the screw loosening and movement of the prostheses. Conversely, ten full-arch FDPs exhibited repeated decementation. A total 6 of these 10 cases occurred in patients with poor oral hygiene, suggesting that microbial plaque acidity may contribute to cement degradation [ 32 ]. Overall, the follow-up evaluations and the patients’ experience suggest that both prosthetic designs are durable and successful in terms of esthetics and function. Regarding marginal bone level changes, all patients exhibited bone loss within the first year after loading, which remained within accepted clinical success limits without pathological signs [ 33 ]. In regard to crestal bone level changes, the criteria for successful osseointegration of the implants according to Smith and Zarb were adopted [ 34 ]. Some implants with initial bone loss may develop an infection of the bone-implant interface leading to peri-implantitis [ 35 ]. Thirteen patients showed progressive loss in subsequent years. One patient with DM and a smoking history exhibited bone loss exceeding 3 mm, and another with a history of periodontitis lost an implant on the 3rd quadrant. Although there is no statistical correlation between periodontal/peri-implant history and late implant failure in the present study, it is thought that the patients’ periodontal history could be related to the peri-implantitis and loss of implant. Regardless of the specific conditions, radiological bone loss in other patients is attributed to poor oral hygiene, and parafunctional habits as determined by clinical evaluations. It is essential to strike a balance between prosthetic and anatomic considerations when planning and providing complex restorations. However, maintaining hygiene in complete-arch fixed prostheses represents a significant challenge [ 36 ,  37 ]. In the present study as in the reported cases, the hygienic design of the prostheses and the regular follow-up protocol reduced the risk of peri-implant complications [ 38 – 43 ]. According to the results, 7 of the 15 prostheses, which exhibited enflammation, were belonged to patients with periodontitis history, and 4 belonged to patients with DM. From a prosthetic perspective, the mono-block design overcomes the technical complications associated with implant supported FDP owing to the multiple compartments [ 32 ,  38 – 41 ]. The same technique was applied, and a fixed treatment modality with predictable results and higher satisfaction was provided to the patients in the present study. This study has several limitations. Its retrospective nature resulted in some information gaps, precluding formal ICC analysis for bone measurements. The limited sample size (n = 24) constrained the statistical power, making the findings exploratory rather than definitive. Additionally, a “definite” diagnosis of bruxism via polysomnography was not possible. The heterogeneity in follow-up durations (4–10 years) was addressed using Kaplan–Meier analysis to ensure accurate temporal representation. Lastly, because this was not a prospective trial, standardized biological parameters such as bleeding on probing could not be consistently provided [ 44 – 46 ]. Consequently, all results should be interpreted with caution. Despite these limitations, the 4–10 year ‘real-world’ data provides significant clinical insights into the long-term maintenance requirements of full-arch implant-supported rehabilitations. Conclusion Full-arch implant-supported FDPs are one of the most common treatment options for edentulism, yet long-term evidence on complication rates and patient- / prostheses-related risk factors remains scarce. Within the limitations of this exploratory study, implant-supported full-arch FDPs demonstrate high cumulative survival rates over a 4–10-year period. While biological success is favorable, technical complications remain frequent, requiring long-term maintenance. The findings suggest a potential correlation between parafunctional habits, such as bruxism, and minor technical complications; however, this relationship should be interpreted with caution due to the borderline statistical significance. Additionally, maintaining conservative cantilever lengths appears to support marginal bone stability. Ultimately, long-term success in full-arch rehabilitations necessitates meticulous pre-operative risk assessment and a prosthetic protocol tailored to patient-specific factors.

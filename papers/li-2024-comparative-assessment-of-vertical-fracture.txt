## ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study conducted a thorough assessment of vertical root fracture (VRF) resistance of endodontically treated teeth filled with various canal-filling systems and techniques through frequentist network meta-analysis (NMA).
MethodsA systematic literature search was performed to retrieve relevant publications using PubMed, Embase, ScienceDirect, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library databases. The retrieval time range was from Jan 2000 to Sep 2023. The literature selection and data extraction were independently conducted by two investigators. Eligible studies were critically appraised for risk of bias and quality of evidence. Subsequently, we used the ‘network’ package in Stata/MP 17.0 software to compare the VRF resistance of roots treated with different obturating systems or techniques.
ResultsForty-eight trials involving 2724 single-canal roots were included in the NMA. The surface under the cumulative ranking curves indicated that Resilon/Epiphany (80.8%), although outdated, provided the highest VRF resistance among all obturating systems. This was followed by GP/iRootSP (55.5%), GP/MTA-Plus (47.9%), GP/AH-Plus (47.4%), GP/AH-26 (45.9%), and GP/ZOE (12.3%). The roots filled with Resilon/Epiphany showed better VRF resistance as compared to those filled with gutta-percha/AH-Plus (SMD = 0.77, 95%CI 0.10 to 1.45) and gutta-percha/zinc oxide eugenol (SMD = 1.64, 95%CI 0.47 to 2.80). The single cone technique (SCT) group displayed the highest VRF resistance, and roots filled with all techniques, except Thermafil, outperformed the positive control group.
ConclusionsResilon/Epiphany demonstrated the greatest root strength among all six obturating systems, followed by GP/iRootSP and GP/MTA-Plus, which are calcium silicate-based systems. In contrast, the gutta-percha/zinc-oxide eugenol system exhibited the weakest performance. All canal-filling techniques, particularly the SCT, strengthen endodontically treated roots, except for the Thermafil technique. Further well-designed clinical trials with large sample sizes are essential for validation.

## Introduction

Pulpitis and periapical inflammation are prevalent conditions affecting the health of dental tissue and periapical tissue, with root canal therapy being the primary clinical treatment choice. Prior studies suggested that roots post-endodontic treatment exhibit increased vulnerability to crown or root fractures compared to vital teeth [1]. This heightened susceptibility may arise from factors such as excessive removal of tooth structure, dentin dehydration, access cavity preparation, root canal instrumentation, irrigation, application of excessive pressure during obturation, and post-space preparation [2]. Among all types of fractures, vertical root fracture (VRF) is not only the most common but also the most severe, often necessitating tooth extraction [3]. Hence, there is an imperative need for VRF prevention in clinical practice.

Over the past few decades, various canal-filling materials have been recommended to enhance the fracture resistance of endodontically treated teeth to achieve an optimal mechanical interlock between dentin and obturating materials [4]. Gutta-percha (GP) has long been the preferred root canal-filling material due to its excellent biocompatibility, low cytotoxicity, thermal plasticity, and ease of manipulation [5]. However, GP alone falls short in effectively sealing root canal walls and should be used in conjunction with a sealer [1]. The combining of GP with the resin-based sealer AH-Plus (GP/AH-Plus) represents the gold standard within the current canal-filling systems. Studies have demonstrated that the GP/AH-Plus canal-filling system significantly enhanced resistance against VRF [6–8]. Another system, Resilon/Epiphany, used to be widely employed to improve the fracture resistance of endodontically treated teeth [9]. In this system, Resilon, a polyester-based material that is bioactive and radiopaque, performs similarly to GP [10]. When Resilon is used in conjunction with Epiphany, a durable resin-based sealer, it can penetrate dentine tubules, providing a monoblock status of obturation and reinforcing the strength of endodontically treated teeth [10]. There has been ongoing controversy regarding the superiority in fracture resistance of the two aforementioned canal-filling systems. Although Tan et al. previously conducted a traditional pairwise meta-analysis to compare the VRF resistance of roots endodontically treated by the two canal-filling systems, the result was marginally significant (P = 0.05) [11]. However, Resilon/Epiphany is no longer in use and has been removed from the market, possibly due to its relatively high long-term failure rate associated with biodegradation [12, 13]. This underscores the necessity of the current network analysis, which aims to conduct comprehensive comparisons of canal-filling systems involving other types of resin-based, zinc-oxide eugenol, and calcium silicate-based sealers, in addition to Resilon/Epiphany and GP/AH-Plus.

To date, no study has quantitatively synthesized the available evidence on VRF resistance in teeth treated with various endodontic techniques, such as the lateral compaction technique (LCT), single cone technique (SCT), warm vertical compaction technique (VCT), and the Thermafil obturation technique. LCT is frequently chosen for its user-friendly operation, minimal clinical equipment needs, and cost-effectiveness [14, 15]. However, the wedging forces and stresses applied by the spreader during LCT may increase the risk of VRF [16, 17]. In contrast, the SCT mitigates the risk of VRF by preventing the deep insertion of packing instruments into the canals. Furthermore, the use of bioceramic sealers in SCT further reduces this risk. Its simplicity and time efficiency make SCT a popular choice among clinicians [18]. While VCT offers better adaptation to canal anatomy, it is more time-consuming and requires greater technical proficiency, and it may not be suitable for all canal anatomies [19–21]. The newly developed Thermafil obturation technique stands out for its ability to control volume shrinkage and adapt to irregular root canals [22]. Although these different obturation techniques exert varying pressures on the root canal walls, their relative impact on the risk of VRF remains uncertain.

Unlike traditional meta-analyses, network meta-analysis (NMA) integrates data from multiple interventions, allowing direct and indirect comparisons even in the absence of head-to-head studies [23]. This method enables the estimation and ranking of the efficacy of all interventions [23]. In this study, we conducted an NMA using in vitro randomized controlled trial data to thoroughly assess the VRF resistance linked to different root canal-filling systems and techniques following endodontic treatment. Our objective is to provide dentists with evidence-based insights for making well-informed decisions.

## Materials and methods

### Study reporting and registering

This study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses for Network Meta-analyses (PRISMA-NMA) guideline [24]. The protocol for this systematic review and NMA has been registered in the International Platform of Registered Systematic Review and Meta-analysis Protocols under registration number INPLASY2023100034, which is accessible at inplasy.com.

### Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies that assessed the VRF resistance of endodontially treated teeth in vitro and met criteria as follows were included: (1) The specimens consisted of freshly extracted single-rooted human teeth with closed apices, which provided sufficient space for obturation following standard root canal preparation [11]; (2) After excluding irrelevant obturating systems, each study should include at least two eligible comparative endodontic treatments, encompassing both positive and/or negative controls; (3) Given the numerous obturating systems developed in recent decades, it was impractical to consider all of them. Therefore, we selectively included studies that involved the six most widely used obturating systems, identified through a preliminary literature review: Resilon/Epiphany, GP/AH-26, GP/AH-Plus, GP/zinc-oxide eugenol (GP/ZOE), GP/iRoot SP, and GP/mineral trioxide aggregate (GP/MTA-Plus); (4) Studies that either provided the mean and standard deviation of VRF resistance or offered sufficient data to compute these statistics; (5) Randomized controlled trial conducted in vitro.

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) literature comments, reviews, or conference abstracts; (2) teeth from non-human sources; (3) studies that concurrently evaluated the effects of other factors (e.g., radiotherapy) on the fracture resistance of endodontically treated roots [25]; (4) studies in which a single root was obturated with different materials in separate segments; (5) inadequate data on the VRF resistance of roots; (6) studies were also excluded if, after removing irrelevant obturation systems, only the positive and negative control interventions were left; (7) studies in which the obturating materials and/or sealers were not clearly specified; (8) data with obvious errors were also excluded from the analysis.

### Data sources and retrieval strategies

A systematic literature search was conducted across Embase, ScienceDirect, PubMed, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library databases (Fig. 1), covering the period from January 2000 to September 2023. Subject terms were combined with free terms such as “root canal obturation”, “treated roots”, “obturating material”, “root canal filling materials”, “canal filling system”, “root reinforcement”, “endodontic treatment”, “root obturat*”, “vertical fracture”, “tooth fracture”, “root fracture”, “fracture resistance”, and “dental stress analysis” (protocol INPLASY2023100034). The publications were limited to those in English. Additionally, we manually reviewed the reference lists of relevant reviews and all eligible studies to identify further studies. Supplementary Table 1 details the search strategies for the five databases.

Fig. 1The PRISMA diagram showed the process for article retrieval

### Literature selection and data extraction

The literature selection and data extraction were carried out independently by two researchers (JC and SL). Any disagreements were resolved by a senior researcher (GL). After removing duplicate records, we first screened the titles and abstracts to eliminate studies that clearly did not meet the criteria. The remaining studies were then evaluated for eligibility by reviewing their full texts according to the specified inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Data were collected using a standardized form that recorded the first author, publication year, country/region, sample size, tooth type, irrigant, obturating techniques and systems, control groups, test machine loading rate, and the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (range) of the VRF resistance, as applicable. The median (range) values were transformed into mean ± SD for data analysis using the formula proposed by Hozo et al. [26]. Thereafter, the means ± SDs of different subgroups within studies were combined following the formula recommended by Zhang et al. [27]. We converted the measurement units for VRF to Newtons before the formal data analysis.

### Quality assessment

Two reviewers (SL and JT) independently evaluated the methodological quality using the bias risk assessment tool developed by Tan et al. [11], with a minor modification to include an additional item: whether the study reported the test machine loading rate. Finally, each of the following nine items were classified as ‘yes’ or ‘no’ based on the descriptions provided in the individual studies: randomization of teeth, pre-experiment examination of teeth, standardization of root dimensions, sample size calculation, endodontic treatment performed by a single operator, use of materials according to the protocol provided by the manufacturer, reporting test machine loading rate, blinding of the examiner, and application of appropriate statistical methods. Studies were subsequently categorized into high, medium, or low risk of bias categories based on their total scores: ≤3, 4–5, and ≥ 6, respectively.

### Data analysis

We employed the ‘network’ package in Stata/MP 17.0 software (StataCorp LLC., College Station, TX, USA) to perform the network meta-analysis (NMA) using the frequency framework and random-effects models. To compare the VRF resistance of roots treated with various obturating systems or techniques, we used the standardized mean difference (SMD) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) as effect sizes. Network diagrams were created to depict the relationships between different interventions, with node size indicating the number of teeth and line thickness representing the number of studies.

Before conducting the NMA, inconsistency tests were performed in two stages. First, global inconsistency was assessed using the design-by-treatment interaction model, followed by a Wald χ2 test [28]. Second, local inconsistency was examined using a loop-specific approach, which assessed inconsistencies in each closed loop of network interventions individually [28]. The inconsistency factor (IF) was determined by subtracting direct and indirect estimations for each comparison within a specific loop [28]. Additionally, the 95%CI of inconsistent factor (IF) and P-value from the Z test were also calculated. Local consistency was deemed acceptable if the lower limit 95%CI of the IF includes 0 or P-value > 0.05. The consistency model was applied when both global and local consistency assumptions are met.

The NMA results were synthesized from all possible pairwise comparisons, encompassing both mixed and indirect comparisons. The findings were then displayed using forest plots. Contribution plots were also drawn. The study ranked the VRF resistance of roots treated with various obturating systems or techniques according to the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) values, which range from 0 to 1. A higher SUCRA value indicates a greater likelihood that the intervention has the highest fracture resistance. Additionally, comparison-adjusted funnel plots were created to visually assess publication bias and small-study effects [29].

## Results

### Study selection

The initial search of five databases yielded 5193 potentially relevant articles. After removing the duplicates, we reviewed the titles and abstracts of 3662 articles, which led to the exclusion of 3533 studies. After a thorough evaluation of full texts and retrieval from reference lists, 48 articles [1, 4, 7, 8, 10, 30–72] and 46 articles [1, 4, 7, 8, 10, 30, 32–39, 41−72] were deemed eligible for the NMA, comparing the VRF resistance with different obturating systems and techniques, respectively. The flowchart illustrating the article retrieval process is presented in Fig. 1.

### Characteristics of included studies

The studies included in the analysis were published between 2002 and 2023. All of the specimens involved were single-rooted human teeth without open apices. A total of 2724 roots were involved in the NMA comparing VRF resistance of different obturating systems, including 336 unprepared and unfilled roots (negative control, NC), 580 prepared and unfilled roots (positive control, PC), 1808 roots filled with the six target obturating systems. Regarding the type of teeth, human premolars were used as specimens in 31 studies [1, 7, 8, 10, 30–38, 40−42, 44, 49, 50, 52, 53, 55, 56,58–63, 67, 72], incisors were used in 10 studies [4, 39, 43, 48, 51, 54, 57, 64, 68, 71], canines were used in 2 studies [66, 70], anteriors were used in 1 study [45], and the type of teeth was not reported in 4 studies [46, 47, 65, 69]. ### Inconsistency test

No indications of global inconsistencies were identified in the NMAs that compared the VRF resistance of various obturating systems and techniques (P = 0.47 and P = 0.86, respectively). Concerning obturating systems, the local inconsistency test confirmed that all of the closed loops met the consistency criteria (all of the limit 95%CIs of the IFs contained 0 and all P values were higher than 0.05) (Supplemental Fig. 1). Regarding obturating techniques, both direct and indirect comparisons within all loops were consistent, except for the SCT-Thermafil-VCT loop, where the 95% CI of IF did not include 0 (P < 0.001)  (Supplemental Fig. 2). Therefore, both NMAs employed consistency models.

### Pairwise comparisons

In Fig. 3A, the VRF resistance of roots in the other groups was consistently lower than that in the NC group. In contrast, the VRF resistance of roots filled with GP/AH-Plus (SMD = 0.78, 95% CI 0.23 to 1.33) and Resilon/Epiphany systems (SMD = 1.55, 95% CI 0.93 to 2.18) was significantly higher than that in the PC group. Specifically, roots filled with Resilon/Epiphany exhibited superior VRF resistance compared to those filled with GP/AH-Plus systems (SMD = 0.77, 95% CI 0.10 to 1.45) and GP/ZOE systems (SMD = 1.64, 95% CI 0.47 to 2.80). No other substantial pairwise differences in VRF resistance among the obturating systems were detected.

In Fig. 3B, the VRF resistance of roots filled using LCT, SCT, Thermafil, VCT techniques, and the PC group consistently remained lower than that in the NC group. Additionally, the VRF resistance of roots treated with LCT, SCT, and VCT techniques surpassed that in the PC group (LCT vs. PC: SMD = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.32 to 1.38; SCT vs. PC: SMD = 1.32, 95% CI 0.62 to 2.01; VCT vs. PC: SMD = 1.00, 95% CI 0.01 to 1.99). However, the pairwise comparisons of VRF resistance among all four obturating techniques did not yield statistically significant differences.

## Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first NMA investigating the relative resistance to VRF among six obturating systems and four obturating techniques in endodontically treated roots. The SUCRA results suggest that the Resilon/Epiphany system is likely the most effective in resisting vertical fractures in roots following endodontic treatment, with the SCT emerging as the optimal obturating technique. Our findings significantly contribute to the field of endodontics, empowering both researchers and dentists to make evidence-based decisions regarding root canal-filling systems and techniques, particularly concerning VRF resistance, and to select optimal filling systems and techniques that are contextually appropriate.

As highlighted in the introduction, post-endodontic treatment, root fracture resistance is compromised by factors such as tooth structure loss, over-instrumentation, dentin dehydration due to prolonged irrigation, and uncontrollable forces during obturation [73]. This explains the superior VRF resistance observed in the NC group compared to roots treated with various obturation systems and techniques, including the PC group (Fig. 3A&B). Our findings, supported by SUCRA probability, demonstrate the highest VRF resistance in the NC group and the lowest in the PC group (Figs. 4 and 5). These results align with Shafer et al.‘s earlier research, highlighting that enlarged but unfilled roots are notably weaker than filled roots, making them more prone to fracture [66].

Obturation with materials compensating for weakening effects and strengthening roots is crucial. The NMA findings revealed significantly increased VRF resistance in roots treated with Resilon/Epiphany and GP/AH-Plus when compared to the PC group. This can be attributed to their superior sealing properties, effective obturation, and inherent physical characteristics [33, 74]. The combination of these properties facilitates the creation of a stable and resilient obturation, thereby reinforcing root structure. In contrast, the PC group, which underwent instrumentation but remained unfilled, lacks the additional support provided by obturating material. Additionally, the finding that roots filled with Resilon/Epiphany systems were stronger than those in the PC group (SMD = 1.55, 95%CI 0.93 to 2.18) aligns with Tan et al.‘s meta-analysis (SMD = 0.83, 95%CI 0.44 to 1.22) [11]. However, their study failed to detect a significant difference in VRF resistance between roots filled with GP/AH-Plus systems and the PC group (SMD = 0.59, 95%CI -0.02 to 1.21), which contradicted our result (SMD = 0.78, 95%CI 0.23 to 1.33). The discrepancy may stem from our updated NMA incorporating recent studies [1, 4, 30–35, 37, 72] and pooling direct and indirect comparisons for increased statistical power.

Furthermore, Resilon/Epiphany-treated roots exhibited higher VRF resistance than those treated with GP/AH-Plus and GP/ZOE systems. The enhanced performance of Resilon/Epiphany systems in VRF resistance can be attributed to the monoblock concept. Utilizing a primer for tooth root surface preparation through self-etching, this system creates dentinal projections that facilitate interlocking bonding among the tooth, sealer, and core material [33]. However, Resilon/Epiphany is no longer available on the market, likely due to concerns over its relatively high long-term failure rate, which has been linked to biodegradation [12, 13]. Barborka et al. reported that teeth filled with Resilon/Epiphany had a 5.7-fold higher risk of long-term failure compared to those treated with GP/AH-Plus [12]. Based on the SUCRA curves, two calcium silicate-based systems—GP/iRootSP (55.5%) and GP/MTA-Plus (47.9%)—ranked slightly higher than the two resin-based systems, GP/AH-Plus (47.4%) and GP/AH-26 (45.9%). These calcium silicate-based systems may offer a viable alternative with relatively strong VRF resistance in the current market.

The superior performance of calcium silicate-based systems in VRF resistance can be attributed to their distinctive material properties. These systems exhibit bioactivity, which promotes the formation of hydroxyapatite and establishes a chemical bond with dentin, enhancing the long-term stability of the seal and increasing fracture resistance [59, 75, 76]. Furthermore, the bioceramic sealer possesses hydrophilic properties and a low contact angle, enabling it to spread easily across the canal wall. This characteristic facilitates effective adaptation and a robust hermetic seal achieved through mechanical interlocking [77]. Additionally, its ultra-fine particle size, combined with the optimal premixed consistency delivered via a capillary tip system, may enhance its capacity to penetrate the full length of the canal [59]. It is noteworthy that the pairwise comparisons between two calcium silicate-based systems (GP/iRootSP and GP/MTA-Plus) and two resin-based systems (GP/AH-Plus and GP/AH-26) did not yield statistically significant results. This lack of significance may be attributed to the relatively small sample sizes of the calcium silicate-based systems included in this study (72 and 144, respectively). Consequently, our findings will require further validation through future studies.

Our study found no significant difference in VRF resistance among teeth treated with the four investigated obturating techniques. However, according to the SUCRA curves, SCT demonstrated the strongest resistance to VRF. This robust resistance is likely due to the minimal pressure exerted on the root canal walls during SCT [59]. The LCT, while widely used, has certain drawbacks, particularly its potential to induce VRFs due to excessive compaction pressure. The pressure applied by the spreader is critical to the success of LCT, as excessive force can overstress the root structure, potentially causing microcracks in dentin and increasing fracture risk [16, 17, 78]. To minimize this risk while ensuring effective canal filling, the pressure must be carefully controlled, and it is generally deemed safe to apply a force below 5 kg for most teeth [79]. Additionally, compared to other filling techniques, LCT is more time-consuming, less suitable for irregular or complex root canals, and often results in poor homogeneous fillings, which can negatively impact treatment outcomes [80, 81]. Although the VRF resistance among SCT, LCT, and VCT groups did not show statistical differences, these results can be considered in conjunction with the clinical context to offer clinicians certain insights. Interestingly, all obturating techniques demonstrated superior VRF resistance compared to the PC group, except for the Thermafil group. This inconsistency may be linked to the insufficient tight bonding between Thermafil carriers and GP. The force applied by the plugger, coupled with the associated heat during the procedure, could lead to thermal expansion in the root dentin, potentially compromising the overall strength of the roots [82].

### Limitations

While this NMA facilitated a comprehensive exploration of various obturating systems and techniques, revealing no evidence of publication bias, it is crucial to acknowledge several limitations. Firstly, around one-third (14/48) of the included studies carried a medium risk of bias, and only three out of 48 adhered to blinding examiner criteria. Secondly, our investigation focused on six commonly used obturating systems, rather than encompassing all available options. Thirdly, a loop-specific inconsistency in the SCT-Thermafil-VCT loop was identified, diminishing the overall level of evidence. Fourthly, unaccounted variables such as tooth types, root canal preparation techniques, and irrigants during root canal preparation may influence root strength but were not considered in our analysis. Fifthly, in vitro studies inherently encounter challenges in accurately simulating biological, chemical, or physical conditions in vivo [11]. Furthermore, although the present NMA employed VRF resistance as a measure for evaluating root strength, actual oral conditions involve more complex and varied forces in different directions [34]. Finally, the study is limited by the narrow range of obturating system brands included. Future network meta-analyses could address this by categorizing systems according to sealer types, such as bioceramics, resin-based, and ZOE. Careful consideration is warranted when extrapolating the findings of the NMA to the clinical setting. Notwithstanding the aforementioned limitations, the NMA offers valuable guidance for clinicians in formulating evidence-based endodontic treatment strategies.

## Conclusion

In summary, the NMA results revealed that the Resilon/Epiphany system exhibited the highest root strength among the six obturating systems considered, followed by GP/iRootSP and GP/MTA-Plus, both of which are currently available alternatives on the market and demonstrate promising performance in enhancing VRF resistance. In contrast, the GP/ZOE system demonstrated the weakest. The strength of roots filled using all techniques surpassed that of the PC group, except for the Thermafil techniques. Future well-designed clinical trials with large sample sizes are warranted to validate our findings.

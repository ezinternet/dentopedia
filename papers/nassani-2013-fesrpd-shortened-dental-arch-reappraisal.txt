INTRODUCTION
The shortened dental arch (SDA) is "a dentition with a reduction of occlusal units starting
posteriorly." An occlusal unit means "a pair of antagonist teeth that supports the occlusion (e.g.,
premolars and molars)." According to the SDA concept, all treatment efforts should be concentrated on
preserving sound anterior and premolar teeth and avoiding extensive restorative treatment in the molar
regions; occlusion is stabilized and the remaining dentition rehabilitated without replacement of
missing posterior teeth.
Clinical trials showed an SDA comprising anterior and premolar teeth appeared to have, in the long
term, sufficient adaptive capacity for adequate oral function (chewing ability, aesthetics, stability
of the dentition, TMJ function, functional habits). However, in many situations planning according to
the SDA concept is contraindicated; some patients, particularly younger ones, may be reluctant to
leave SDAs unrestored; and there are situations where extending the SDA should be considered (loss of
posterior teeth creating aesthetic problems, occlusal instability, chewing difficulties).
When a decision is made to extend the SDA this can be achieved by a free-end saddle removable partial
denture (FESRPD), cantilevered fixed bridge, or implant-supported prosthesis. Each option has
unsatisfactory elements; the difficulty has been called "a prosthodontic dilemma."

THE PROBLEM OF FREE-END SADDLE REMOVABLE PARTIAL DENTURE
FESRPDs are problematic because of the different responses of teeth and mucosa to occlusal loads.
Support is derived mainly from the mucosa covering the edentulous areas; when shared by remaining
teeth (periodontal membrane) plus saddle mucosa, the MIXED nature of support makes even distribution
of masticatory force difficult, because the mucosa is much more compressible than the periodontal
membrane. Under occlusal load, uneven displacement occurs and the denture may ROTATE around an axis
joining the most distal occlusal rests. These rotational forces affect stability and may generate
damaging forces that in the long term affect abutment teeth and enhance residual ridge resorption.
The problem is greater in the MANDIBLE, where non-tooth support area is far more reduced than in the
maxilla (hard palate distributes load), so the mandibular FESRPD results in relatively more bone loss
in edentulous saddle areas and greater potential to rotate about abutment teeth.
Ritchie: the majority of FESRPDs, especially mandibular dentures, are NOT WORN.

CLINICAL INVESTIGATIONS ON OUTCOME
- Clinical surveys indicate a high failure rate of FESRPDs, especially in the mandible; ultimate
  failure perceived as abandonment of wearing the dentures.
- Elias and Sheiham: great numbers of FESRPDs are not worn because SUBJECTIVE needs for replacement
  are less than NORMATIVE needs — a gap between patients' and dentists' perception.
- Inadequate quality of prescription in designing RPDs for SDA patients has been reported.
- Kayser: prosthodontic intervention to extend the SDA by FESRPD is a kind of "OVERTREATMENT"; does
  not contribute to maintenance of a healthy natural functioning dentition for life, and may introduce
  unfavorable conditions for the remaining dentition.
- Nijmegen group clinical trials: extending an SDA of THREE TO FIVE occlusal units by an FESRPD did
  NOT lead to evident improvement in oral function (chewing ability, oral comfort, aesthetics,
  stability of dentition, TMJ function, functional habits).
- Witter et al.: routine conventional removable prosthodontic intervention as a preventive measure in
  SDAs to avoid occlusal collapse should be DISCOURAGED; SDAs of anterior + premolar teeth maintain
  long-term occlusal stability.
- Molars replaced by FESRPD vs not replaced per SDA concept: NO significant difference in incidence of
  tooth loss at 38 months post-treatment; confirmed over 5-year follow-up.
- Leak et al.: replacement of missing posterior teeth with an RPD appears to have a small
  socio-functional impact until the patient has FEWER THAN THREE posterior functional units.
- RCTs: extending the SDA by cantilevered fixed RESIN-BONDED BRIDGES offered an effective alternative
  to conventional mandibular RPDs at a LOWER BIOLOGICAL PRICE.
- Aras et al.: SDA with bilaterally missing molars can be an effective alternative to FESRPDs for
  masticatory performance despite significant reduction in maximum occlusal force and occlusal contact
  area; NO significant differences in masticatory performance between SDA subjects WITH FESRPDs and
  SDA subjects WITHOUT. In EXTREMELY shortened arches, rehabilitation with FESRPDs DID improve
  masticatory performance and time — but not comparable to completely dentate subjects.
- Creugers et al.: mandibular FESRPDs do not affect temporomandibular function and do not contribute
  to posterior occlusal support in SDAs of three to five occlusal units (minimum one occlusal unit per
  side); such treatment restores only a FRACTION of occlusal support in terms of occlusal contacts.
- A recent systematic review concluded evidence is NOT YET SUFFICIENT to draw conclusions about the
  relative effectiveness of fixed partial dentures vs RPDs in restoring SDAs.

PATIENTS' ATTITUDES TOWARD FESRPDs
- Jepson et al.: patients' compliance with wearing RPDs was significantly related to the presence of
  ANTERIOR replacement teeth; dentures that were NEVER WORN were more likely to have no replacement
  anterior teeth.
- Armellini et al.: RPDs had a positive impact on quality of life of SDA subjects ONLY when the denture
  replaced ANTERIOR teeth.
- Nassani et al.: patients place LITTLE VALUE on both acrylic and metal-based RPDs that replaced
  missing MOLAR teeth in an SDA.
- Ikebe et al.: elderly patients attached higher utility values to cantilevered fixed bridges and metal
  FESRPDs than to implants or no replacement of missing molars for SDAs. CAVEAT: study populations in
  the latter two studies were not SDA subjects and design was based on HYPOTHETICAL clinical scenarios.
- RCT on mandibular SDA restoration: RESIN-BONDED BRIDGES considered a more comfortable and acceptable
  treatment option compared with bilateral FESRPDs.
- Multicenter RCT: restoration of SDAs with RPDs significantly improved OHRQoL (OHIP-49) before
  treatment and at 6 and 12 months after. HOWEVER only 10 OUT OF 17 participants who received RPD
  treatment completed the 12-month follow-up. RPDs were conventional cast framework retained by
  precision attachments. Furthermore, NO significant differences in oral health rating between SDA
  subjects treated by an RPD and subjects treated per the SDA concept (premolar occlusion preserved,
  molar support not replaced).

DISCUSSION
The prognosis of FESRPD is NOT PREDICTABLE and its contribution to oral functions in SDA patients is
considered DUBIOUS. Cantilevered fixed resin-bonded bridges can be considered a more efficient and
comfortable alternative. In some cases extending the SDA by FESRPD may positively affect masticatory
function, patient satisfaction and OHRQoL; however no significant differences in treatment outcome were
recorded between SDA-concept treatment and FESRPD. Restoration by FESRPD incurs MORE costs to deliver
and follow up than functionally oriented treatment per the SDA concept, coupled with increased
biological price.
Despite these findings the FESRPD will probably continue to be preferable when a decision is made to
extend the SDA, attributable to economic considerations — relatively cheap, simple, non-invasive, and
within the skills of most dentists. However long-term maintenance/repair costs and potential for
failure should be considered. A cost-effectiveness study of RPD treatment for SDA patients is required.

WHICH SDA CASES ARE MOST SUITABLE?
- Fueki et al.: significant predictors for SEEKING prosthetic restoration in SDA subjects = young age,
  increased number of missing occlusal units, asymmetric dental arch, existence of chewing complaint.
  Seeking prosthetic restoration was encountered in:
      3% of SDA subjects with missing second molar(s)
     58% of SDA subjects with missing first AND second molars
     93% of SDA subjects with missing premolar(s)
  Perceived impairment of chewing ability due to decreased occlusal units is a decisive factor.
- Witter et al.: extending the SDA by fixed or removable prosthesis depends on degree of shortening and
  the patient's perceived impact on OHRQoL.
      SLIGHTLY shortened (missing molars): NO evidence to support prosthetic extension.
      MODERATELY shortened (missing molars + first premolar): extend in EXCEPTIONAL cases, particularly
        for aesthetic reasons.
      EXTREMELY shortened (missing molars + premolars) and ASYMMETRICAL extremely shortened: SHOULD be
        prosthetically extended — but restoration should be kept to the level of a moderately shortened
        arch, meaning FIXED BRIDGES rather than RPDs may be sufficient.
- Knezovic Zlataric et al.: 205 patients with RPDs. Maxilla 57.4% Kennedy class I, 28.8% class II;
  mandible 75.2% class I, 17.1% class II. NO impact on patients' evaluation of RPD quality from age,
  self-supporting lifestyle, social/economic status, marital status, smoking, chronic disease, or
  period of use. Kennedy classification, denture base material, base shape and denture support did NOT
  significantly impact general satisfaction or comfort. HOWEVER: patients with HIGHER EDUCATION were
  LESS satisfied with aesthetics and hygiene; LESS satisfaction with chewing among MALES with
  mandibular RPDs; the GREATER the number of missing teeth in the mandible, the LESS the oral comfort.

Effectiveness of virtual reality-based toothbrushing instruction on oral health outcomes: A randomized controlled trial
Kwon HJ, Ryu SH, Choi EJ, Lee JA, Kim SJ, Cho HJ. Clin Oral Investig 2026;30(4). PMID 41843223, PMCID PMC12995924, DOI 10.1007/s00784-026-06819-6
Source: PubMed Central full text via PubMed MCP get_full_text_article (tables flattened; figure/reference markers stripped by the API)

ABSTRACT
Background: Oral diseases, significantly driven by dental plaque, remain a major global health concern. Traditional toothbrushing instruction (TBI) methods are often limited in interactivity and long-term skill retention. Recent digital innovations, particularly virtual reality (VR) offer highly interactive educational experiences. This randomized controlled trial (RCT), therefore, aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of VR-based TBI compared to traditional instruction using plaque-disclosing agents.
Methods: This single-blind, two-arm RCT was conducted at a school of dentistry. Healthy adults aged 20-39 years were recruited and randomly allocated using a computer-generated randomization sequence to either a VR group or control group. The VR group received a structured toothbrushing curriculum delivered through immersive VR (Meta Quest 3), whereas the control group received one-on-one instruction from a dental hygienist using plaque-disclosing agents. The primary outcome was the change in plaque index (PI) from baseline to the two weeks follow-up, and secondary outcomes was oral health knowledge-attitude-behavior (KAB) scores. Participants were blinded to group assignment; however, the investigators who performed the assessments were not.
Results: 30 participants were randomized (15 in each group), and all participants were included in the final analysis. The VR group demonstrated a significantly greater mean reduction in PI compared with the control group (0.44 +/- 0.48 vs. 0.07 +/- 0.26; p = 0.012). Both groups showed improvements in KAB scores. Notably, the VR group achieved significantly greater gain in oral health attitude (39.06 +/- 4.80 vs. 38.30 +/- 3.66; p = 0.008) and behavior (4.73 +/- 1.39 vs. 4.53 +/- 1.30; p = 0.004). No harms or unintended events such as dizziness or discomfort was reported.
Conclusion: Immersive VR toothbrushing education improved plaque control and enhanced oral health attitudes and behaviors compared with traditional instruction. This approach may also address the limitations of conventional education, including reliance on professionals and restrictions of time and place, and holds promise for broader public health applications. Further research should examine long-term effects and scalability.
Trial Registration: KCT0010914 (CRIS); retrospectively registered on August 19, 2025.

INTRODUCTION
Oral diseases affect approximately 3.5 billion people worldwide. Dental plaque is a key etiological factor; effective plaque management through consistent, precise toothbrushing is essential. Traditional oral health education relies on verbal instructions, static visual aids and pamphlets, with passive delivery, insufficient engagement and poor skill retention. VR offers interactive, immersive learning and repeated practice. A recent systematic review identified 5 VR-based oral health education studies; most targeted children/adolescents, adult studies mainly involved people with disabilities or healthcare assistants, most assessed subjective outcomes by questionnaire, and control groups were often absent, no-intervention, or weak (verbal instruction); existing VR education mostly used passive viewing via headsets. This study evaluates VR-based TBI in adults in an RCT with a robust control group (traditional instruction using plaque-disclosing agents), with clinical indices (plaque index, bleeding on probing) and behavioral measures (knowledge-attitudes-behaviors survey).

METHODS
Reported per CONSORT 2010.
VR content: developed by dentists, dental hygienists and a content company (X2R, Asan, Korea); Unity engine; Meta Quest 3 with controllers as virtual hands. VR environment resembled a powder room: one wall with a 3D dentition model with synchronized animation and narration; opposite wall a virtual mirror to practice brushing on virtual teeth. Curriculum of 4 sequential modules: (1) Brushing technique - Bass method, brush tilted 45 degrees toward gingival margin; (2) Most posterior molars - thorough brushing of distal molars; (3) Brushing sequence - systematic sequence; (4) Toothbrushing Master session - self-directed brushing with virtual mirror; pink linear plaque along gingival margins removed only when the user executed Bass technique at 45 degrees; simulation started with a 100% plaque gauge that decreased as correct brushing was performed.
Participants and randomization: a priori power analysis with G*Power 3.1.9.4, medium effect size 0.25, 80% power, alpha 0.05, total sample size 28. Online recruitment. Inclusion: adults 20-39 years; >=24 natural teeth (excluding third molars); able to operate a toothbrush independently. Exclusion: uncontrolled systemic disease; acute dental needs or planned scaling within 2 weeks; vestibular/motion-related symptoms; limited manual dexterity; regular electric toothbrush use; current orthodontic appliances; glasses wearers who could not use contact lenses. Randomized 1:1 using Microsoft Excel.
Protocol: single-blind, two-arm RCT at Seoul National University School of Dentistry, one-month period in January 2025. Pre-visit: no toothbrushing for 6 h and no food for 2 h before each visit; no oral hygiene aids from 2 days before first visit.
First visit: surveys (sociodemographics, oral health status, KAB); clinical exam: periodontal pocket depth, BOP, PI with plaque-disclosing agent (Trace, Young Innovations). VR group: toothbrushing education using VR content with minimal researcher intervention; average education time approximately 5 min; then toothbrushing without a mirror; participants were restricted from viewing their disclosed plaque; immediately after education a second PI measurement. Control group: one-on-one TBI from a dental hygienist individualized to plaque, focus on heavily covered areas and common errors, duration approx 5 min; brushed without a mirror; second PI measurement.
All participants then received professional mechanical tooth cleaning with an Airflow device (EMS) to standardize status; standardized soft manual toothbrush (3-row, end-rounded nylon) and 996-ppm fluoride toothpaste provided by the investigators.
Intervention period: two weeks; brush as taught; usual toothpaste and toothbrush (as stated in the paper); oral hygiene aids and all dental visits including scaling prohibited.
Second visit (2 weeks): follow-up surveys; VR group also satisfaction and dizziness surveys; clinical exam: BOP and third PI measurement.
Outcomes: PI by Turesky modification of Quigley-Hein index (0-5), 6 sites per tooth (mesial, middle, distal; buccal and palatal/lingual). BOP with UNC probe at same 6 sites, present 1/absent 0, across the 28 teeth. Questionnaires: demographics; oral health behaviors survey; KAB survey (8 knowledge, 9 attitude, 7 behavior items) adapted from previous studies and translated into Korean; VR group also Dizziness Handicap Inventory and modified 20-item System Usability Scale.
Blinding: participants blinded to group (study described as "Comparison of various TBI methods"; VR equipment concealed from control group). Blinding of investigators not feasible.
Analysis: SPSS 19.0; independent t-tests; chi-square for categorical; repeated measures ANOVA for PI, BOP and KAB; Bonferroni post-hoc; between-group change in PI baseline to 2 weeks by independent t-test; KAB knowledge and behavior items scored 1/0, attitude items 5-point Likert with reverse scoring; Cronbach's alpha for KAB and SUS; p<0.05.
Ethics: IRB No. S-D20230019, Seoul National University School of Dentistry.

RESULTS
32 recruited; 1 did not meet inclusion criteria and 1 declined; 30 enrolled, randomized equally (n=15 each). Mean age 24.93 (SD 3.49); VR 23.93 (2.19), control 25.93 (4.28), p=0.12. Sex: 15 male/15 female. Brushing frequency once/day 1 (3%), twice 21 (70%), thrice 8 (27%), p=0.46. Interdental brush use 3/30 (10%), floss use 12/30 (40%).
Plaque index (mean, SD): baseline control 2.94 (0.25), VR 2.92 (0.34). Immediately after education: control 2.34 (0.26), VR 2.12 (0.35). 2-week follow-up: control 2.87 (0.38), VR 2.47 (0.58). PI difference baseline minus 2 weeks: control 0.07 (0.26), VR 0.44 (0.48); t(28)=2.68, p=0.012. Repeated measures ANOVA showed significant PI change over time within groups (p=0.024).
BOP: no significant change within or between groups; control 0.04 to 0.04; VR 0.05 to 0.05; p=0.785.
Questionnaires: Cronbach alpha KAB 0.65; modified SUS 0.91. No participants reported dizziness symptoms, including mild cases. Knowledge: control 7.00 (1.30) to 7.40 (1.12); VR 6.90 (1.16) to 7.53 (0.74); p=0.583. Attitude: control 37.70 (3.08) to 38.30 (3.66); VR 35.40 (5.36) to 39.06 (4.80); p=0.008. Behavior: control 3.87 (1.25) to 4.53 (1.30); VR 2.53 (1.25) to 4.73 (1.39); p=0.004.

DISCUSSION
At 2 weeks the VR group demonstrated a significant reduction in PI compared with control (text cites p=0.024 in the Discussion; the between-group change test in Results is p=0.012). Authors speculate results stem from VR's educational affordances (realism, reflective observation, immediate feedback, repeated practice) rather than mere use of VR. No BOP difference, possibly due to short intervention period. Knowledge did not change significantly: high baseline (max 8 points) and intervention targeted practical skills. Attitude improvement in both groups may partly reflect the Hawthorne effect and social desirability bias. Behavior score improved significantly more in VR (Discussion text states p<0.001; Table 5 reports p=0.004).
Comparison with prior work: RCT of 90 female high school students - VR oral health education reduced plaque (p<0.01) and gingival indices (p=0.017) vs verbal instruction or no intervention; children 9-12 years - VR increased flossing, tongue brushing and daily brushing frequency (p<0.001), knowledge not improved (p=0.550); preschool children immersive VR better behavioral outcomes (p=0.004); a recent SR of 9 immersive technology education studies concluded VR consistently improved knowledge, attitudes, self-efficacy and hygiene behaviors.
Strengths: dental-team-developed interactive content; robust control (TBI with plaque-disclosing agents); RCT with participant blinding; first study to evaluate both clinical outcomes (PI, BOP) and KAB in adults after VR TBI; can be delivered independently once set up (scalability).
Limitations: small sample and short observation period; examiners not fully blinded; requires specialized, costly hardware/software; potential VR side effects (none reported here).
Conclusions: VR-based TBI vs traditional TBI with plaque-disclosing agents significantly improved plaque control, oral health behaviors and attitudes among adults; short duration limited measurable change in gingival inflammation. Future research: larger groups, longer follow-up, rigorous blinding.

Title: Defining Porphyromonas gingivalis strains associated with periodontal disease
Authors: Murugaiyan V, Utreja S, Hovey KM, Sun Y, LaMonte MJ, Wactawski-Wende J, Diaz PI, Buck MJ
Journal: Scientific Reports. 2024;14(1):6222
DOI: 10.1038/s41598-024-56849-x
PMID: 38485747
PMCID: PMC10940620
Source: PubMed Central full text (retrieved via PubMed MCP get_full_text_article)

ABSTRACT

Porphyromonas gingivalis, a Gram-negative anaerobic bacterium commonly found in human subgingival plaque, is a major etiologic agent for periodontitis and has been associated with multiple systemic pathologies. Many P. gingivalis strains have been identified and different strains possess different virulence factors. Current oral microbiome approaches (16S or shotgun) have been unable to differentiate P. gingivalis strains. This study presents a new approach that aims to improve the accuracy of strain identification, using a detection method based on sequencing of the intergenic spacer region (ISR) which is variable between P. gingivalis strains. Our approach uses two-step PCR to amplify only the P. gingivalis ISR region. Samples are then sequenced with an Illumina sequencer and mapped to specific strains. Our approach was validated by examining subgingival plaque from 153 participants with and without periodontal disease. We identified the avirulent strain ATCC33277/381 as the most abundant strain across all sample types. The W83/W50 strain was significantly enriched in periodontitis, with 13% of participants harboring that strain. Overall, this approach can have significant implications not only for the diagnosis and treatment of periodontal disease but also for other diseases where P. gingivalis or its toxins have been implicated, such as Alzheimer's disease.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontitis is among the most common infections in the United States, with an estimated prevalence of 42.2% for adults aged 30 years or older, and 7.8% of individuals experience severe periodontitis. Untreated periodontitis can lead to impaired oral function with eventual tooth loss and an overall reduced quality of life. Periodontitis has been associated with a variety of systemic disorders such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, Alzheimer's disease, respiratory tract infection, and adverse pregnancy outcomes.

Periodontitis has been characterized as a dysbiotic disease owing to a shift in the subgingival microbial communities that colonize the periodontal pockets from a predominantly Gram-positive aerobic bacteria, to a dominance of Gram-negative anaerobes. The most notable are the so-called "red-complex" bacteria. A growing body of literature suggests P. gingivalis, the keystone pathogen in chronic periodontitis, has implications in the onset of different systemic pathologies, including rheumatoid arthritis, cardiovascular disease, and neurodegenerative pathologies. A recent study provides evidence of presence of gingipains, a toxic protease produced by P. gingivalis, in the brain of Alzheimer's patients suggesting P. gingivalis may play a critical role in the etiology of Alzheimer's disease.

P. gingivalis is found in healthy individuals as well as in patients with chronic periodontal disease. Several characterization studies have examined the genetic diversity among P. gingivalis strains and their varying potency to cause periodontal diseases. Approaches including multilocus enzyme electrophoresis, RAPD fingerprinting, and MLS analysis have separated P. gingivalis into 41-73 different strains.

Currently, there are 67 unique P. gingivalis genomes in the NCBI database. Experimentally, strains of P. gingivalis vary in the manifestation of measured virulence traits. For example, subcutaneous injection of certain strains, including W83, can cause spreading and ulcerating lesions at distant sites whereas others produce only small, localized abscesses. Platelet activation, collagenase activity, and macrophage avoidance have all been shown to vary by strain.

Current microbiome approaches involve sequencing of the hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA gene or shotgun sequencing of all isolated DNA. Both approaches are limited in their ability to detect bacterial subspecies or strains. 16S sequencing approaches completely miss strain differences. Shotgun sequencing is not practical for the oral microbiome because ~90% of DNA obtained from the mouth is from the human host, making adequate strain-level sequencing depth cost-prohibitive, especially since P. gingivalis abundance in subgingival plaque ranges from only 5% to 0.1% even in moderate-to-severe periodontal disease.

To determine P. gingivalis strains cost-effectively, the authors adapted an approach examining the intergenic spacer region (ISR) between the 16S and 23S ribosomal subunit genes, which is highly variable among strains. They identified an information-rich region within the P. gingivalis ISR and generated specific primers targeting only P. gingivalis, flanking a region containing strain-identifying differences, validated on subgingival plaque from 153 participants with and without periodontal disease.

RESULTS

Specificity, sensitivity, reproducibility of ISR-specific primer
Two primer sets were identified; the more informative set amplified P. gingivalis DNA (~215-250 bp) specifically in participant samples and positive controls (ATCC33277 strain, pooled subgingival plaque), with no amplification in negative controls (Zymo mock DNA, microbial-DNA-free water). Sensitivity was confirmed by spike-in experiments (1 ng and 2 ng ATCC33277 DNA in Zymo mock DNA background) — amplification only occurred in spiked reactions, confirming specificity is not inhibited by other microbial DNA.

Cohort: Buffalo Osteoporosis and Periodontal Disease (OsteoPerio) Study — a prospective cohort of postmenopausal women (ancillary to the Women's Health Initiative Observational Study), previously characterized by 16S rRNA sequencing. 161 subgingival plaque samples were examined (12 plaque pools, 2 positive controls, 6 negative controls); after QC, analysis covered samples from 153 participants.

ISR DNA concentration was significantly greater in moderate and severe periodontal disease groups than in the none/mild group, consistent with higher P. gingivalis abundance in more severe disease. Sequence reads were mapped to a custom-built ISR reference database (67 unique P. gingivalis genomes) using DADA2.

Detection of P. gingivalis strains
139 unique ISR sequences were detected. Strains with identical ISR sequence in this region cannot be distinguished (e.g., W83 and W50 are named together as "W83_W50"). Two positive control samples showed 99.99% and 100% of reads mapping correctly to ATCC33277, and 5 technical replicates (split before DNA extraction) were highly concordant, demonstrating reproducibility. Most participant samples were dominated by two different strains; for most samples regardless of disease state, the single top strain represented >90% of all P. gingivalis reads. Alpha diversity (Shannon, Chao1) did not differ significantly by periodontal disease status.

W83/W50 is associated with periodontal disease
Samples were grouped as None/Mild vs Moderate/Severe periodontal disease. After filtering low-abundance/singleton strains, 18 P. gingivalis strains were analyzed. The most abundant strain overall was the avirulent ATCC33277_381 (CLR 6.38 in none/mild vs CLR 5.34 in moderate/severe — nonsignificant trend toward enrichment in healthier group). 15 of 18 strains were numerically more abundant in the Moderate/Severe group, but only strain W83_W50 reached significance after multiple-testing correction: detected exclusively in the moderate/severe group, present in 13% of participants in that group, CLR-abundance significantly higher in moderate/severe (p = 0.0003; q = 0.005).

Linear correlations between CLR-transformed strain abundance and whole-mouth mean pocket depth (PD) / clinical attachment level (CAL) ranged from -0.134 to 0.142 (PD) and -0.095 to 0.152 (CAL); none were significant after multiple-testing correction.

DISCUSSION

The ISR-sequencing method addresses two limitations of prior microbiome methods: (1) low P. gingivalis abundance relative to host DNA background, and (2) the need for strain-discriminating sequence variability captured across all samples in one assay. W83/W50 — previously shown by heteroduplex typing and multiple prior studies to be more virulent and strongly associated with periodontitis — was the only strain significantly enriched in Moderate/Severe disease in this cohort. ATCC33277, proposed as a lower-virulence/"healthier" strain, was the most abundant strain overall with a nonsignificant trend toward enrichment in None/Mild disease.

Limitations: (1) a substantial number of P. gingivalis strains share identical ISR sequences in the targeted region and cannot be discriminated by this assay alone (would require additional primers in virulence genes); (2) strain assignment depends on matching to a reference database of characterized ISR sequences, which will improve as more strains are sequenced; (3) low-complexity amplicon libraries on Illumina platforms require substantial spike-in/dephasing-base strategies or multiplexing with high-complexity libraries to maintain base-call quality.

METHODS

153 postmenopausal women from the OsteoPerio ancillary study (Buffalo, NY clinical center of the WHI Observational Study) with complete clinical periodontal exams (PD, CAL per CDC/AAP criteria: none/mild/moderate/severe). Subgingival plaque collected with paper points in Ringer's solution, frozen at -80°C. Metagenomic DNA extracted via QIAsymphony SP with an enzymatic Gram-positive lysis pretreatment. Two-step PCR generated ISR amplicon libraries (P. gingivalis-specific primers flanking the strain-discriminating ISR region) with dual-index Illumina adapters; Zymo mock DNA and microbial-DNA-free water as negative controls, ATCC33277 as positive control. Sequencing on Illumina MiSeq (2×300), multiplexed with shotgun microbiome samples at 1:5 ratio to offset low library complexity. DADA2 pipeline (primer trimming via Cutadapt, quality filtering Q<30 removed, error-rate learning, dereplication, ASV inference, chimera removal) generated an ASV table mapped against the custom ISR reference database (67 P. gingivalis genomes). Novel unmatched ISR sequences were named "PG-Strain #" and characterized by BLAST similarity. CLR (centered log-ratio) transformation applied for compositional-data-appropriate abundance comparison; discrete FDR correction for multiple testing; Pearson correlations for CLR-strain-abundance vs. PD/CAL; alpha diversity and univariate strain-enrichment tests via MicrobiomeAnalyst. All participants gave informed consent; study approved by University at Buffalo Health Sciences IRB.

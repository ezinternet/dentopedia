Expert consensus on the diagnosis and treatment of cemental tear

Liang Ye, Liu Hongrui, Xie Chengjia, Yu Yang, Shao Jinlong, Lv Chunxu, Kang Wenyan, ... Zhou Xuedong, Ge Shaohua (multi-institution Chinese expert consensus group)

International Journal of Oral Science (2025) 17:61
DOI: 10.1038/s41368-025-00381-9
PMID: 40846694 | PMCID: PMC12373928
Source: PubMed Central full text (get_full_text_article)

===== ABSTRACT =====

Cemental tear is a rare and indetectable condition unless obvious clinical signs present with the involvement of surrounding periodontal and periapical tissues. Due to its clinical manifestations similar to common dental issues, such as vertical root fracture, primary endodontic diseases, and periodontal diseases, as well as the low awareness of cemental tear for clinicians, misdiagnosis often occurs. The critical principle for cemental tear treatment is to remove torn fragments, and overlooking fragments leads to futile therapy, which could deteriorate the conditions of the affected teeth. Therefore, accurate diagnosis and subsequent appropriate interventions are vital for managing cemental tear. Novel diagnostic tools, including cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), microscopes, and enamel matrix derivatives, have improved early detection and management, enhancing tooth retention. The implementation of standardized diagnostic criteria and treatment protocols, combined with improved clinical awareness among dental professionals, serves to mitigate risks of diagnostic errors and suboptimal therapeutic interventions. This expert consensus reviewed the epidemiology, pathogenesis, potential predisposing factors, clinical manifestations, diagnosis, differential diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis of cemental tear, aiming to provide a clinical guideline and facilitate clinicians to have a better understanding of cemental tear.

===== FULL TEXT =====

Introduction

Cemental tear can be an incomplete or complete detachment of the cementum along the cemento-dentinal junction (CDJ) or a partial detachment along the incremental lines within the body of the cementum. It is a specific type of root surface fracture involving cementum; sometimes, the tear also involves dentin, termed cemento-dentinal tear. There are no specific clinical symptoms at the early stage of cemental tear forming; surrounding tissue is gradually destroyed with progression resulting from the low-grade inflammation induced by mechanical stimulation of torn fragments, leading to sinus tract, isolated deep periodontal pocket, purulent discharge, occlusal pain, etc. The diagnosis of cemental tear is challenging because of its clinical presentations similar to vertical root fracture (VRF), periodontitis, and apical periodontitis. In addition, low awareness of cemental tears can also lead to misdiagnosis and unnecessary treatment, even for experienced clinicians, posing significant clinical dilemmas. Therefore, a clinical guideline is needed for improving cemental tear management, and this consensus aims to summarize recent progress on the epidemiology, pathogenesis, possible predisposing factors, clinical presentation, diagnosis, differential diagnosis, treatment, and treatment outcomes of cemental tear and to help clinicians enhance awareness of cemental tear and develop feasible treatment plans.

Epidemiology of cemental tear

Most literature on cemental tear consists of case reports and reviews, and epidemiological analysis is limited. Currently, only two articles based on radiographic images have reported the prevalence of cemental tear. Keskin et al. conducted a retrospective observational study based on periapical radiographs of 4,629 permanent teeth of 1,451 adult patients in Turkey reporting a prevalence of 0.89%, while Özkan et al. reported a prevalence of 1.9% by assessment of 813 cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) images in another region of Turkey. More comprehensive information with a three-dimensional (3D) perspective in CBCT images which can facilitate detecting cemental tear compared with periapical images, as well as the different study populations and sample sizes, may result in the different prevalence in the above two studies. However, the diagnosis of cemental tear in both studies was verified by radiography alone and lack of clinical examination, and further studies with larger sample sizes in different regions should be conducted to understand the prevalence of cemental tear comprehensively. There was no report on the incidence of cemental tear till now; perhaps its rare occurrence in clinics and low awareness of this disease hinder the data collection.

Histology of cementum

Cementum is the mineralized tissue similar to bone covering the entire root surface, contributing to periodontal support along with periodontal ligament and alveolar bone. Unlike bone, cementum has no blood vessels, Haversian canals, or nerves, and it is resistant to normal remodeling and resorption. Cementum is thinner near the cervical part of the root, about 20 to 50 µm thick, and about 150 to 200 µm thick at the root apex, and its thickness gradually increases with age due to the lack of dynamic remodeling. Cementum can be histologically classified into cellular and acellular cementum by the inclusion or absence of cementocytes. Generally, acellular cementum is thinner and covers the cervical part of the root, while cellular cementum is thicker and covers the apical part of the root. Additionally, cementum contains two types of fibers: extrinsic and intrinsic. Extrinsic fibers are secreted by fibroblasts and embedded in the periodontal ligament, and intrinsic fibers are laid down only by cementoblasts and located within the cementum. Based on the existence of cementocytes and the different structures of cementum, Schroeder proposed the current classifications of cementum.

Cementum is a complex of collagen fibers and calcified substances connecting dentin and periodontal ligament, except AAC (acellular afibrillar cementum). According to the surrounding environment or requirement (adaptation and tooth anchorage), different types of cementum are generated, i.e. AEFC (acellular extrinsic fiber cementum), CMSC (cellular mixed stratified cementum), and CIFC (cellular intrinsic fiber cementum). As an interconnection system, cementum is continuous with periodontal ligament by extrinsic fibers, and it is attached to dentin via a 100–200 µm thick interface (CDJ) within which a 10–50 µm wide hygroscopic proteoglycan (PG)-rich layer exists. Within the CDJ, collagen fibers from cementum that transverse radially to the dentin are tied with PGs, which differ from the interconnection between cementum and periodontal ligament.

Pathogenesis and possible predisposing factors of cemental tear

The etiology of cemental tear is unclear, and two mechanisms have been proposed for the formation and progression of cemental tear, namely internal and external factors. Internal factors are mainly innate and related to the inherent structural weakness of cementum. On the one hand, the connective tissue at CDJ contains many glycoproteins, especially bone sialoprotein and osteopontin, which contributes to the weaker fibrillar interconnection between cementum and dentin than the connection between cementum and periodontal ligament, as these glycoproteins may disrupt the continuity of fibers connecting different tissues. On the other hand, the structural weakness of the secondary cementum, consisting of cementocytes in the lacunae and intrinsic collagen fibers, also predisposes it to be detached. External factors are mainly related to repeated stress from occlusal trauma or sudden stress from dental trauma.

Once a cemental tear forms, the torn fragment mechanically irritates adjacent periodontal tissues under functional occlusal load, inducing persistent low-grade inflammation. With the progression of inflammation, alveolar bone defects gradually enlarge, eventually creating periodontal pockets or sinus tracts that communicate directly with the oral cavity. These pathological spaces facilitate plaque accumulation and retention, thus exacerbating periodontal and periapical tissue destruction. Additionally, microbial pathogens can continuously invade through exposed dentinal tubules, accessory canals, or lateral canals, potentially leading to pulp inflammation. Notably, when the cemental tear occurs in the apical region without communication to the oral cavity, the periapical inflammation typically remains sterile, unless there is concomitant primary root canal infection.

Current evidence indicates several possible predisposing factors, including age, gender, systemic condition, tooth type, occlusal trauma, history of periodontitis, and history of dental trauma. Current evidence derives primarily from case reports or retrospective series (Oxford CEBM Level 4 evidence).

Age: Cemental tear is highly prevalent in individuals over 60 years old, possibly related to the gradual thickening of cementum with age. The thickness of cementum in the elderly can be 3–5 times greater than in the youth. The hygroscopic activity of CDJ decreases over time because of mineral formation across the CDJ. With increasing age, the adhesion strength of glycoproteins at CDJ also decreases, and the collagen that limits the stretching of periodontal ligament fibers weakens, which may lead to the detachment of cementum from the dentin surface.

Gender: A higher proportion of teeth with cemental tear in males (77.5%) compared with females (22.5%) was reported in Lin's multicenter retrospective study (71 teeth, Taiwan), possibly due to greater occlusal forces. Lee et al. reviewed 41 published cases and revealed a larger percentage (63%) of male patients. However, no significant correlation between gender and cemental tear was found in Keskin's or Özkan's studies.

Systemic condition: A special case report of one individual with 14 affected teeth suggested that systemic conditions such as malnutrition and aplastic anemia (impairing cementoblast function to secrete intrinsic fibers) may be associated with cemental tear, especially for cases with multiple affected teeth.

Tooth type: Previous studies suggested cemental tear largely occurred in single-rooted teeth, especially incisors. A recent retrospective study reported it was more prevalent in molars, particularly the palatal roots of maxillary molars and the mesial roots of mandibular molars (CBCT application may explain the difference). Cemental tear often occurs in the middle and apical third of the root.

Occlusal trauma or excessive occlusal force: A potential contributing factor, classified as primary (excess/abnormal forces on healthy periodontium — bruxism, premature contacts, prosthesis on antagonist, severe wear, abutment teeth) and secondary (normal/excessive forces on teeth with inadequate periodontal support, typically periodontitis patients). An in vitro study demonstrated cementum cracks developed in weak, stress-concentrated areas such as the cervix under repeated/strong stress, progressing to the apex with disease development.

History of periodontitis and periodontal therapy: Periodontal diseases affect ~20%–50% of the world's population. In periodontitis, cementum shows decreased thickness, mineralization, and degradation of collagen fibers (SEM). These are linked to prolonged exposure to proteolytic enzymes (host-derived collagenase and matrix metalloproteinases) plus bacterial byproducts. Thin, less-mineralized cementum can be damaged or removed easily during mechanical treatment.

History of dental trauma: Although not a significant risk factor in Lin's study, excessive forces from trauma can separate cementum from the root surface; 9.5%–10% of cemental tear cases have a history of dental trauma. A possible modality is direct damage to periodontal tissues when overloaded forces are placed on teeth used as an anchorage/fulcrum during adjacent tooth extraction. A case in an autotransplanted tooth suggests a possible association with previous extraction procedures.

Clinical manifestations of cemental tear

Clinically, the cemental tear typically presents as hard tissue fragments either completely or partially detached from the root surface, appearing as sheet-like, thin, prickle-like, or tear-like pieces. Although some cases may be asymptomatic and discovered incidentally, they are more commonly associated with periodontal and/or periapical tissue destruction, as well as signs of occlusal trauma. Common clinical signs include gingival or mucosal swelling, isolated deep periodontal pockets, sinus tract or periodontal suppuration, and often a poor response to conventional root canal treatment (RCT). Exploring along the root surface may reveal a hard, ledge-like projection suggestive of a cemental tear. Additional findings may include bleeding or suppuration on probing, localized rapid attachment loss, increased tooth mobility, and distinct signs of occlusal trauma such as fremitus, wear facets, or premature contacts. Cemental tears may occur in multiple teeth, and in some cases, may recur on the same tooth.

Radiographical manifestations of cemental tear

The typical X-ray appearance of cemental tear is "prickle-like" or "flake-like" (fine, sharp, and vertical fragments) radiopaque mass near the affected root surface. The radiolucent lesion indicates surrounding alveolar bone resorption. Radiolucent lesions associated with cemental tear can involve the alveolar crest and apex; periodontal and periapical lesions have been reported in approximately 86% and 65% of cases, respectively. Traditional X-rays have limitations in observing 3D objects because biplanar imaging cannot detect cemental tear on the labial (buccal) or lingual (palatal) surface. When a cemental tear is suspected, small field-of-view CBCT is the preferred examination method, as it improves the detection rate and discloses position and extent from a 3D perspective.

Histopathological manifestations of cemental tear

Histopathological examination of the suspected fragments removed during scaling and root planing or surgery is the gold standard for diagnosis. Histologically, cemental fragments contain cellular and/or acellular cementum. Torn fragments were often detached at CDJ (77.6%) or within the cementum (22.4%). Occasionally, torn fragments may include portions of dentin (cemento-dentinal tear), causing more severe damage and resulting in non-retainable teeth if too much dentin is involved. Some detached fragments demonstrate obvious periodontal ligament attachment. Torn fragments are often partially or completely surrounded by soft tissues, of which 92.3% was granulation tissue and only 7.7% was cystic. Many cases involve focal destruction of the lamina dura and surrounding medullary bone.

Diagnosis of cemental tear

Cemental tear lacks specific clinical signs and symptoms, making diagnosis particularly challenging. Accurate identification requires: detailed medical and dental history collection; comprehensive clinical examination (especially probing for hard, step-like projections on the root surface); and radiographic assessment. CBCT is particularly valuable in detecting detached cemental fragments. Histopathological examination of retrieved fragments remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis.

Medical and dental history collection: assess general systemic health and chief dental complaints; inquire about parafunctional habits (bruxism/clenching), hard-food consumption, pain during mastication, bleeding during brushing, and any history of periodontal or root canal treatment; note history of dental trauma or prosthetic procedures.

Clinical examination:
- Occlusal examination: determine traumatic occlusion (visual inspection, palpation, articulating paper).
- Periodontal condition: oral hygiene, gingival margin/condition, root surface irregularities, bleeding/suppuration on probing, probing depth (PD), attachment loss, tooth mobility. Detection of root surface irregularities (rough, jagged, uneven with a sharp explorer) is important; periodontal endoscopy is preferred to disclose detached fragments.
- Tooth hard tissues, pulp, and periapical condition: sinus tract, wear, caries, defects, restorations, cracks; pulp status by cold/heat/electric tests; periapical abnormalities by vertical percussion. For a tooth with a sinus tract, suspect cemental tear if no endodontic disease/failed endodontic treatment is present, especially for teeth with vital pulp.

Auxiliary examination:
- Radiographic examination: detection of a "prickle-like"/"flake-like" radiopaque mass near the root surface is the critical evidence. Differential of the radiopacity includes lamina dura and residual alveolar bone; in cemental tear these fragments appear attached to or separate from the root surface, located within a radiolucent lesion. CBCT preferred.
- Methylene blue dye staining: helps identify the boundary of cemental tear and detect invisible cracks during surgery/intentional replantation, aiding complete removal.
- Histopathological examination: gold standard. Fragments obtained during scaling, root planing, periodontal/periapical surgery. Exploratory surgery may be required but risks complications — weigh benefits/risks; avoid unnecessary surgery in hopeless teeth.

Differential diagnosis of cemental tear

Most clinical features overlap with VRF, primary endodontic diseases, and primary periodontal diseases.
- VRF: tooth extraction is often the most predictable treatment due to poor prognosis; cemental-tear teeth usually remain functional after appropriate treatment. VRF is more commonly associated with root-filled teeth; cemental tear usually does not affect pulp (affected teeth mainly vital if no previous RCT/apical involvement). Radiographically, cemental tear = thin radiopaque fragments along root surface; VRF = vertical separation of root structure with a radiolucent line between root filling and root surface.
- Primary endodontic diseases: sinus tract common to both, but pulp can remain vital in cemental tear. Careful pulp vitality evaluation avoids futile RCT. "Flake-like" radiopaque mass separated from the root surface aids differentiation, especially for teeth with previous adequate RCT but no relief.
- Periodontal diseases: deep periodontal pocket, suppuration, bleeding on probing common to both. Periodontal disease usually involves multiple teeth and generalized bone loss vs localized lesions on specific root surfaces in cemental tear. Detection of ledge-like projections on the root surface helps distinguish cemental tear from periodontal abscess.

Clinical management of cemental tear

Principal objectives: thoroughly remove torn fragments and infected tissues, and reconstruct the structure and function of affected tissues. Modalities: periodic medical follow-ups, occlusal adjustment, scaling and root planing, RCT, surgical treatment, and tooth extraction. Treatment strategy is based on the location of the torn fragment, severity of associated periodontal/periapical lesions, and patient preference. A treatment decision tree is proposed based on periodontal/periapical lesion condition and accessibility of torn fragments. For cemental tear in the cervical third of the root, complete removal can sometimes be achieved through scaling and root planing alone; if fragments/infectious tissues remain, surgical treatments are required (periodontal surgery, apical surgery, regenerative therapy, intentional replantation, hemisection, root amputation).

Current treatment modalities:
- Periodic medical follow-ups: no active intervention for patients with only radiographic findings and no clinical signs/symptoms; inform patient and schedule regular follow-ups; intervene if progression appears.
- Occlusal adjustment: necessary when the affected tooth has traumatic occlusion or pronounced looseness; conducive to clinical periodontal attachment gain. Necessary tooth splinting for stabilizing mobile teeth should be considered before surgical intervention and regenerative periodontal therapy.
- Subgingival scaling and root planing: removes torn cementum completely reachable through periodontal pockets (locking pliers may aid removal of separated fragments); dental operating microscope / periodontal endoscope improves visualization. Only 28.6% of cases were cured after non-surgical periodontal treatment (limited visualization / narrow space). Periodontal re-evaluation after 1–2 months is essential; consider surgery if no improvement.
- RCT: unnecessary for teeth without root apex involvement (test pulp vitality carefully). Cemental tear in the apical third usually accompanies periapical lesions, which may cause pulp necrosis when communicating with the oral cavity; apical resection may undermine apical neurovascular supply — adequate RCT then required.

Surgical therapy:
- Periodontal surgery: required when cemental tear occurs in the middle/apical third of the root, or a deep pocket (PD > 5 mm) persists after non-surgical treatment. Through surgery, 57.7% of cases healed (including repair of bone defects). Microscopes/microsurgical instruments and root-surface smoothing with diamond-coated burs aid thorough removal. Cemental tear is often accompanied by intrabony defects; regenerative surgery decisions:
  - Vertical depth: shallow intrabony defects (<3 mm) — bone regeneration difficult, osseous resective surgery suitable; deep intrabony defects (≥3 mm) — regenerative surgery with enamel matrix derivative (EMD) or guided tissue regeneration (GTR) shows significant improvement vs open flap.
  - Width / bony walls: narrow 3-wall (<2 mm width) — bone grafts, GTR, or biological factors separately; wide 3-wall (≥3 mm width) and 2-wall — bone grafts combined with GTR or biological factors (EMD); 1-wall — combination of bone grafts + biological factors + GTR. Narrow two-wall or three-wall intrabony defects show the most significant regeneration potential. Esthetic risk assessment for anterior teeth.
- Apical surgery: minimally invasive apical surgery to completely remove fragments and infectious tissues at the apical region. Regenerative therapy favorable if periapical bone defect diameter ≥10 mm or residual buccal bone plate height ≤3 mm. For through-and-through (buccal-to-lingual) defects, combined bone grafts + regenerative membrane show favorable prognosis. Apico-marginal lesions are the most challenging.
- Intentional replantation, hemisection, root amputation: intentional replantation is feasible when the affected tooth is near important vascular/neural structures and/or access is difficult. Hemisection or root amputation may be valid for multi-rooted teeth.
- Tooth extraction: for teeth with poor/hopeless prognosis (severe bone resorption, extensive involvement) or strong patient preference — early extraction can avoid further bone destruction. Alveolar ridge preservation or immediate implantation preserves soft/hard tissues; minimally invasive extraction avoids pressure on thin labial/lingual bone.

Treatment outcome of cemental tear

Complete removal of torn fragments is crucial. 94% of treated teeth after cemental tear removal were functionally retained at 1-year follow-up; active intervention is recommended. Outcome depends on the coronal-apical location of torn fragments, with healing rates of 60%, 66.7%, and 11.1% for tears in the coronal third, middle third, and apical third of the root, respectively. Higher healing rate with surgical intervention vs non-surgical treatment alone.

Conclusion

The cemental tear is a partial or complete detachment of the cementum from the CDJ or along the incremental line within the body of the cementum, in some cases with adjacent bone resorption. Mechanism = internal factors (weakness of CDJ and cementum) + external factors (stress applied to cementum, like occlusal trauma). Possible predisposing factors: age, gender, tooth type, occlusal trauma, history of periodontitis/periodontal treatment, and history of dental trauma. Preliminary diagnosis mainly relies on radiographic examination (CBCT preferred). Complete removal of torn fragments is essential for tissue healing, and regenerative therapy is favorable for suitable cases. Successful treatment is feasible if correctly diagnosed; referral to endodontists and/or periodontal specialists is highly recommended if necessary.

1. Introduction
Commercially pure titanium and titanium alloys have long been 
considered the gold standard dental implant materials owing to 
their superior biocompatibility, corrosion resistance, and mechanical 
properties, all of which contribute to their high long-term clinical 
success rates[1–3]. However, recent reports have highlighted immu -
nological reactions and hypersensitivity related to the release of tita-
nium particles[4–6], as well as esthetic concerns caused by titanium 
color visible through the soft tissue, particularly in patients with thin 
mucosa[7, 8]. Consequently, zirconia implants have attracted atten -
tion as potential alternatives to titanium and its alloys[ 9 –11]. Among 
these, yttria-stabilized tetragonal zirconia polycrystals (Y-TZP) have 
emerged as a promising implant material due to their high mechani-
cal strength, fracture toughness, excellent biocompatibility, and 
superior esthetics[9,12–17]. Furthermore, zirconia has been shown to 
exhibit lower bacterial adhesion than titanium, potentially reducing 
plaque accumulation and the risk of peri-implant diseases[ 9,16,18]. 
Dental implants are broadly classified into one-or two-piece types. 
One-piece zirconia implants, in which the implant body and abut -
ment are integrated, were initially more prevalent. However, their 
limited prosthetic flexibility and constraints in immediate restora -
tion or angle correction have led to the increased use of two-piece 
zirconia implants in recent years. These systems connect the implant

Abstract
Purpose: This study evaluates the effects of different abutment screw tightening protocols and implant materials on 
screw loosening and morphological changes after cyclic torsional loading of two-piece implants.
Methods: Titanium (T) and zirconia (Z) two-piece implants were used. Abutment screws were tightened to 35 Ncm. Three 
protocols were tested: (A) retightening after 5 min, (B) immediate retightening, and (C) no retightening (n = 6 each). All 
samples underwent 100,000 cycles of cyclic torsional loading. Removal torque values before and after loading (RT-pre 
and RT-post) and torque loss percentage (TL) were calculated. Surface morphology was assessed using scanning electron 
microscopy (SEM), and dimensional changes were quantified using root mean square (RMS) values from three-dimensional 
data.
Results: In the titanium group, protocol A (TA) had lower RT-post values than protocols B (TB) and C (TC) ( P < 0.001), and 
TB was lower than TC (P < 0.05). In the zirconia group, protocol A (ZA) was significantly lower than protocols B (ZB) and C 
(ZC) (P < 0.05, P < 0.001, respectively). RT-post and RT-pre were significantly lower in the zirconia group than in the titanium 
group under all conditions ( P < 0.001). No differences were observed in TL. SEM revealed wear on the screw heads and 
threads in all groups. RMS values were significantly lower in the zirconia group (P < 0.05).
Conclusions: Abutment screw retightening does not consistently enhance torque maintenance under torsional loading. 
The implant material affects torque retention and deformation, where titanium implants demonstrating greater stability 
than zirconia implants.

have reported favorable long-term outcomes of two-piece zirconia 
implants. In a prospective clinical trial with a mean follow-up of 80 
months, no significant differences in implant survival were observed 
between zirconia and titanium implants[ 22]. Similarly, a retrospec -

findings suggest that two-piece zirconia implants may achieve long-
term clinical outcomes comparable with those of titanium implants. 
However, in two-piece systems, the mechanical stability of the as -
sembly may be affected by the interface design and combination 
of materials used for each component. In particular, the abutment 
screw joint, where the abutment is secured to the implant, may be 
susceptible to instability owing to the differences in elastic modulus 
and hardness between materials, inconsistent torque application, 
component misfit, occlusal interference, or improper implant angu -
lation and placement. These factors may lead to stress concentra -
tion, micromovement, and an increased risk of screw loosening or 
fracture[24–29]. Clinically, abutment screw loosening is one of the 
most common prosthetic complications in implant dentistry, with an 
incidence rate of 7–1 1%[30]. The primary cause is thought to be the 
“settling effect,” in which microscopic irregularities on the contact 
surfaces are compressed under load, leading to a partial loss of the 
initial torque and a reduction in clamping force. To address this, 
retightening of the abutment screw after the initial tightening has 
been proposed. Retightening within 5 to 10 min or immediately after 
the first torque application is recommended to re-establish preload 
and improve screw stability and has currently been adopted in many 
clinical settings[ 31–33]. However, these findings were primarily de -
rived using titanium implants. Zirconia, characterized by its higher 
elastic modulus and hardness as well as minimal plastic deforma -
tion, may exhibit distinct mechanical behavior compared with that 
of titanium. Fatigue-load testing is widely used to evaluate the 
mechanical properties of implants, primarily under bending forces. 
However, these tests may not accurately replicate the complex load -
ing conditions encountered in an oral environment, where tensile, 
compressive, and torsional forces act simultaneously during mastica-
tion[34–37]. Torsional stress is particularly relevant because it often 
acts in combination with bending and shear forces. Nevertheless, 
basic research on torsional stress in dentistry is limited, and its re -
production and evaluation remain challenging[ 38–40]. Convention-
ally, tensile tests are used for metallic materials and flexural tests are 
used for ceramics, making it difficult to directly compare dissimilar 
materials. However, torsional loading tests allow the assessment of 
the mechanical behavior of dissimilar materials under uniform condi-
tions[40]. Thus, this study aimed to investigate the effects of different 
abutment screw-tightening protocols and implant body materials 
on screw loosening and morphological changes after cyclic torsional 
loading of two-piece dental implants. The null hypotheses were as 
follows: (1) no differences occur in removal torque values among the 
different tightening protocols; (2) the implant body material does 
not affect removal torque values of the abutment screws; and (3) the 
implant body material does not influence morphological changes of 
the components.
2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Specimens
Two types of clinically available two-piece dental implants were 
used in this study. One was a commercially pure titanium implant (T 
group), and the other was a zirconia implant (Z group). Each implant 
system included a titanium base and abutment screws ( Table 1  
and Fig. 1). The titanium implants (Grade 4) used were Straumann 
Standard Plus Implants (Ø4.1 mm, length 10 mm; Straumann, Basel, 
Switzerland), and the zirconia implants (Y-TZP) were Straumann 
PURE Ceramic Implants (Ø4.1 mm, length 10 mm; Straumann). The 
titanium bases and abutment screws in both T and Z groups were 
made of titanium alloys. A zirconia superstructure was cemented 
onto each titanium base using universal resin cement. The super -
structures were fabricated using multilayered zirconia (Shofu Disk 
ZR Lucent Supra, Shade A3; Shofu, Kyoto, Japan). The shapes were 
designed using CAD software (Autodesk Inventor Professional, 
Autodesk, San Francisco, CA, USA) and arranged on zirconia disks 
using CAM software (GO2dental, Shofu). Milling was performed us -
ing a pre-sintered zirconia disk with a milling machine (DWX-53DC, 
Roland DG, Shizuoka, Japan), followed by full sintering in a zirconia 
furnace (OUSTROMAT 674i, Shofu). The sintering schedule included 
a heating rate of 5 °C/min from 20 °C to 1,450 °C, holding for 2 h at 
1,450 °C, and then cooling at 10 °C/min. After sintering, the super -
structures were polished and shaped into regular hexagonal prisms 
with opposing vertex distance of 7 mm and a height of 7 mm ( Fig. 
2). The superstructure was cemented to a titanium base according 
to the manufacturer’s instructions. The bonding surfaces of both 
components were sandblasted with 0.25 MPa alumina and cleaned 
ultrasonically. A universal adhesive (BeautyBond Xtreme, Shofu) was 
applied, followed by gentle air-drying for 3 s, strong air-drying for 
5 s, and light-curing from multiple directions using an LED curing 
unit. Universal resin cement (ResiCem EX, Shofu) was applied to the 
bonding surface, and hand pressure was applied for 10 s, followed by 
10 min under a 5 kg load. Excess cement was removed, and final light 
curing was performed using a dental laboratory curing unit for 180 
s. This procedure yielded a titanium-based superstructure assembly.
2.2. Implant assembly
Each implant body was fixed using a tabletop three-jaw scroll 
chuck. The titanium-based superstructure assembly was connected 
to the implant body using an abutment screw. A digital torque me -
ter (HDP-5, HIOS, Tokyo, Japan) was used to tighten the abutment 
screws to the manufacturer-specified torque of 35 Ncm.
To evaluate the effects of tightening conditions on screw sta -
bility, the specimens were divided into groups according to three 
distinct protocols, following the methodologies described by Sun et 
al.[31] and Katsuta et al.[38] (Fig. 3).

Under condition A (re-tightened after 5 min), the screw was 
tightened to 35 Ncm, left untouched for 5 min, loosened, and then 
re-tightened. The screw was then loosened again for removal torque 
measurement before being finally retightened to 35 Ncm. Under 
condition B (immediate re-tightening), the screw was tightened to 35 
Ncm, immediately loosened for removal torque measurement, and 
then retightened to 35 Ncm. Under condition C (no retightening), 
the screw was tightened to 35 Ncm. Based on the implant type and 
applied conditions, the specimens were classified into the following 
six groups: group T (titanium implants), TA (titanium implants with 
condition A), TB (titanium implants with condition B), and TC (tita -
nium implants with condition C). Z (zirconia implants), ZA (zirconia 
implants with condition A), ZB (zirconia implants with condition B), 
and ZC (zirconia implants with condition C). Each group consisted 
of six specimens, for a total of 36 specimens. The removal torque 
was defined as the maximum torque measured during loosening of 
the abutment screw. All procedures were conducted under identi -
cal conditions by the same operator to ensure the consistency and 
reproducibility of torque application.
2.3. Cyclic torsional loading test
Cyclic torsional loading was applied using a custom-built 
torsional fatigue-testing machine (AG-XR; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) 
(Fig. 4). The testing parameters were established in accordance with 
the ISO/TS 13498:2011[ 41] and ISO/TR 18130:2016[ 42]. The implant 
assemblies were mounted with the implant fixture as the fixed side 
and the superstructure as the movable side and were clamped 5 
mm above and below the implant fixture–titanium base interface 
using three-jaw scroll chucks. The applied load was set to 10% of 
the manufacturer-specified tightening torque at a rotational speed 
of 10°/min. One cycle was defined as a complete reciprocating 
rotation. In total, 100,000 cycles were performed. After testing, the 
removal torque value after loading (RT-post) was measured using a 
digital torque meter. For groups A and B, the removal torque value 
before loading (RT-pre) was measured beforehand. The torque loss 
percentage (TL) was calculated according to Katsuta et al. [38] using 
the following equation:

2.4. Scanning electron microscopy
The surface morphology before and after the cyclic torsional 
loading was observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) 
(JSM-7800F Prime, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). Magnifications of ×50, ×150, 
and ×300 were used to evaluate deformation and wear. Prior to ob -
servation, zirconia samples were sputter-coated with platinum using 
an ion coater (QUICK COATER SC-701 MK II ADVANCE, Sanyu Electron, 
Tokyo, Japan).

baseline STL data. After testing, STL data were acquired again. All 
the scans were performed at a uniform resolution (20.94 μm). The 
STL data were aligned using 3D measurement software (Geomagic

2.6. Statistical analysis
An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power soft -
ware (version 3.1.9.7 , Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düs -
seldorf, Germany) based on preliminary experimental means and 
standard deviations (SD = 3.723; n = 3 per group). The effect size was 
calculated using Cohen’s f = 0.723. Assuming α = 0.05 and power = 
0.80, the required total sample size was estimated to be 36, indicat -
ing that n = 6 per group was appropriate.
Statistical analyses were performed using Bell Curve for Excel 
(Social Survey Research Information Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). For 
RT-post, normality was confirmed via the Shapiro–Wilk test ( P > 
0.05), but Levene’s test indicated non-homogeneity of variance ( P < 
0.05). Welch’s t-test with Bonferroni correction was used for multiple 
comparisons (P < 0.05). RT-pre was analyzed using a one-way analysis 
of variance and Tukey’s honestly significant difference test, as both 
normality and homogeneity of variance were satisfied. TL showed a 
non-normal distribution and was analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis 
test (P < 0.05). RMS values were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U 
test because of their non-normal distribution. Because no significant 
differences were observed among the tightening protocols, the 
groups were combined to compare the T and Z groups (P < 0.05).

4. Discussion
The present study demonstrated that both the tightening proto-
col and the implant body material significantly affected the removal 
torque values of the abutment screws. Therefore, the following null 
hypotheses were rejected: (1) no differences occur in removal torque 
values among the different tightening protocols; (2) the implant 
body material does not affect the removal torque values of abut -
ment screws; and (3) the implant body material does not influence 
the morphological changes of the components. The following sec -
tions discuss the insights obtained from the experimental methods 
and outcomes after cyclic torsional loading.
To replicate the torsional stresses encountered during mastica -
tion, we used a dynamic test setup with a torsional fatigue-testing 
machine. In this system, the implant body was fixed and the super -
structure was allowed to rotate by applying repeated clockwise 
and counterclockwise torsional loads. Torsional stress is known to 
induce micromovements and stress concentrations at the interfaces 
between components, particularly in screw-retained two-piece 
implants, where mechanical stability depends heavily on the con -
nection between the implant body and the titanium-base-super -
structure assembly[ 43]. Conventionally, the mechanical properties 
of dental implants are evaluated by fatigue testing under bending 
forces in accordance with ISO 14801:2016 guidelines[ 44]. However, 
the oral environment involves complex loading, including tensile,

compressive, and torsional forces during mastication[ 40]. To simu -
late clinical conditions more realistically, the present study applied 
torsional loading, which is a method used in mechanical engineer -
ing to evaluate rotational fatigue resistance. The test parameters 
were set to low-load conditions to ensure safety margins, based on 
Katsuta et al. [38], and 100,000 loading cycles were applied to ap -
proximate one month of masticatory function following prosthesis 
delivery, as suggested by Kim et al. [45]. The load was set at 10% of 
the recommended torque, not to replicate maximum clinical stresses 
directly, but to provide a minimally invasive yet valid stress level 
consistent with mechanical design principles and ISO/TR 18130:2016 
guidelines[42]. To investigate the influence of retightening under 
cyclic torsional loading, three tightening protocols were examined 
according to the retightening time: Condition A (retightening after 5 
min), Condition B (immediate retightening), and Condition C (no re -
tightening). These protocols were designed based on the reports by 
Sun et al.[31] and Katsuta et al.[38]. For Condition A retightening was 
performed 5 min after the initial tightening to compensate for the 
settling effect, as suggested by Katsuta et al. [38], and was also con -
sistent with previous studies[31–33] demonstrating that retightening 
either 5–10 min after or immediately after the initial tightening could 
restore screw stability. Condition B was intended to represent a more 
clinically applicable retightening procedure, whereas Condition C 
reflected the most common clinical approach without retightening. 
By comparing these three conditions, the present study sought to 
determine whether the retightening effect previously observed 
under other loading modes was also applicable under torsional load-
ing. Two widely used implant systems, one made of titanium and the 
other made of zirconia, were used. Each implant body had the same 
diameter and length, and abutment screws of identical materials 
and similar designs were employed to minimize structural variability. 
Nevertheless, the present comparison inherently involved differ -
ences in the implant body design, abutment screw- and titanium-
based configurations, and interfacial characteristics, in addition to 
the material differences of the implant bodies. Accordingly, the study 
findings should be interpreted not as a reflection of the intrinsic 
material properties alone but rather as representing the mechani -
cal response of the entire clinically utilized implant system. For the 
superstructure, a homogeneous hexagonal zirconia block was milled 
to allow precise centering on the testing axis. This design ensured 
an even stress distribution and avoided axial deviation, enabling 
isolated evaluation of the effects of the material and tightening pro -
tocols. RMS values were used to quantify the dimensional changes 
in the abutment screws. As these screws were aligned along the axis 
of torsional loading, the stress concentration was expected to result 
in greater deformation. This hypothesis was verified by comparing 
pre- and post-test data using micro-CT and three-dimensional (3D) 
measurement software.
This study evaluated the effects of different tightening proto -
cols on RT-pre, RT-post, and TL. No significant differences in RT-pre 
were observed within the same implant material regardless of 
retightening or its timing. This suggests that the timing of retight -
ening does not significantly affect the initial removal torque, which 
is consistent with the findings of Sun et al. [31]. In contrast, RT-post 
showed notable differences; in both the T and Z groups, with 
specimens without retightening (group C) exhibiting the highest RT-
post values. Significant reductions were observed between TA and 
TC, TB, and TC, and ZA and ZC, whereas no significant differences 
were found between ZB and ZC. These results deviate from those 
of previous reports[ 31–33], suggesting that retightening improves 
the joint stability by compensating for the settling effect. This 
discrepancy can be attributed to the use of cyclic torsional loading 
in the present study. The torsional stress generates shear forces at 
the screw interface, potentially causing cumulative wear and plastic 
deformation[38]. According to mechanical engineering principles, 
torsional stability depends on multiple factors including the thread 
pitch, frictional diameter of the bearing surface, coefficient of fric -
tion, axial preload, and screw geometry[ 31]. Under repeated shear 
stress, micro-slippage (fretting) and localized wear may occur[ 37].

This study also investigated how implant body material affected 
RT-pre, RT-post, and TL values. Under all tightening conditions, the 
T group exhibited significantly higher RT-post values than those 
of the Z group. This tendency was attributed to the inherent me -
chanical and frictional properties of the materials used. The zirconia 
implants used in this study have a higher elastic modulus (200–220 
GPa) and greater hardness (1100–1500 HV) than commercially pure 
titanium and thus exhibit minimal plastic deformation. According 
to Hanawa[ 17], high rigidity materials, such as Y-TZP , have limited 
elastic deformation and are less capable of generating sufficient 
axial preload during screw tightening. This difference in the elastic 
modulus may also influence the stress distribution within the 
implant–abutment complex. The higher stiffness of zirconia could 
result in a greater stress concentration at the screw joint compared 
with titanium, which may partly explain the differences in torque 
retention and mechanical response observed in this study. Con -
sequently, torque retention in the abutment screw joint may be 
unstable in zirconia-based systems. In contrast, a higher coefficient 
of friction between the abutment screw head and threads has been 
associated with reduced screw loosening[ 46]. However, zirconia has 
a lower coefficient of friction and negligible plastic deformation than 
titanium[17], making it less effective at dissipating stress through 
deformation. Consequently, local stress concentrations are more 
likely to persist in zirconia-based assemblies. Conversely, titanium 
exhibits a higher coefficient of friction and a lower elastic modulus 
(approximately 110 GPa) than zirconia[ 17, 47], which allows it to ac -
commodate local misfits or stress concentrations through plastic 
deformation. These characteristics enable titanium to produce a 
more stable axial preload during screw tightening, contributing to 
enhanced joint stability between the titanium implant body and the 
titanium base-superstructure assembly.
Regarding RT-pre, the T group consistently showed higher re -
moval torque values than the Z group, suggesting that differences in 
material properties may already affect torque retention even before 
loading. Although the TL did not show significant differences be -
tween the groups, the Z group exhibited greater variability (standard 
deviation) than the T group. This implies that the stress relaxation 
behavior of zirconia may be inconsistent, whereas the plastic defor -
mation characteristics of titanium may mitigate individual variations 
and support more stable torque retention.
Taken together, these findings indicate that the torque retention 
in abutment screw joints is strongly influenced by material-specific 
mechanical factors such as the elastic modulus and coefficient of 
friction, as well as by the behavior of each material under torsional 
loading. Notably, under torsional stress, retightening does not nec -
essarily improve the settling effect and may compromise torque 
retention. Future investigations should explore more comprehensive 
tightening protocols that consider both the loading direction and 
material properties.
The morphological changes in abutment screws were evalu -
ated using the RMS values obtained before and after cyclic torsional 
loading. The T group consistently exhibited significantly greater 
dimensional changes than the Z group ( P < 0.05). SEM observations 
did not show notable deformation at the interfaces between the 
implant body and titanium-based superstructure assembly in any 
group. However, wear marks were consistently observed on the 
screw heads and threads in all samples. These findings suggest that 
the observed morphological changes are primarily attributed to the 
material properties of the components and the wear behavior at the 
contact surfaces. Because all abutment screws were made of the 
same titanium alloy, the differences in screw deformation and wear 
were assumed to have originated from differences in implant body 
materials. Commercially pure titanium has a lower elastic modulus 
and higher ductility than zirconia[ 17]. These characteristics make 
titanium more prone to local plastic deformation under tightening 
or torsional loading, particularly at points of misfit or stress concen -
tration. Consequently, the greater amount of deformation observed 
in the T group likely reflected the ability of the material to undergo 
plastic adaptation. Wear is defined as the gradual loss of material 
resulting from mechanical interactions between two contacting sur -
faces with relative motion[48]. High-friction interfaces increase shear 
stress and accelerate surface damage[47, 49]. Therefore, the combina-

In addition, the T group exhibited a wider range of RMS values 
than the Z group. This variability may reflect the effect of titanium 
ductility on stress concentration and deformation during screw 
tightening, which subsequently affects the extent of morphological 
changes observed after loading. These findings align with previous 
reports by Shinohara et al.[29], who suggested that differences in ma-
terial combinations between implant bodies and abutment screws 
affect screw-loosening behavior. Our results support this claim and 
highlight the importance of minimizing variations in tightening 
procedures to ensure stability, particularly when titanium implants 
are used. Carefully controlled and uniform tightening protocols 
tailored to the mechanical properties of the material are crucial for 
maintaining the integrity of the screw joint. However, the differences 
in RMS values observed in this study were of the order of only a few 
micrometers, which is negligible compared with the manufacturing 
tolerances and clinically acceptable ranges. Therefore, these differ -
ences should not be considered to have an immediate clinical impact 
but should instead be interpreted as supplementary observations 
indicating trends in wear and deformation at the contact surfaces.
5. Conclusions
The principal conclusions drawn within the limitations of this 
study are as follows.
1. Retightening the abutment screws did not necessarily improve 
the maintenance of the tightening torque under cyclic tor -
sional loading. Specifically, retightening 5 min after the initial 
tightening resulted in a significant reduction in the removal 
torque values, whereas immediate retightening or no retight -
ening yielded comparatively higher torque retention.
2. Differences in implant body material significantly affected the 
mechanical response to tightening, with titanium implants 
consistently exhibiting higher removal torque values than 
zirconia implants under all conditions.
3. The inherent properties of implant materials appear to affect 
the extent of abutment screw deformation and wear under 
torsional loading.

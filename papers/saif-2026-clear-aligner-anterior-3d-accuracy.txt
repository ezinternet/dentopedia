Introduction

The alignment of anterior teeth holds particular significance in orthodontics, as the anterior region plays a crucial role in facial aesthetics and overall smile appearance. Although clear aligners are increasingly popular in orthodontic treatment, there is still a need for thorough research on the efficacy of this system, particularly regarding its impact on the alignment of anterior teeth. Efficient anterior teeth alignment not only enhances aesthetic appeal but also contributes to achieving functional occlusion. Assessing the efficiency of anterior teeth alignment involves evaluating the effectiveness of the aligners in achieving the desired tooth position within the predicted timeframe. It is reported that the anticipated and final tooth positions differed in all tooth movements in non-extraction cases. Similarly, Sachdev’s study showed that an overall accuracy of 56.18% in anterior tooth movement can be achieved with clear aligners.

Measuring anterior teeth alignment involves carefully evaluating various linear and angular parameters. These measurements guide the orthodontist in planning an effective treatment strategy and help understand and anticipate treatment progress. Previous studies have employed multiple approaches to assess the effectiveness of clear aligner treatment. However, digital models have provided orthodontists with a precise and efficient tool for assessing treatment outcomes.

While several studies have established baseline expectations for clear aligner accuracy, recent research continues to refine our understanding of movement-specific and tooth-specific performance. Prospective studies, such as Haouili et al.reported an overall mean accuracy of 50% for Invisalign across all tooth types, highlighting persistent challenges with rotation (46%) and mandibular incisor intrusion (35%). Subsequent investigations have corroborated these trends, with systematic reviewsindicating that buccolingual crown tipping remains the most predictable movement, while extrusion, intrusion, and rotation, particularly of rounded canines and premolars, exhibit lower and more variable accuracy. Comparative analyses between aligners and fixed appliances by Murphy et al., further demonstrate that aligners achieve significantly less tooth movement in all directions, especially in torque, tipping, and rotation of maxillary canines. However, many of these studies analyze outcomes after refinements or include posterior teeth, potentially conflating the efficacy of the initial aligner set with additional corrective treatment. This study provides a focused, three-dimensional evaluation of anterior tooth movement accuracy achieved solely with the first set of Invisalign aligners and without refinement. This design isolates the initial treatment efficacy for the most aesthetically critical region and establishes a clear baseline for the predictability of the first treatment phase before any corrective interventions. The null hypothesis is that there are no significant differences in anterior teeth alignment compared to the predicted treatment outcome using the first set of aligners.

Methods

This retrospective study was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the ethical committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University (XJTU1AF2022LSK-186). Sample size was calculated using G*Power (v3.1; Heinrich Heine Universität Düsseldorf) to detect clinically significant differences in tooth movement between planned and achieved tooth positions, with detection thresholds set at 0.25 mm for linear displacement and 2° for angular changes. With α = 0.05, 85% power, and a medium effect size (d = 0.5), the results showed that at least 38 patients were required.

The study sample comprised 40 adult participants (18 male, 22 female), with an average age of 27.8 years. Every participant received Invisalign clear aligner orthodontic treatment (Align Technology, Inc., California, USA) from a proficient orthodontist certified in Invisalign treatment at the orthodontics department of the first affiliated hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University.

Patients treated with the Invisalign system between January 2019 and February 2024 were selected according to the following inclusion criteria: (1) Adult participants. (2) Non-extraction treatment except for third molars. (3) Finishing the first aligner set without midcourse correction or refinement. (4) No active periodontal disease or systemic conditions. Patients with skeletal discrepancies requiring surgical intervention, craniofacial syndromes, or congenital anomalies were excluded. As treatment commenced, written consent was obtained from all participants, permitting the use of their records for research and educational purposes.

Patients’ baseline characteristics are presented in (Table). Participants were directed to wear the appliance full-time daily, except when eating or cleaning. Additionally, they were advised to wear the next set of orthodontic aligners every 10 days, with each aligner designed to deliver up to 0.25 mm of linear movement, 2° of rotation, and 1° of mesiodistal or buccolingual crown tipping according to the Invisalign treatment protocol. On average, 27 aligners were used to complete the treatment, with a mean duration of 9 months. The anterior tooth power ridges and attachments frequency are shown in (Table).

For each patient, three STL files were obtained and analyzed: (1) the initial intraoral scan (T0), representing the pre-treatment dental status; (2) the post-treatment scan (T1), representing the achieved outcome following completion of the first aligner series; and (3) the predicted ClinCheck digital models (Tc), which is the virtually planned treatment results. All digital models were imported into Geomagic Control X software (version 2022, 3D Systems, Rock Hill, SC) for three-dimensional analysis and comparison.

Digital model superimposition was performed using a global and fine registration algorithm, with the pre-treatment intraoral scan (T0) serving as the reference model. Both the achieved outcome (T1) and the predicted outcome (Tc) were independently aligned to T0 using a validated whole-arch, surface-based best-fit registration algorithm. The registration parameters included: 50 maximum iteration cycles, 80% sampling ratio, and a maximum permitted average deviation of 0.01 mm to ensure precision (Fig.). The validation of this superimposition method was based on prior investigations that evaluated the accuracy of the whole-arch, best-fit registration method for aligning separate clinical intraoral scans and measuring tooth movement. This validated protocol was applied uniformly to align both the predicted (Tc) and achieved (T1) models to the T0 reference, relying on a whole-arch, surface-based best-fit algorithm to establish a stable common coordinate system from which anterior tooth movements were measured.

Alignment of digital models. (): Pre-treatment (T0) model; (): Post-treatment (T1) model; (): Predicted Clincheck model (Tc); (): Alignment of (T0) and (T1) models; (): Alignment of (T0), (T1), and (Tc) models.

The Construct Geometry tool in the software was utilized to establish a stable, three-dimensional coordinate system on the pre-treatment (T0) model for all measurements. First, a reference Occlusal Plane was defined using a least-squares fitting algorithm (the “Pick Multiple Points” method within the “Add Plane” tool) applied to four fitting points: the midpoints of the incisal edges of both maxillary central incisors and the distobuccal cusp tips of the second molars. A Mid-Sagittal Plane was then constructed perpendicular to this Occlusal Plane using two midline landmarks: the midpoint between the palatal surfaces of the maxillary central incisors at the cervical level and the facial midpoint of their mesial contact point. Finally, a Coronal Plane was automatically generated by the software as the plane perpendicular to both the Occlusal and Mid-Sagittal Planes, positioned to pass through the mesial contact points of the first premolars. This coordinate system served as the foundation for all subsequent linear and angular measurements (Fig.).

Fig. 2

Construction of coordinate planes. (): occlusal plane; () sagittal, and coronal planes.

To quantify vertical linear movement, the perpendicular distance from the mid-incisal edge of each incisor or the cusp tip of each canine to the established occlusal plane was measured on the (T0), (T1), and (Tc) models. These measurements represented the vertical crown displacement relative to the pre-treatment baseline (Fig.).

Fig. 3

Vertical movement analysis relative to referenced occlusal plane.

To establish a reproducible long axis for each anterior tooth for angular measurements, the isolated clinical crown was selected using the ‘Flood Selection’ tool. The ‘Construct Geometry’ function was then employed to generate a best-fit 3D vector a ‘polygon best-fit line through the point cloud of the crown polygon. This algorithm-derived vector, based on the complete crown geometry, approximates the tooth’s clinical long axis, extending from the cervical region to the incisal edge or cusp tip. The same protocol was applied sequentially to the pre-treatment (T0), achieved (T1), and predicted (Tc) models after superimposition, ensuring that the axis for each tooth at each time point was derived from the same anatomical structure using the same algorithm. This standardized digital approach guarantees methodological consistency across all models and tooth morphologies.

To quantify mesiodistal rotational movement, the ‘Flood Selection’ tool was used to obtain complete crown coverage of each target tooth, enabling the construction of a best-fit plane for the polygon tooth surface. A reference plane was created on the crown surface of the T0 model, and corresponding planes were constructed on the T1 and Tc models. Using the ‘Measure Angle’ function in Geomagic Control X, the angular difference between the T0 and follow-up planes was calculated. This calculation was constrained by defining the tooth’s long axis as the axis of rotation, and the resulting angle was projected onto the occlusal plane. This measured angular difference represents the isolated mesiodistal rotational change of the clinical crown, effectively excluding confounding movements such as buccolingual tipping. This same methodology was applied to the T0-Tc comparison (Fig.).

Mesiodistal tipping was measured as the angle between the tooth’s long axis vector and the sagittal plane (Fig.). Buccolingual tipping was measured as the angle between the same vector and the coronal plane (Fig.). For each type of tipping, the achieved angular change was calculated as (T1 - T0 angle), and the predicted change as (Tc -T0).

Statistical analysis

A second assessment of four digitally aligned models was carried out one week later to determine the consistency of measurements. The confirmation of normal data distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test permits the use of paired t-tests to determine the differences between the achieved and Clincheck-predicted tooth movement for each tooth. Statistical significance was defined as< 0.05 for assessing the difference between predicted and achieved values.

The percentage of tooth movement accuracy is determined using the following equation: percentage of accuracy = (achieved)/(predicted) *100%. All statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 23; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY).

Results

The sample’s initial malocclusion status was Class I malocclusion with a mean overjet of 3.812.1 mm, overbite of 3.85 ± 2.03 mm, upper crowding of 3.5 ± 1.5 mm, and lower crowding of 3.6 ± 1.7 mm.

The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) results demonstrated excellent agreement regarding intra-rater reliability with a score of 0.962 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.937–0.977) for linear measurements and 0.949 (95% [CI], 0.920–0.968) for angular measurements.

The overall mean accuracy of anterior teeth alignment was found to be (50.3%). The mean accuracy of anterior teeth alignment was (47.6%) and (53%) for the upper and lower dentition, respectively.

Discussion

Accurate digital model registration is vital for measuring precise tooth movement during orthodontic treatment. Several previous researches have performed digital model registration for tooth movement evaluation with clear aligners. Digital models have provided orthodontists with a precise and efficient tool for assessing orthodontic treatment outcomes. Therefore, this investigation has employed digital model analysis to quantify the efficacy of clear aligners in achieving the predicted anterior teeth movements. The initial intra-oral scan (T0), the final scan right before the refinement stage (T1), and the predicted ClinCheck digital models (Tc) were used to quantify the amount of clinical tooth movement.

The findings of this study demonstrate a 50.3% overall mean accuracy for anterior tooth alignment with the first aligner set. The mean accuracy of anterior teeth alignment was (47.6%) and (53%) for the upper and lower dentitions, respectively. Our results substantiate the consistent clinical observation that a significant discrepancy exists between virtually planned and clinically achieved outcomes, with the literature reporting an accuracy range of 55% to 72%. This study’s specific value falls within this spectrum, reinforcing the well-documented biomechanical limitations of the system. More importantly, our data on movement-specific accuracy offer a detailed confirmation and extension of previous findings. The high accuracy for lingual crown tipping, especially in lower canines (82.9%), echoes reports that buccolingual tipping is the most predictable movement due to the aligner’s mode of force application. Conversely, the profound difficulty in achieving upper incisor intrusion (23.1%) mirrors the conclusions of multiple investigators who identify vertical control, particularly true intrusion, as a major challenge for aligners. While the overall trend is consistent, our study provides a unique view by isolating the performance of the initial aligner series, thereby offering clinicians a more realistic expectation for the first phase of treatment and underscoring the inherent necessity of planning for refinement stages.

In the upper dentition, the distal tipping movement achieved high accuracy (64.9%), while the intrusion movement had the lowest accuracy (23.1%). The intrusion movement of the central incisor (24.5%) was shown to be less accurate than that of the lateral incisors (28.4%) and canines (44.7%). This can be explained from two perspectives; firstly, the mean predicted intrusion movement of central incisors (2.38 mm) was greater than that of lateral incisors (2.33 mm) and canines (1.51 mm), which may surpass the capability of clear aligners’ intrusion. Additionally, the intrusion movement needs space to take place, and the amount of available space close to canines attained through distalization of the posterior dentition in non-extraction cases is usually greater than that available for incisors; hence, the canines take advantage of the available space for intrusion and alignment ahead of incisors. Therefore, deep overbite correction through upper anterior teeth intrusion needs to be planned carefully. Haouili and Sachdev concluded that maxillary incisor intrusion without auxiliaries is still challenging. Another research also reported that the mean precision of anterior teeth intrusion was 51.19%. Consequently, achieving significant deep overbite correction using clear aligners alone appears to have limited predictability.

Extrusion movement of the upper (55.06%) and lower (51.82%) canines was more accurate than that of the incisors. This could be due to the use of horizontal rectangular attachments on these teeth, which provide a larger surface area for force application. Additionally, the higher accuracy of lingual crown tipping in upper (69.7%) and lower (82.9%) canines contributes to achieving higher extrusion movement accuracy through the relative extrusion concept.

As highlighted in previous studies, anterior teeth rotational movement has often been unpredictable, particularly for teeth with round geometry, like canines. Our research aligns with this notion, as we observed that while rotational movement exhibited acceptable predictability for upper and lower incisors in both mesial and distal directions, canines proved to be less accurate. In the upper dentition, the mean accuracy of mesial rotational movement was higher for central incisors (74.4%) and lateral incisors (60.4%) compared to canines (55.1%). Similarly, the accuracy of distal rotational movement was higher for central incisors (67.2%) and lateral incisors (63.9%) compared to canines (52.8%). This trend persisted in the lower dentition, where the mean accuracy of mesial rotational movement for central (60.7%) and lateral (68.7%) incisors exceeded that of canines (53.1%). Likewise, the accuracy of distal rotational movement for central (65.8%) and lateral (62.5%) incisors was higher compared to canines (50.1%). These findings are consistent with those reported by Haouili, where it was observed that mesial rotation of upper and lower anterior teeth was more accurate than distal rotation, and the rotation of upper canines was shown to be less predictable than incisors.

In the anterior lower dentition, the highest accuracy was attained during lingual tipping movement (75.5%) and labial tipping movement (66.5%). Interestingly, the lingual tipping movement of canines (82.9%) demonstrated greater accuracy compared to that of central (78.2%) and lateral incisors (65.4%). Similarly, in the upper dentition, the lingual tipping movement of canines (69.7%) exhibited higher accuracy than that of central (53.4%) and lateral incisors (49.2%). These findings suggest that Invisalign can effectively control the tipping movement of anterior teeth with precision. Furthermore, mesial tipping (65.3%) and mesial rotational movement (60.9%) demonstrated high accuracy, while vertical extrusion (45.3%) and intrusion (49.8%) movement showed to be less accurate.

Practitioners must employ Clincheck software to visualize the anticipated treatment results and design the biomechanics involved. Understanding the aspects where the tooth position will differ more from the predicted position enables them to prioritize complex movements and incorporate overcorrections into their virtual treatment plans. This approach could enhance the treatment efficiency and facilitate superior treatment outcomes.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, tooth movement was evaluated using crown-based landmarks derived from intraoral scans. Although this approach reflects clinically relevant outcomes related to aesthetics and occlusion, it does not assess root position or quantify true bodily movement. Consequently, the effectiveness of specific auxiliaries, such as root control attachments, in achieving root translation could not be evaluated. Future studies incorporating three-dimensional imaging modalities capable of visualizing root movement are needed to fully assess aligner biomechanics.

Second, the retrospective design limited control over patient compliance and did not account for potential overcorrections or additional aligners, which may influence the accuracy of achieved tooth movements.

Finally, the extended inclusion period represents a further limitation, as incremental updates to Invisalign materials, attachment designs, and SmartForce features were introduced during the study timeframe. As a result, the findings may not fully reflect the performance of the most recent aligner system. Nevertheless, all treatments were planned and delivered by a single experienced provider using a standardized protocol and were evaluated after completion of the first aligner series without refinements, allowing for a consistent assessment of the intrinsic predictability of anterior tooth movements with clear aligners.

In summary, this study provides a clear, focused analysis of clear aligner accuracy by examining results from the first set of aligners only before any refinements are made. This approach establishes a new baseline for what clinicians can expect from the initial treatment phase, specifically for the aesthetically important anterior region. Using a precise 3D measurement method, we were able to separate and measure different types of tooth movements. Our most important finding is the wide range in predictability: some movements, like lingual tipping of lower canines, are highly accurate (82.9%), while others, like intruding upper anterior teeth, are much less predictable (23.1%). These detailed, movement-specific results give clinicians practical information to better plan treatment, prioritize certain movements, and build necessary overcorrections into the initial digital plan, improving both efficiency and patient satisfaction.

Conclusions

Invisalign treatment outcomes vary significantly across different types of tooth movements. Distal tipping in upper dentition and lingual tipping movement of canines demonstrate greater predictability, while certain movements, such as intrusion of upper incisors, remain challenging.

Anticipated overbite correction through upper anterior teeth intrusion requires careful planning due to its lower predictability. The use of attachments and deliberate overcorrections may enhance the effectiveness of this movement.

Additional refinements for anterior teeth alignment would be advisable to achieve excellent treatment outcomes.

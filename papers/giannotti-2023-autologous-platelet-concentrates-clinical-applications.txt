Title: Progress in Regenerative Medicine: Exploring Autologous Platelet Concentrates and Their Clinical Applications
Authors: Giannotti L, Di Chiara Stanca B, Spedicato F, Nitti P, Damiano F, Demitri C, Calabriso N, Carluccio MA, Palermo A, Siculella L, Stanca E
Journal: Genes (Basel)
Year: 2023
DOI: 10.3390/genes14091669
PMID: 37761809
PMCID: PMC10530962
Source: PubMed Central (open access, full text via PMC MCP)

Abstract:
The goal of regenerative medicine is to achieve tissue regeneration. In the past, commonly used techniques included autologous or allogeneic transplantation and stem cell therapy, which have limitations, such as a lack of donor sites in the case of autologous transplantation and the invasiveness of stem cell harvesting. In recent years, research has, therefore, focused on new and less invasive strategies to achieve tissue regeneration. A step forward in this direction has been made with the development of autologous platelet concentrates (APCs), which are derived from the patient's own blood. They can be classified into three generations: platelet-rich plasma (PRP), platelet-rich fibrin (PRF), and concentrated growth factors (CGFs). These APCs have different structural characteristics, depending on the distinctive preparation method, and contain platelets, leukocytes, and multiple growth factors, including those most involved in regenerative processes. The purpose of this review is to clarify the most used techniques in the field of regenerative medicine in recent years, comparing the different types of APCs and analyzing the preparation protocols, the composition of the growth factors, the level of characterization achieved, and their clinical applications to date.

1. Introduction

Regenerative medicine is an emerging field of medicine that was born in the 1990s. It focuses on the development of alternative therapies for the repair of cells, tissues, and organs to restore functions damaged by congenital defects, disease, trauma, or ageing. The goal is to induce self-renewal processes in damaged tissues, unlike traditional medicine, which primarily involves the replacement of damaged parts.

Historically, the gold standard of regenerative medicine has been the use of autologous transplants, where the donor and recipient are the same person, mainly due to the absence of immunogenic reactions. The clinical benefits of autologous stem cell transplantations have been observed in many clinical trials addressed to a large spectrum of disease and injury. However, despite its high efficacy, this method has drawbacks; the autologous bone graft, for example, has limitations related to the limited quantity and availability of donor sites, pain and morbidity in the donor site, and the need for further surgery for the patient, which can lead to hematomas, infections, blood loss, and longer operative times. An alternative to autologous transplantation is allogeneic transplantation, in which the donor and recipient are not the same person, but this method also has drawbacks that are mainly related to high costs and possible immune reactions leading to graft rejection.

1.1. Stem Cell Therapy for Tissue Regeneration

Recently, regenerative medicine has focused on research into minimally invasive techniques to regenerate damaged tissues. A breakthrough in this direction has been the use of stem cell therapy, which aims to regenerate damaged tissues and organs with new cells derived from stem cell transplantation.

In the literature, numerous studies have investigated mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) for tissue regeneration, including bone, cartilage meniscus, tendons, ligaments, and intervertebral discs, both in vitro and in vivo. Although stem cell therapy represents a novel approach to tissue regeneration, its clinical application is hampered by challenges, such as the limited survival and differentiation capacity of transplanted cells. In addition, MSC therapy involves the collection, isolation, and in vitro expansion of these cells to ultimately enable their transplantation. This clinical use is complex due to the potential contamination of the cell culture, the overall duration of the whole process, and the high cost involved. Furthermore, the risks related to tumorigenicity, pro-inflammatory properties associated with high exposure to pro-inflammatory cytokines, and the danger of developing fibrosis due to the MSC's capability to differentiate into myofibroblasts should be taken into consideration. To address these limitations, tissue engineering technology has been used to enhance the viability and proliferative potential of stem cells.

1.2. Growth Factors and Tissue Regeneration

New strategies in regenerative medicine aim to better understand and exploit the potential of endogenous stem cells and autologous growth factors, which are specialized polypeptide molecules that bind receptors on target cells and deliver messages regarding migration, proliferation, differentiation, survival, and cell secretion. The classes of growth factors most involved in tissue regeneration include transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF). These growth factors are all produced mainly by platelet activation following tissue injury.

TGF-β superfamily consists of thirty-three polypeptides, including three 25 kDa isoforms (TGF-β1, TGF-β2, TGF-β3), BMPs, and activins. Members of the TGF-β family are secreted by platelets, endothelial cells, lymphocytes, and macrophages, and act by binding as dimers to serine/threonine kinase receptors (TGFBR-I/II), activating Smad2/Smad3/Smad4 signaling. TGF-β1, the predominant isoform, plays a crucial role in wound healing — inflammation, angiogenesis, re-epithelialization, connective tissue regeneration, ECM synthesis (fibronectin/collagen), inhibition of ECM degradation, osteoblast precursor chemotaxis/mitogenesis, and upregulation of VEGF.

BMPs are the largest TGF-β subset (~30 ligands); they form homodimers binding heterodimeric type I/II serine/threonine kinase receptors, activating RA-Smads (Smad1/5/8) + Smad4 (blocked by Smad6/7). BMPs are morphogenetic factors stimulating differentiation and endochondral bone formation rather than proliferation; BMP-2, -4, -6, -7 are most highly expressed in tissue damage.

VEGF family (VEGF-A/B/C/D/E, placenta growth factor) governs vasculogenesis, angiogenesis, lymphangiogenesis. VEGF-A, produced by endothelial cells, keratinocytes, fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells, platelets, neutrophils, macrophages, stimulates endothelial migration/proliferation/survival (via Bcl-2), has chemotactic effects on macrophages/granulocytes, and stimulates neurogenesis. It binds VEGFR-1/VEGFR-2, activating PLC/PI3K/Akt/Ras/Src/MAPK pathways. After injury, activated platelets/macrophages release VEGF-A and TNF-α, inducing further VEGF-A expression in keratinocytes/fibroblasts; other cytokines (TGF-β1, EGF, TGF-α, KGF, FGF-b, PDGF-BB, IL-1) act as paracrine VEGF-A stimulators; hypoxia strongly upregulates VEGF.

PDGF family (PDGF-AA/AB/BB/CC/DD) is abundant in platelet α-granules, also present in macrophages, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, keratinocytes; binds transmembrane tyrosine kinase receptors, triggering Src/PI3K/PLC-γ/RAS signaling. PDGF is released upon platelet degranulation and stimulates proliferation/chemotaxis of neutrophils, macrophages, fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells; stimulates macrophage TGF-β secretion; participates in angiogenesis (VEGF/VEGFR-2 induction); stimulates fibroblast proliferation and ECM production.

2. Autologous Platelet Concentrates (APCs)

APCs are biomaterials derived from centrifugation of the patient's own peripheral blood, containing a high concentration of platelets, leukocytes, and growth factors (PDGF, TGF-β1/2, VEGF, FGF, IGF-1) involved in cell proliferation, matrix remodeling, and angiogenesis. Clinical applications include dentistry, plastic surgery, implantology, refractory ulcer/burn healing, musculoskeletal/tendon/ligament repair, and osteoarthritis treatment. APCs are classified into three generations based on preparation method and characteristics.

2.1. Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP)

First generation (discovered 1970). Two-stage centrifugation (separation then concentration, e.g., 5600 rpm then 2400 rpm) of anticoagulated whole blood yields platelet-poor plasma (PPP), buffy coat (BC, = PRP after resuspension), and red blood cells (RBC). Activation requires bovine thrombin + calcium chloride, producing a gel via fibrin mesh incorporation. Platelet/growth-factor concentration in PRP is estimated 3–5× higher than peripheral blood. Subdivided into leukocyte-poor/pure PRP (P-PRP, low fibrin density, low/absent leukocytes, platelets may be damaged during preparation) and leukocyte-and-platelet-rich PRP (L-PRP, high platelet+leukocyte concentration, loose fibrin network). Drawback: requires anticoagulants + bovine thrombin, which can interfere with physiological healing. PRGF (Anitua) replaced bovine thrombin with 10% calcium chloride to preserve platelet integrity, though leukocyte content remains low.

2.2. Platelet-Rich Fibrin (PRF)

Second generation (Choukroun, 2000), an alternative to PRP. Venous blood (10 mL) without anticoagulant, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, yields RBC (lower layer), fibrin clot/PRF (middle layer), and PPP (upper layer). Without anticoagulant, platelets activate within minutes of tube contact, releasing clotting factors; fibrinogen concentrates then converts to fibrin via endogenous thrombin, localized between RBC and PPP layers. Cellular components: leukocytes, platelets, macrophages, granulocytes, neutrophils, RBC — platelets drive the initial healing phase, leukocytes/macrophages/granulocytes/neutrophils the anti-inflammatory phase. Subdivided into P-PRF (leukocyte-poor, denser/more stable fibrin matrix via calcium-chloride-induced polymerization), L-PRF (low leukocyte content but high platelet uptake, no anticoagulant/gelling agent, platelets+leukocytes trapped in dense fibrin mesh), and I-PRF (injectable PRF, reduced centrifugation speed 700 rpm/3 min, liquid state usable for soft-tissue/mucosa/skin injection, e.g., facial rejuvenation — remains liquid ~20 min post-centrifugation before fibrin forms). A-PRF (advanced PRF) uses lower speed/longer time (1500 rpm, 14 min), yielding a softer fibrin clot with more entrapped platelets than P-PRF/L-PRF.

2.3. Concentrated Growth Factors (CGFs)

Third generation (Sacco, 2006), a modified PRF form. Blood (8 mL) centrifuged at alternating speeds for 13 min (2 min@2700 rpm, 4 min@2400 rpm, 4 min@2700 rpm, 3 min@3000 rpm) using the Medifuge MF200 (Silfradent, Italy). Yields PPP (upper), CGF (middle — fibrin + growth factors, itself subdivided into upper white part, lower red part, intermediate buffy coat), RBC (lower). The alternating-speed protocol produces a denser fibrin matrix containing more growth factors and cells than PRF/PRP; the fibrin scaffold traps platelets, leukocytes, and CD34+ cells. Growth factors most abundant in CGFs: PDGF, TGF-β1, FGF-b, VEGF, IGF-1, EGF, BMP — trapped in the fibrin matrix and released gradually, ensuring sustained/controlled regenerative activity and reduced inflammatory risk from growth-factor bolus concentrations.

2.4. Comparison Among the Three Generations of APCs

2.4.1. Commonly used devices — PRP: Eclipse PRP Centrifuge, Arthrex Angel System, Regen Lab PRP Kit, EmCyte Pure PRP II System, Drucker Horizon PRP Centrifuge (high-speed density separation); double-syringe systems (GPS III/Biomet Biologics, EmCyte Pure PRP II) auto-mix anticoagulant; single-syringe systems (Regen Lab One PRP Kit, Arthrex Angel System). PRF: Choukroun PRF Centrifuge, Process for PRF Centrifuge, Intra-Spin System (low-speed centrifugation); some manual-manipulation box/tube systems without centrifugation; i-PRF System for anticoagulant-free PRF (common in dental/oral surgery). CGF: compact centrifuges for smaller dental/medical clinics — Medifuge MF200 (customizable alternating-speed programs for CGF/PRF), MediSpin, Fibrinet System, Intra-Spin System.

2.4.2. Growth factor release — Masuki et al.: CGF and A-PRF contain similar growth-factor amounts, both higher than PRP. Qiao et al.: TGF-β, PDGF, VEGF, IGF-1 concentrations similar across CGF/PRF/PRP, but CGF and PRF have significantly higher FGF-b than PRP. Park et al.: CGF contains 1.5× more VEGF than PRP and PRF. PRP releases essentially all its growth-factor content within 1 hour (only supports the initial regeneration phase); PRF releases continuously for 7–10 days; A-PRF release lasts up to 14 days. Schär et al. (28-day culture): PRP releases most growth factors early, PRF shows more consistent release over time; L-PRP forms an unstable matrix that disintegrates easily (immediate release), while L-PRF's progressively polymerized fibrin network sustains release. Kobayashi et al. (10-day comparison): PRP releases the most initially, but PRF/A-PRF cumulatively release more over the full period. CGF contains the highest overall growth-factor quantity among the three generations (VEGF, TGF-β1, BMP-2, MMP-2/MMP-9). Borsani et al.: CGF growth-factor release over 8 days shows programmed, factor-specific kinetics. Honda et al.: CGF growth-factor release lasts up to 13 days. Stanca et al.: CGF soluble-factor release lasts a full 28 days — VEGF peaks ~day 14 then declines; TGF-β1 and BMP-2 peak ~day 21, remaining high through day 28. Isobe et al.: CGF growth-factor release shows two phases — an immediate phase (centrifugation-activated platelet release/diffusion) and a late phase peaking at day 14 (fibrin-structure degradation + de novo production by CGF-resident cells); MMP-2/MMP-9 peak earlier (~day 7) then decline, exceeding initial extracted levels (suggesting active CGF-cell synthesis, not just passive release).

2.4.3. Cellular content and release — Platelet concentration correlates with beneficial biological effect. Normal blood platelet count: 150,000–450,000/µL. PRGF: ~3× whole-blood platelet concentration (optimal for fibroblast/osteoblast proliferation and tissue healing; >6× may inhibit healing). PRP: 4–8× higher platelet concentration than peripheral blood. A-PRF: 17.8× higher; CGF: 15.5× higher than whole blood. CGF's outer surface has denser fibrin than its interior (where activated platelets/cells concentrate); CGF nucleated cells are uniformly CD34+/CD45+/CD105+ (hematopoietic stem cells, lymphocytes, monocytes, fibroblast-like cells present). Di Liddo et al.: the platelet concentrate CPL-MB also contains autologous multipotent stem-like cells. CGF primary cells show fibroblast-like or spherical morphology (later predominantly elongated), express CD105/CD45 (minimal CD34), lack MSC markers CD73/CD90, but express markers spanning MSC (CD105), hematopoietic stem cell (CD45/CD34), and endothelial progenitor (CD31/eNOS) lineages; also express pluripotency genes Oct3/4 and Nanog. Stanca et al.: CGF primary cells can differentiate into osteoblasts (losing stem-cell markers); Giannotti et al.: this osteogenic differentiation is enhanced by silicon-doped hydroxyapatite (HA-Si) bioceramic scaffolds. Calabriso et al.: CGF-derived CD34+ cells express endothelial markers (eNOS, VE-cadherin, VEGFR-2, CD31), behaving as endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) that produce VEGF/TGF-β1/MMP-2/MMP-9 and functionally integrate into capillary-like structures — contributing to angiogenic response.

2.4.4. Applications in regenerative medicine — Facial rejuvenation: PRP improves wrinkles/skin texture/elasticity via collagen synthesis/fibroblast proliferation; PRF has greater regenerative potential than PRP; CGF shows even better in-vitro effects on fibroblast/endothelial proliferation and collagen synthesis. Ear/nose reconstruction: PRP (Fang et al.) stimulates chondrocyte proliferation and inhibits fibrocartilage formation via TGF-β/SMAD; CGF (Chen et al., 2019) stimulates chondrocyte proliferation/migration/ECM synthesis via IGF-1R/PI3K/AKT. Bone defect regeneration: Rochira et al. — CGF induces osteogenic differentiation in human BMSCs; Kim et al. — no significant difference between PRP/PRF/CGF in early bone regeneration; other studies — CGF and A-PRF outperform PRP in periosteal cell proliferation; Park et al. — CGF has greater regenerative potential than PRF in early femoral-defect repair; CGF + bone substitutes (autograft, chitosan-alginate hydrogel, scaffolds) improves later-stage bone repair. Stanca et al. attribute part of this to high L-glutamic acid/taurine content in CGF (glutamic-acid-rich sequences induce osteogenic differentiation/mineralization; taurine promotes MSC osteoblast differentiation via ERK1/2, upregulating osterix/RUNX2/osteopontin/ALP).

Dental implantology: a clinically significant APC application area given biocompatibility, growth-factor content, ease of preparation. PRF increases osteogenic differentiation markers in gingival stromal progenitor cells, periodontal ligament stem cells, alveolar bone osteoblasts, periodontal ligament fibroblasts (in vitro); successfully used in vivo for maxillary sinus lifting, intraosseous defects, dental extractions; recently shown to increase dental implant stability and bone density. CGF is an emerging trend in periodontology/implantology (fewer studies to date): Sohn et al. — CGF in maxillary sinus floor augmentation induces rapid new bone formation; other studies show better peri-implant bone quality/osseointegration. Palermo et al. — first demonstration that CGF permeation of the implant surface improves clinical success by reducing post-surgical complications; Shetye et al. — CGF accelerates osseointegration, improves implant stability values (agrees with Palermo). Özveri Koyuncu et al. — CGF placed in the implant osteotomy (before implant insertion) showed no effect on implant stability vs conventional implants — contrasting with Palermo's technique (implant pre-permeated with CGF before insertion), suggesting technique/timing of CGF application (permeating the implant itself vs placing CGF in the socket) may explain divergent stability results.

Neural domain: Borsani et al. (2020) — CGF induces neuronal differentiation in human neuroblastoma cells (SHSY-5Y) comparable to standard differentiation inducers, suggesting a novel CGF application in neural regeneration.

3. Allogeneic Platelet Gel Treatments

For conditions requiring repeated, extended platelet-gel application (e.g., large chronic skin ulcers), autologous blood draws may compromise patient hemoglobin. Pooled allogeneic platelet concentrates offer a near-unlimited gel supply for long-term repeated use, benefiting patients who cannot undergo repeated autologous blood draws (infants, septicemia/hematological-condition patients, elderly with multiple comorbidities) and emergency cases. Greppi et al. and Wang et al. support pooled allogeneic platelet concentrate use; leucodepleted allogeneic platelet concentrates show no discernible immunological drawback.

4. Conclusions

This narrative review compares three APC generations (PRP, PRF, CGF) across preparation protocols, growth-factor composition, characterization level, and clinical applications. CGF appears the most effective APC: fully autologous, highest growth-factor content among the three, simple/quick preparation, dense fibrin scaffold providing more constant growth-factor delivery (VEGF, TGF-β1, PDGF, FGF-b, IGF-1, EGF, BMP), and retains primary cells capable of osteoblast/endothelial-like differentiation. Most-used clinical applications of APCs are in oral surgery (dental implantology, sinus augmentation), bone-defect regeneration/repair, and facial rejuvenation. Further studies are needed for a comprehensive characterization of APCs and additional application fields.

## ABSTRACT
Background:This study tests the effects of scaling and root planing (SRP) versus SRP plus minocycline HCl microspheres (SRP+MM) on 11 periodontal pathogens and clinical outcomes in Stage II-IV Grade B periodontitis subjects.
Methods:Seventy participants were randomized to receive SRP (n=35) or SRP+MM (n=35). Saliva and clinical outcomes were collected for both groups at baseline before SRP, 1-month reevaluation, and at 3 and 6-month periodontal recall. Minocycline microspheres (MM) were delivered to pockets ≥5mm immediately after SRP and immediately after the 3-month periodontal maintenance. A proprietary saliva test* was utilized to quantitate 11 putative periodontal pathogens. Microorganisms and clinical outcomes were compared between groups using generalized linear mixed-effects models with fixed effects and random effects terms. Mean changes from baseline were compared between groups via group-by-visit interaction tests.
Results:Significant reduction in Tannerella forsythia, Treponema denticola, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Prevotella intermedia, Parvimonas micra and Eikenella corrodens were identified at the reevaluation after SRP+MM. 6-months after SRP with a re-application of MM 3-months after SRP, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Prevotella intermedia, Campylobacter rectus, and Eikenella corrodens were significantly reduced. SRP+MM participants had significant clinical outcome reductions in pockets ≥5mm at the reevaluation, 3 and 6-month periodontal maintenances and clinical attachment loss gains at the 6-month periodontal maintenance.
Conclusion:MM delivered immediately after SRP and a reapplication at 3-months appeared to contribute to improved clinical outcomes and sustained decreased numbers of Fusobacterium nucleatum, Prevotella intermedia, Campylobacter rectus, and Eikenella corrodens at 6-months.

## INTRODUCTION

According to the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), 42.2% of United States (U.S.) adults have periodontitis.1 Periodontitis and microbial translocation associated with periodontitis contribute to cardiovascular diseases (CVD),2–4 pulmonary disease,5 and pre-term child birth,6 although controversial, seem to contribute to poor diabetic control,7–10 among others conditions. The ulcerated gingival epithelium facing the periodontal biofilm provides a gateway for oral microbes and their products to cross the mucosal barrier and stimulate local and systemic proinflammatory responses. Coupled with predisposing factors, the proinflammatory stimuli may ultimately affect not only the periodontium, but also systemic health.11,12

Scaling and root planing (SRP) by manual instrumentation is the standard of care for treatment of periodontitis.13 Historically, site-level metrics to assess and diagnose periodontal disease have included the examination and recording of clinical parameters including probing depth (PD), bleeding on probing (BOP), recession (REC), clinical attachment level (CAL), furcation (FUR), mobility, gingival index (GI) and radiographically evident alveolar bone resorption.14 Evidence has indicated that these clinical outcomes may not distinguish between stable and active disease sites.15–18 In addition, these clinical outcomes do not identify individuals susceptible to periodontitis prior to the onset of CAL, or provide a risk profile of periodontal disease affecting systemic health.11,15,19 Although individual microorganisms provide poor diagnostic, prognostic sensitivity, and specificity in predicting disease initiation or progression, the identification of a group of periodontal pathogens may aid the prediction of periodontitis progression or the success of SRP treatment.19

According to the polymicrobial synergy and dysbiosis (PSD) model, susceptible individuals harbor keystone pathogens capable of changing the nutrient foundation of the entire microbial community with specific virulence factors. Porphyromonas gingivalis is a red complex bacteria, considered a keystone pathogen that has a bidirectional relationship to modify the nutritional foundation of the microbial community for the benefit and expansion of more pathogenic bacteria.20–22 The new nutrient environment changes the metabolic activity of the microbial community and facilitates the expansion of more pathogenic microorganisms (Tannerella forsythia and Treponema denticola).20 These pathobionts further enhance the dysbiosis of the symbiotic community ultimately eliciting a destructive inflammatory response.20,23–25
Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans is a true pathogen associated with early-onset and aggressive periodontitis.11,25
Fusobacterium nucleatum/periodonticum is instrumental in biofilm development and recruitment of other pathogens to contribute to biofilm formation.11,22,26 Other gram-negative bacteria that act as pathobionts contributing to microbial dysbiosis are: Eubacterium nodatum, Prevotella intermedia, Campylobacter rectus, Parvimonas micra, Eikenella corrodens, and Capnocytophaga species (gingavalis, ochracea, sputigena).24

### Periodontal therapy and minocycline hydrochloride microspheres

Clinical trials conducted previously provided evidence of enhanced pocket depth reduction with the combination of SRP plus minocycline microspheres (SRP+MM).16,27–30 MM is approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as an adjunct to SRP procedures for the reduction of pocket depths in patients with periodontitis. Teles et al. suggested that repeated application of MM may produce a reduction in periodontal pathogens.31 This suggestion aligns with Goodson et al. who reported a decrease in red complex bacteria one-month after participants in the SRP+MM group completed treatment.32

To date, the effect of reducing periodontal pathogens from the combination of SRP+MM and continuous periodontal maintenance as assessed through salivary analysis has not been investigated. The primary goal of this study is to determine the adjunctive effects of MM on clinical outcomes of PD, CAL, BOP, and GI after SRP+MM compared to SRP alone. Secondary outcome variables were the relative numbers of 11 periodontal pathogens in saliva after SRP+MM compared to SRP alone.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Experimental design and treatment

This single site randomized clinical trial was approved by the human subjects ethics board of the University of Minnesota Institutional Review Board (STUDY00004896) and was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2013, and registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (CT.gov) (NCT03762915). Participants in both groups had all quadrants treated with ultrasonic scaling followed by hand instrumentation for the completion of quadrant and/or localized SRP at baseline, a 1-month reevaluation and a 3 and 6-month periodontal maintenance with ultrasonic scaling and hand instrumentation at the UMN Oral Health Clinical Research Center (OHCRC) by the principal investigator (PI) (MA). The SRP+MM group had MM administered in sites of PD≥5mm at baseline immediately following SRP (n=594 sites) and a reapplication of MM at 3-months immediately following completion of the periodontal maintenance (n=566 sites; 95%). The protocol windows were as follows: 1-month reevaluation occurred after baseline SRP +/− one week, 3-month periodontal maintenance occurred three months after baseline +/− one month, and the 6-month periodontal maintenance occurred six months after baseline +/− one month. Figure 1 provides the protocol flow. If the baseline SRP treatment could not be competed in one visit, the baseline SRP was split into two parts that were scheduled ≤10 days apart. This was done to allow enough time to complete the procedure, and if applicable, decrease dosage of local anesthesia administered at one time.

### Sample size estimation

A sample of 75 participants’ treatment planned for SRP at the UMN School of Dentistry (SOD) or their dental home were invited to be screened for eligibility. This total included an estimated 20% attrition rate. Pre and post changes in PD and CAL were not available when the trial was planned, therefore; these outcomes were expressed in terms of standardized effect sizes, also known as Cohen’s d statistic. It was estimated that for each outcome measure, a sample size of 60, 30 participants in each group, would provide 80% power to detect a difference in means of 0.736 standard deviations using Cohen’s d statistic, using a two-group t-test with a two-sided 0.05 level of significance.

The inclusion criteria were adults at least 18 years of age, a diagnosis of periodontitis (Stage II-Stage IV Grade B)33 documented in their UMN SOD or dental home electronic dental record, treatment planned for localized or quadrant SRP and a minimum of eight sites of PD≥5mm and eights sites of BOP in any of the four quadrants. The exclusion criteria included: unable to comply with the study protocol, localized or quadrant SRP within the last six months, cigarette use within the last year, antibiotic use, pregnant, planning to become pregnant or unsure of pregnancy status, diagnosed cardiac conditions, uncontrolled medical conditions, tetracycline allergy, and any medication that may affect periodontal conditions (Table 1).

### Recruitment and randomization

Recruitment began in May 2019 and concluded October 2021. Clinical research visits occurred June 2019 until March 2022. In March 2020, the UMN paused all human research due to the COVID-19 pandemic. In April 2020, this study was approved as a Tier-3 “essential” study, for continued in-person visits for enrolled participants and research activities resumed at the end of May 2020.

For recruitment, electronic dental records were prescreened for inclusion criteria using a dental management software.† Additional recruitment methods included: recruitment flyers with key inclusion criteria posted on each floor of the UMN SOD, study and contact information posted on the UMN StudyFinder website, and on CT.gov. Anyone willing to volunteer was able to contact the study team via email or phone to request a prescreening. A current full mouth series of radiographs (FMS), full periodontal charting, and a treatment plan outlining the need for localized or generalized SRP was required for prescreening. This documentation was deemed current if it had been completed within the past two years.

A total of 787 dental records were prescreened (731 UMN SOD and 56 volunteer inquiries guided by the recruitment flyer, UMN StudyFinder, or CT.gov). During the prescreening of dental records, a total of 701 did not meet the inclusion criteria (Table 1) and were ineligible for an in-person screening visit. The remaining 86 received a study invitation letter. Of the 86, 16 were not interested or were a screen fail and 70 met the inclusion criteria at the in-person screening visit, confirmed by the principal investigator (MA) and were enrolled in the study.

A clinical trial management system (CTMS)† was used for block randomization. A block size of four was used to ensure an equal number of participants were assigned to each group. A total of 35 enrolled participants were randomized to the SRP alone group and 35 to SRP+MM group.

### Outcome measures

Primary outcomes of PD, CAL, BOP, and GI were recorded using a University of North Carolina (UNC) manual probe for both groups at each visit prior to treatment. CAL was measured by adding or subtracting the distance of the gingival margin to the cemento-enamel junction (CEJ) to the PD recording. BOP was measured as a dichotomic presence or absence of bleeding at 15 seconds after probing. Gingival index was measured utilizing the Löe gingival index system.34 For each tooth, PD, CAL, BOP, and GI were recorded at 6 surfaces (facial, lingual, distal-facial, distal-lingual, mesial-facial, and mesial-lingual).

Secondary outcomes were to quantitate 11 putative periodontal pathogens in the saliva of all subjects at baseline before SRP, the 1-month reevaluation, 3 and 6-month periodontal maintenance visits. A proprietary saliva test* was utilized according to the manufacture instructions. Each participant swished and gargled with 5mL of a sterile saline mixture for at least 30 seconds before expectorating that volume with their saliva in the funnel connected to the collection tube. Following this a 1mL vial of sample preservative was added to the saliva/saline mixture, labels were then affixed, and the closed sample was placed into a plastic zip-style biohazard material bag containing an absorptive pad. The preserved saliva samples were stored at room temperature and transported by a courier to a lab** for processing and analysis. Subsequently, 3.5X saliva DNA preservative‡ was added to the saliva sample to inhibit DNAse activity. Saliva samples were first stored and then shipped at room temperature to Access Genetics lab for processing and analysis.

The 11 periodontal pathogens were measured serially by method of quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). A standard amount of saline oral rinse was processed for genomic DNA, both bacterial and host, which was then used in 4 multiplex qPCR reactions. The fluorescent signals generated in those reactions, were then compared against a series of external controls for each pathogen to an interpolated concentration and expressed in genome/mL of saline oral rinse (the primary sample). Pathogen levels were analyzed by the changes of the aggregate of the 11 pathogen analytes. The combined measure of these 11 pathogens is the cumulative bacterial burden SCORE (scale 0-100). This measure is the sum of the base 10 logarithm of each individual pathogen. The scale ranges from 0-100; a value of 0 means none of the analytes is above the limit of quantification in the assay. A value of 100 means the analytes are at the highest level of measure. Using the log10 value for the concentration of each bacteria type, the pathogenicity of each pathogen was adjusted by the multiplication of a coefficient value corresponding to the high, moderate, and low risk periodontal pathogens. From these scores, changes occurring over time were expressed in the overall bacterial load. All outcome measures were collected by the PI (MA) at the time of each research visit, before treatment for both groups (Figure 1).

### Statistical analysis

All randomized participants were included in the analyses according to the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle regardless of whether they completed the study protocol. Demographic characteristics were summarized using counts and rates or means and standard deviations. Differences between randomization groups at baseline were summarized using the standardized mean difference (SMD). The primary and secondary outcome measures were recorded from baseline to the 6-month periodontal maintenance. They were analyzed using generalized linear mixed-effects models, with fixed effects terms for treatment group, visit, and group-by-visit interaction, and random effects terms for participants. Mixed-effects models account for within-participant correlation of measurements and provide robust estimation in the presence of participant dropout under the missing at random assumption. Pathogen quantities were modeled using a gamma family with log link to account for their positive and right-skewed distributions. Models of PD, CAL, GI, and BOP included additional fixed effects terms for tooth, surface, group-by-tooth, and group-by-surface interactions, and additional random effects for tooth within participant and surface within participant. Similar models also including fixed effects terms for log10 pathogen quantity were used to examine the relationships between pathogen quantity and PD, CAL, GI, and BOP at each visit. Mean changes from baseline were compared between treatment groups by testing the group-by-visit interaction terms for 1-month reevaluation, 3 and 6-month periodontal maintenances. Residual quantile-quantile plots were examined to confirm approximate normality of distribution. Outcomes were summarized using estimated marginal means with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Analyses were conducted using R version 4.1.1 including the lme4 and emmeans packages.

## Results

### Demographic results

Seventy participants were enrolled and 64 completed all study visits (n=64, 91%). Retention for the SRP+MM group was 97% (n=34) and retention for the SRP alone group was 86% (n=30). Table 2 provides the demographic information of the participants ITT. The average age for both groups was >50 years of age. There were more men in both groups (54% SRP; 66% SRP+MM). The majority of the participants identified as Caucasian (69% SRP; 57% SRP+MM). Most participants (84% SRP; 83% SRP+MM) had some college education or higher. The baseline clinical outcomes of PD, CAL, GI, and BOP were similar in both groups (Table 2).

### Pathogenic quantities

The cumulative salivary pathogens (bacterial burden SCORE) decreased from baseline similarly in both groups and remained lower throughout the 6-month periodontal maintenance (Figure 2). Each of the 11 individual pathogens also decreased from baseline in both groups. However, larger decreases were observed in the SRP+MM group compared to SRP alone. Of the 11 pathogens, the SRP+MM group had six (Tannerella forsythia, p=0.003; Treponema denticola, p=0.01; Fusobacterium nucleatum/periodonticum, p=0.0009; Prevotella intermedia, p=0.04; Parvimonas micra, p=<0.0001 and Eikenella corrodens, p=0.02) with significantly larger decreases from baseline compared to SRP alone at the 1-month reevaluation. At the 3-month periodontal maintenance, Tannerella forsythia showed a greater decrease from baseline (p=0.03) for SRP+MM than SRP alone. At the 6-month periodontal maintenance, four of the 11 pathogens in the SRP+MM group showed significantly greater decreases from baseline than SRP alone (Fusobacterium nucleatum/periodonticum (p=0.02), Prevotella intermedia (p=0.05), Campylobacter rectus (p=0.05), and Eikenella corrodens p=0.0002) (Figure 2).

### Clinical measures

Both groups resulted in improved full mouth clinical outcomes of reduced PD, BOP, GI and CAL gains from baseline to all other time points (Table 3). The SRP+MM had greater reductions than SRP alone for BOP (25.8% to 6.8% vs. 23.4% to 8.8%, p<0.0001) and GI (1.34 to 1.06 vs. 1.32 to 1.09, p<0.0001) at the 1-month reevaluation. Reductions were similar for PD (3.49 to 2.80 vs. 3.57 to 2.87, p=0.31) and CAL (3.69 to 3.10 vs. 3.84 to 3.21, p=0.06). At the 3-month periodontal maintenance, the SRP+MM group showed greater improvement from baseline than SRP alone for BOP (25.8% to 5.7% vs. 23.4% to 6.7%, p<0.0001) and somewhat lesser improvement in PD (3.49 to 2.68 vs. 3.57 to 2.73, p=0.03), GI (1.34 to 1.03 vs. 1.32 to 0.98, p=0.04), and CAL (3.69 to 3.00 vs. 3.84 to 3.10, p=0.04). At the 6-month periodontal maintenance, the SRP+MM group had greater improvement from baseline than SRP alone for BOP (25.8% to 5.0% vs. 23.4% to 5.6%, p=0.0003), GI (1.34 to 0.95 vs. 1.32 to 0.96, p=0.0004), similar reductions for PD (3.49 to 2.57 vs. 3.57 to 2.67, p=0.73) and CAL (3.69 to 2.95 vs. 3.84 to 3.10, p=0.77).

For sites PD≥5mm, the SRP+MM group had greater reductions from baseline than SRP alone at the 1-month revaluation for PD (5.40 to 3.80 vs. 5.42 to 3.91, p=0.02), GI (1.70 to 1.11 vs. 1.64 to 1.19, p<0.0001), and BOP (48.8% to 12.6% vs. 45.5% to 16.7%, p=0.0003), and similar reductions in CAL (5.48 to 4.00 vs. 5.54 to 4.11, p=0.37). At the 3-month periodontal maintenance, SRP+MM had greater improvements than SRP alone for PD (5.40 to 3.54 vs. 5.42 to 3.65, p=0.03), GI (1.70 to 1.07 vs. 1.64 to 1.08, p=0.02), BOP (48.8% to 10.4% vs. 45.5% to 12.3%, p=0.01), and similar reductions in CAL (5.48 to 3.79 vs. 5.54 to 3.87, p=0.65). At the 6-month periodontal maintenance, the SRP+MM group achieved greater improvement than SRP alone for all clinical outcomes (PD: 5.40 to 3.34 vs. 5.42 to 3.54, p<0.0001; GI: 1.70 to 0.98 vs. 1.64 vs. 1.02, p=0.0003; CAL: 5.48 to 3.60 vs. 5.54 to 3.81, p=0.002; BOP: 48.8% to 7.6% vs. 45.5% to 11.0%, p=0.001).

More severe site-level metrics (≥5mm) of PD, CAL and greater proportion of GI and percentage of BOP were associated with higher quantities of Porphyromonas gingivalis, Tannerella forsythia, Treponema denticola, Eubacterium nodatum Fusobacterium nucleatum/periodonticum, Prevotella intermedia, Parimonas micra, and Capnocytophaga species (gingavalis, ochracea, sputigena) across all visits, collectively for both groups (Table 4). Specifically, higher numbers of Campylobacter rectus were associated with more severe CAL and higher salivary quantities of Parimonas micra were associated with more severe CAL and BOP. For sites with baseline PD≥5mm, there were larger decreases of Fusobacterium nucleatum/periodonticum, Prevotella intermedia, Campylobacter rectus, and Eikenella corrodens in the SRP+MM group at the 6-month periodontal maintenance, after a reapplication of MM at the 3-month periodontal maintenance. These results showcase the association of the primary clinical outcomes to the secondary outcomes of the 11 putative periodontal pathogens in the saliva.

## DISCUSSION

### Clinical measures

SRP with ultrasonic scaling and hand instrumentation with or without MM improved clinical outcomes (Table 3). In both groups, all sites showed an average of a 1mm reduction in PD and a greater reduction of 1.5mm to 2mm in PD≥5mm. These results align with the existing literature for reduction of PD and CAL gains post SRP.35–38 Although the clinical improvements may seem incremental at each time point for both groups, for sites PD≥5mm, the SRP+MM had a greater reduction from baseline measures in PD (2.07mm) and more CAL (1.88mm) gains compared to baseline measures of SRP alone (PD 1.88mm; CAL 1.74mm). The SRP+MM group had significant reductions of BOP compared to SRP alone in all sites and sites with PD≥5mm. It is noteworthy, BOP has been noted in the literature as weak measure to predict future CAL.38 However, BOP is an essential clinical metric to assess inflammation and has been historically documented as the most useful metric to determine stable versus unstable periodontal pockets.39 This may suggest MM administered immediately following SRP and a reapplication at the 3-month periodontal maintenance in sites of PD≥5mm may further reduce PD, enhanced CAL gains and suggest a benefit to accelerate the healing process towards achieving periodontal pocket stability measured by BOP. When applying these results to a clinical practice setting, the clinician should consider the evidence for the inclusion of MM and determine if the cost/risk will benefit the individual patient compared to the standard of care.

### Periodontal Pathogens

Removal of subgingival calculus and disruption of plaque biofilm with ultrasonic scaling and hand instrumentation has been established in the literature to reduce periodontal pathogens.40–42 In this study, the combined measure of 11 pathogens (bacterial burden SCORE) decreased in both groups and MM did not seem to benefit the overall cumulative pathogen quantities. However, when analyzing the 11 pathogens individually, Tannerella forsythia, Treponema denticola, Fusobacterium nucleatum/periodonticum, Prevotella intermedia, Parvimonas micra and Eikenella corrodens were significantly decreased in the SRP+MM group compared to SRP alone at the 1-month reevaluation. While at the 6-month periodontal maintenance, only Fusobacterium nucleatum/periodonticum, Prevotella intermedia, Campylobacter rectus and Eikenella corrodens were significantly reduced. There was no significance in the difference of the keystone pathogen Porphyromonas gingivalis quantities, but there was a larger significant decrease of red complex pathogens (Tannerella forsythia, Treponema denticola), orange complex pathogens (Fusobacterium nucleatum/periodonticum, Prevotella intermedia, Campylobacter rectus, and Parvimonas micra), and green complex pathogen Eikenella corrodens in the SRP+MM group (Figure 2). MM remains in the periodontal pocket for up to 14 days and the half-life is approximately 16-18 hours. The wider spectrum of pathogens that decreased in the SRP+MM group and greater reductions in PD, BOP and GI and CAL gains at the 1-month reevaluation may be attributed to the application of MM immediately following SRP to promote healing and potentially reduce dysbiosis of the microbial community.

It appears the reapplication of MM at the 3-month periodontal maintenance, not only sustained the trajectory of improved clinical outcomes, but also may have contributed to maintaining lower numbers of Tannerella forsythia, Fusobacterium nucleatum/periodonticum, Campylobacter rectus and Eikenella corrodens by the 6-month periodontal maintenance (Figure 3). Since repeated disruption of biofilm and calculus removal is required to sustain positive clinical outcomes, post SRP, it is not surprising a reapplication of MM may be required to sustain a suppressed level of these 11 pathogens (Figure 3). These findings agree with existing literature that the application of SRP+MM may be advantageous for periodontal subjects with a higher numbers of red, orange and green complex bacteria.31,32,43

This is important information in the context of precision medicine for treating periodontal subjects who may require advanced therapies including locally delivered antibiotics.19 A common challenge is the identification of patient’s pathogenic burden not responding to conventional therapies.19 The identification of specific periodontal pathogens through salivary testing may provide individualized risk assessment for precision periodontics.15,44,45 However, periodontitis is multifactorial inflammatory disease and raises the need for sensitive, specific and reliable individualized saliva testing for diagnosis and treatment interventions. Precision periodontics is still in its infancy to identify valid biomarkers and keystone periodontal pathogens.46

Limitations include the lack of blinded examiner for clinical outcomes, and lack of patient-reported outcome measures. The usage of patient-reported outcome measures would have shown treatment impacts on patients’ oral health-related quality of life. This is important to understand the patients’ perspective of treatment outcomes. The PI collected saliva, recorded clinical measures and provided treatment for both groups. The participants in this study were predominantly from the Midwestern region, male, Caucasian and over the age of 50 years old, which does not represent the general population of individuals with periodontitis.

This is the first known study to assess the shift in 11 pathogens comparing SRP+MM to SRP alone. Longitudinal research should investigate the benefits or risks of long-term administration of SRP+MM and reapplications of MM on these 11 pathogens for sensitivity, specificity, and reliability for precision periodontics. Future research should investigate the impact of SRP+MM on biomarkers Interleukin (IL)-1, 6, tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) and matrix metalloproteinase-8 (MMP-8) in the GCF and serum. In addition, the impact of SRP+MM on the release of acute phase reactants such as high sensitivity C-reactive proteins (hsCRP), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and haptoglobin (Hp) associated with periodontitis should be investigated.

## CONCLUSION

SRP+MM resulted in improved clinical outcomes compared to SRP alone. The cumulative 11 pathogens (bacterial burden SCORE) decreased in both groups. The administration of MM immediately following SRP achieved decreased numbers of Tannerella forsythia, Treponema denticola, Fusobacterium nucleatum/periodonticum, Prevotella intermedia, Parvimonasmicra and Eikenella corrodens by the 1-month reevaluation. The administration of MM following SRP and a reapplication at the 3-month periodontal maintenance appeared to contribute to improved clinical outcomes and sustained the decreased numbers of Fusobacterium nucleatum/periodonticum, Prevotella intermedia, Campylobacter rectus, and Eikenella corrodens at 6 months.

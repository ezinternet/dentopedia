TITLE: Impact of root canal treatment timing on the prognosis of autotransplanted teeth with complete root formation: a systematic review and meta-analysis
AUTHORS: Li S, Chen J, Li S, Ge X
JOURNAL: BMC Oral Health. 2026;26(1).
DOI: 10.1186/s12903-026-08413-4

ABSTRACT

Objective

This systematic review evaluated the influence of root canal treatment (RCT) timing on the prognosis of autotransplanted teeth with complete root formation.

Materials and methods

A systematic review was conducted according to the PRISMA guidelines. This study estimated survival rate, success rate, and root resorption rate of the teeth overall and across three RCT timings: postoperative prophylactic, intraoperative immediate, and clinical observation-based. Subgroup analyses were performed to investigate the influence of tooth type on outcomes.

Results

A total of 24 studies were included, involving 1473 autotransplanted teeth. The overall survival rate was 95% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 91%–97%), the success rate was 92% (95% CI: 80%–97%), and the root resorption rate was 16% (95% CI: 8%–30%). No statistically significant differences were observed in the survival rate and success rate among the postoperative prophylactic RCT group, intraoperative immediate RCT group, and clinical observation-based RCT group (all> 0.05). However, the postoperative prophylactic RCT group exhibited the lowest root resorption rate (11%, 95% CI: 6%–18%).

Conclusions

Current evidence indicates that different timings of RCT exert no significant impact on the overall prognosis of autotransplanted teeth with complete root formation. Nevertheless, postoperative prophylactic RCT may confer potential advantages in reducing the risk of root resorption. Due to the limitations of the non-randomized study design and heterogeneity of the included literature, this conclusion warrants verification by more high-quality studies.

Clinical implications

By systematically clarifying the prognostic differences and temporal evolution patterns associated with different RCT timings, this study provides exploratory observations and reference directions for the field of mature tooth autotransplantation, which is conducive to promoting the standardized application of this technique.

Introduction

Tooth autotransplantation is defined as “the transplantation of an unerupted or erupted tooth from one location to another extraction site or a surgically created new socket within the same individual’s oral cavity” []. Compared with dental implantation, autotransplanted teeth do not impede alveolar bone development in patients during the growth and development stage, maintain the activity of periodontal tissues, possess proprioceptive function, and are compatible with the eruption process of adjacent teeth. They can preserve the natural morphology of gingival papillae, achieving an aesthetic effect identical to that of natural teeth, and when indications are strictly selected, appropriate age groups are targeted, and research designs are standardized, their long-term prognosis is comparable to that of dental implants [–].

Current studies have indicated that the success of tooth autotransplantation is associated with multiple factors, including the patient’s biological sex and age, the type, morphology, location and root development status of the donor tooth, as well as the recipient’s transplantation site, periapical condition of the recipient area, and specific surgical details [–]. Among these factors, root development status has been recognized as one of the most critical determinants of successful tooth autotransplantation [–]. Immature young permanent teeth not only have adequate blood supply and abundant stem cells, but their open apical foramen also provides more favorable conditions for pulp blood supply. These characteristics enable immature young permanent teeth to more easily achieve pulp revascularization and periodontal ligament healing after transplantation, thereby promoting continuous root development and maintaining normal tooth vitality []. Therefore, for young patients, tooth autotransplantation is often regarded as the preferred option for clinical restoration of missing teeth.

Currently, numerous studies have also shown that autotransplantation of mature teeth yields a relatively high success rate [,], with an expected 1-year survival rate of 98.0% and a 5-year survival rate of 90.5% []. Although pulp healing usually cannot be achieved after transplantation, periodontal healing remains unaffected. Since most donor teeth from adult patients have closed apical foramina, clinical evidence has demonstrated that fully developed teeth that have undergone root canal treatment (RCT) exhibit higher survival rates after autotransplantation and a reduced incidence of complications such as inflammatory root resorption, replacement root resorption, and ankylosis [–]. However, some other studies have suggested that a subset of autotransplanted teeth with fully developed roots may possess the potential for revascularization. For such teeth, routine intervention is unnecessary; instead, close monitoring of the transplanted teeth is required, and treatment should be administered only when relevant clinical symptoms and/or radiographic findings indicate the need [–].

There is no unified standardized operational definition for the timing of RCT intervention in autotransplanted teeth. Differences exist among various studies regarding the initiation time of treatment and operational procedures, which can be mainly summarized into the following three approaches: The first is postoperative prophylactic RCT, with the core principle of initiating intervention after the transplanted tooth stabilizes. Most studies initiate treatment 2 weeks postoperatively and complete permanent obturation within 3–4 weeks [,]; however, some adjust the initiation time to 3–6 weeks postoperatively [,,]. In terms of filling materials, calcium hydroxide is generally used for temporary filling and gutta-percha for permanent obturation []. This method can effectively prevent inflammatory root resorption caused by pulp necrosis but has obvious limitations: on the one hand, it requires patients to make multiple follow-up visits, resulting in a relatively long treatment cycle; on the other hand, it imposes high requirements on the operator’s technical proficiency. Overfilling or underfilling of the root canal may induce an inflammatory response in the periapical tissues.

The second is immediate intraoperative RCT, which refers to the one-time completion of in vitro RCT via a retrograde or anterograde approach immediately after the donor tooth is extracted. Currently, most studies do not set strict time limits for the operation, only specifying that the root must be kept moist throughout the in vitro procedure to avoid desiccation injury [,]. This method enables strict control of RCT quality and reduces the number of patient visits, but prolonged in vitro operation may damage the periodontal ligament, thereby affecting the long-term prognosis of the transplanted tooth.

The third is clinical observation-based RCT, whose core involves continuous and close monitoring of pulp vitality after the donor tooth is implanted, followed by timely intervention upon detection of abnormalities. Studies vary in terms of monitoring intervals and intervention thresholds: some recommend conducting pulp vitality tests and periapical radiographs every 4 weeks, and initiating RCT if the tooth shows negative vitality and no radiological signs of periapical healing []; other studies set the initial monitoring interval at 3 weeks, which is later extended to 12 weeks, and start treatment when either clinical symptoms (such as pain, mobility, etc.) or radiological pathological findings (such as root resorption, periapical lesions, etc.) are present. This method fully considers the natural repair potential of the dental pulp []. A long-term follow-up study has confirmed that autotransplanted teeth with fully formed roots do not necessarily require routine RCT, and positive vitality test results in some teeth suggest the possibility of revascularization []. However, this scheme places extremely high demands on the operator’s monitoring capability, requiring regular clinical examinations and radiological evaluations to track the tooth status; otherwise, the optimal treatment timing may be delayed due to the occult progression of inflammation.

At present, there is no systematic review or meta-analysis that thoroughly explores the association between the timing of RCT and prognosis of autotransplanted teeth. Therefore, this study aimed to clarify the relationship between the timing of RCT for autotransplanted teeth with complete root formation and the overall survival rate, overall success rate, and root resorption outcomes.

Materials and methods

Registration

This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta- Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, and it has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with the registration number CRD420251275391.

Patient, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome (PICO) criteria

This review employed the Patient, Intervention, Comparison and Outcome (PICO) framework. The definitions of each element are as follows: Patients who underwent any type of autologous tooth transplantation (P); Different timings of RCT were performed on autotransplanted teeth with complete root formation (I); Indirect or concurrent comparison of different RCT timings (C); The survival rate of the transplanted teeth was set as the primary outcome indicator, and the success rate and root resorption were set as the secondary outcome indicators (O). Therefore, the core question addressed in this systematic review is: “Does the timing of RCT of donor teeth with complete root formation affect the prognosis and clinical outcomes of autologous tooth transplantation?”

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

(1) Primary human studies (2) Studies that reported or contained at least inferable data on survival rate, success rate, and root resorption rate (3) Studies involving teeth with complete root formation (4) Studies published in English.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Studies that did not report or contain at least inferable data on survival rate, success rate, and root resorption rate (2) Studies on autologous tooth transplantation using donor teeth with incomplete root formation (3) Expert opinions and review articles (4) Animal studies or in vitro studies (5) Studies published in languages other than English.

Information sources and literature search

Relevant literature published from the inception of the databases to September 6, 2025 was retrieved from electronic databases (Embase, PubMed, Science Direct, and Web of Science). The detailed search strategies for each electronic database are provided in Supplementary Table 1.The screening and inclusion of literature were independently conducted by two researchers (S.L. and S.L.). First, the titles and abstracts of the literature were initially screened based on the inclusion criteria. If it was impossible to determine whether to include a study solely based on the title and abstract, the full text was obtained for further screening. Any discrepancies arising during the screening process were resolved through discussions between the reviewers or by consulting a third reviewer (J.C.).

Selection of studies

Two independent reviewers (S.L. and S.L.) imported the retrieved literature into the reference management software EndNote 20.0 (Thomson Research Soft, CT, USA). All duplicate studies were excluded first, followed by the screening of selected abstracts to assess their eligibility for inclusion, and the identification of relevant publications for subsequent full-text analysis. Finally, a third reviewer (J.C.) analyzed the aforementioned studies in strict accordance with the pre-established inclusion criteria, so as to confirm the final set of studies that met the requirements.

Data extraction

Relevant data were extracted from each included study, encompassing the authors and publication year, study design, patient age, number of donor teeth, tooth location, timing of endodontic treatment, splint category, survival rate, success rate, root resorption rate, and follow-up duration. Survival was defined as the presence of the tooth during the follow-up duration. Root resorption was defined as the presence of resorptive signs on radiographs of autotransplanted teeth. All data points underwent independent extraction by two reviewers. Owing to ambiguous reporting of study design details in certain articles, corresponding authors were contacted for supplementary clarification to confirm adherence to the predefined inclusion criteria. Discrepancies arising between the two primary reviewers (S.L. and S.L.) were resolved through adjudication by a third independent reviewer (J.C.).

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS). A maximum of 8 points (stars) was designed to fulfil these quality assessment criteria. Studies with 6–8 points (approximately 75% or more of total points) were considered to be of high quality. Studies with ≤ 6 points were suggested to be of low methodological quality. The assessment was conducted independently by two reviewers (S.L. and S.L.). In case of any discrepancies in their evaluations, a third reviewer (J.C.) was consulted to facilitate consensus, thereby minimizing the impact of subjective bias on the assessment results.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (Version 4.5.1) to estimate the pooled proportions of survival rate, success rate, and root resorption rate for the total sample and samples under the three RCT timings. It should be noted that comparisons between groups with different RCT timings mainly relied on indirect inter-study comparisons, as most of the included studies did not directly compare different treatment timings within the same cohort.

This meta-analysis focused primarily on pooled proportion estimation rather than effect size comparison. The original proportion data were subjected to logit transformation to stabilize variance and make the distribution closer to a normal distribution. For studies with zero events, a continuity correction method was applied to adjust the data, thereby avoiding calculation errors caused by extreme values. A random-effects model was used to conduct the meta-analysis, and subgroup analyses were performed with RCT timing, tooth type, follow-up duration, and fixation method as moderator variables respectively, to explore their potential effects on pooled proportions. Owing to the limited number of studies included in each subgroup and the inherent high risk of confounding bias in observational studies, the univariate subgroup analyses in this study were only designated as exploratory analyses. Their purpose was to initially explore the association trends between variables rather than serve as confirmatory analyses. The analysis results were visually presented using forest plots, where the 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for individual studies and the pooled effect were clearly labeled. Meanwhile, the heterogeneity statistic I² was calculated to quantify the degree of inter-study heterogeneity. The significance level for all statistical analyses was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Study selection

The initial electronic search identified 1082 articles. After removing duplicate studies and screening based on titles and abstracts, 44 articles met the criteria for full-text review. Among these, 20 were excluded due to insufficient data to address the research objectives, or because their study population and language did not meet the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 24 articles were deemed eligible for qualitative and quantitative analyses (Fig.).

Study characteristics

The 24 studies included in this review were published between 1967 and 2025, with data sourced from 11 different countries. None of these studies adopted a controlled study design; among them, 12 were retrospective cohort studies and 12 were prospective cohort studies. The sample sizes varied significantly, ranging from 17 to 319 patients, with a mean of 65.3 patients. These studies involved a total of 1,473 autotransplanted teeth with complete root formation. Specifically, 15 studies implemented prophylactic RCT postoperatively, involving 879 autotransplanted teeth; 3 studies performed immediate RCT during the surgical procedure, involving 129 autotransplanted teeth; and 7 studies administered RCT only after the onset of clinical symptoms, involving 434 autotransplanted teeth (Table).

Quality assessment

Eight studies were considered to be of high quality. These eight studies fulfilled the criteria of “representativeness of the exposed cohort”and “demonstration that the outcome of interest was not”, which was the difference between these five studies and other 12 studies scoring≤6points (Supplementary Table 2).

Survival rate

The overall survival rate reported in the studies was 95% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 91%–97%), with no heterogeneity observed across all studies (I² = 0%). The average survival rate was 95% (95% CI: 89%–98%) in the postoperative prophylactic RCT group, 96% (95% CI: 87%–99%) in the intraoperative immediate RCT group, and 93% (95% CI: 91%–95%) in the clinical observation-based RCT group (Fig.). The results of intergroup comparisons showed no statistically significant differences in the pooled survival rates between any two of the three groups: postoperative prophylactic RCT group, intraoperative immediateRCT, and clinical observation group. Specifically, the comparison between the postoperative prophylactic RCT group and the intraoperative immediate RCT group (= 0.83); the comparison between the intraoperative immediate RCT group and the clinical observation-based RCT group (= 0.44); and the comparison between the postoperative prophylactic RCT group and the clinical observation-based RCT group (= 0.47) (Supplementary Fig. 1 ).

Success rate

Nineteen studies involving 778 autotransplanted teeth reported an overall success rate of 92% (95% CI: 80%–97%), with high heterogeneity observed across all studies (I² = 68%).Data on the success rate of the postoperative prophylactic RCT group were derived from 12 studies, including a total of 586 teeth; the weighted average success rate was 92% (95% CI: 78%–98%), with high heterogeneity among the studies (I² = 62%). For the intraoperative immediate RCT group, data were obtained from 3 studies involving 129 teeth, with a success rate of 95% (95% CI: 57%–100%) and high heterogeneity across the studies (I² = 84%). For the clinical observation-based RCT group, data came from 4 studies including 63 teeth, showing a success rate of 84% (95% CI: 35%–98%) and high heterogeneity among the studies (I² = 80%) (Fig.). Intergroup comparisons revealed no statistically significant difference in the success rate between the intraoperative immediate RCT group and the postoperative prophylactic RCT group (= 0.74). Additionally, when each of these two groups was compared with the clinical observation-based RCT group, there were also no statistically significant differences in the success rate (= 0.54,= 0.46) (Supplementary Fig. 2). This high heterogeneity is primarily attributed to inconsistent definitions of success rate among included studies: there are significant differences in the definition of success rate among different studies, with core criteria including: (1) tooth retention; (2) absence of pain, significant mobility, and gingival inflammation; (3) no severe radiological lesions. Twelve studies simultaneously incorporate multi-dimensional criteria such as tissue healing, functional preservation, and imaging findings [,–,,,,,]; five studies prioritize histological healing indicators including periodontal ligament, gingiva, and alveolar bone [,,,,]; one study takes tooth retention as the core and has relatively loose requirements for tissue healing [].

Data on success rates by tooth type were only available for the postoperative prophylactic RCT group and the intraoperative immediate RCT group. In the postoperative prophylactic RCT group, the success rate was 92% for canines (95% CI: 68%–98%) and 95% for third molars (95% CI: 75%–99%). The results showed no statistically significant difference in success rate between canines and third molars (= 0.65). In the intraoperative immediate RCT group, the success rate was 96% for canines (95% CI: 11%–100%) and 97% for third molars (95% CI: 88%–100%). The results demonstrated no statistically significant difference in success rate between canines and third molars (= 0.97) (Fig.).

Root resorption

A total of 16 studies involving 833 teeth were included. The reported overall mean root resorption rate was 16% (95% CI: 8%–30%). High heterogeneity was observed across all studies (I² = 90%). The root resorption rate in the postoperative prophylactic RCT group was 11% (95% confidence interval: 6%–18%), with data derived from 11 studies (including 671 teeth). All included studies showed high heterogeneity (I² = 66%). For the intraoperative immediate RCT group, data were obtained from 3 studies (involving 119 teeth), showing a mean root resorption rate of 40% (95% CI: 9%–83%), with high heterogeneity across all included studies (I² = 95%). In the clinical observation-based RCT group, data were from 2 studies (including 43 teeth), and the mean root resorption rate was 40% (95% CI: 5%–89%), with high heterogeneity observed among the included studies (I² = 89%) (Fig.). The results of intergroup comparisons showed no statistically significant differences in the pooled root resorption rate between any two of the three groups: postoperative prophylactic RCT group, intraoperative immediate RCT group, and clinical observation-based RCT group. Specifically, the comparison between the postoperative prophylactic RCT group and the intraoperative immediate RCT group (= 0.09); the pooled root resorption rate of the intraoperative immediate RCT group was consistent with that of the clinical observation-based RCT group; and the comparison between the postoperative prophylactic RCT group and the clinical observation-based RCT group (= 0.19)(Supplementary Fig. 3 ).

In the intraoperative immediate RCT group, the root resorption rate was 18% for canines (95% CI: 9%–34%) and 40% for third molars (95% CI: 9%–83%), and the difference between these two tooth types was statistically significant (< 0.001) (Fig.).

Follow-up duration

Analysis of the impact of different follow-up duration on the success rate (Fig.) showed that only one study was included in the group with follow-up duration ≤ 3 years, which reported a success rate of 99% (95% CI: 97%–100%); the success rate in the group with follow-up duration ≤ 5 years was 77% (95% CI: 57%–90%) with high heterogeneity (I² = 77%); the success rate in the group with follow-up duration > 5 years was 80% (95% CI: 68%–88%) with high heterogeneity (I² = 72%); the overall analysis results indicated that the total success rate was 83% (95% CI: 67%–92%) with high heterogeneity (I² = 80%).

Analysis of the impact of different follow-up duration on the root resorption rate (Fig.) showed that: the root resorption rate in the group with follow-up duration ≤ 3 years was 6% (95% CI: 1%–22%) with high heterogeneity (I² = 89%); the root resorption rate in the group with follow-up duration ≤ 5 years was 30% (95% CI: 11%–60%) with high heterogeneity (I² = 92%); the root resorption rate in the group with follow-up duration > 5 years was 16% (95% CI: 12%–22%) with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 46%); the overall analysis results indicated that the total root resorption rate was 17% (95% CI: 8%–33%) with high heterogeneity (I² = 91%).

Analysis of the impact of different fixation methods on the success rate (Fig.) showed that the success rate in the rigid fixation group was 88% (95% CI: 66%–96%) with high heterogeneity (I² = 89%,< 0.001); the success rate in the flexible fixation group was 81% (95% CI: 52%–94%) with high heterogeneity (I² = 79%); the overall analysis results indicated that the total success rate was 84% (95% CI: 65%–93%) with high heterogeneity (I² = 81%).

Study design

we conducted a subgroup analysis of survival rate stratified by study design (prospective cohort studies [PCS] vs. retrospective cohort studies [RCS]). Of the 24 included studies, 12 were PCS and 12 were RCS. Regarding RCT timing distribution: In PCS: 7 studies (58.3%) adopted postoperative prophylactic RCT, 3 (25%) intraoperative immediate RCT, and 2 (16.7%) clinical observation-based RCT. In RCS: 8 studies (66.7%) used postoperative prophylactic RCT, 2 (16.7%) intraoperative immediate RCT, and 2 (16.7%) clinical observation-based RCT.

For survival rate outcomes: PCS showed a pooled survival rate of 98% (95% CI: 93%–99%) with no heterogeneity (I²=0%).RCS exhibited a pooled survival rate of 91% (95% CI: 89%–93%) with low heterogeneity (I²=5%). A statistically significant difference in survival rate was identified between the two study designs (= 0.02), which may be attributed to stricter follow-up protocols, standardized data collection, and reduced selection bias in PCS compared to RCS (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Discussion

Autologous tooth transplantation, as a tooth defect restoration strategy with both remarkable biological advantages and favorable aesthetic outcomes, has been extensively applied in clinical practice. For mature donor teeth with fully formed roots, the regenerative potential of the dental pulp and blood vessels is rather limited. Thus, RCT has become a crucial intervention to ensure favorable transplantation prognosis, yet a consistent consensus on the optimal timing of treatment remains elusive []. The studies included in this review span a 58-year period from 1967 to 2025. The preferences for treatment timing in different eras exhibited distinct temporal characteristics, reflecting the profound impacts of evolving clinical perceptions and technological advances on treatment decision-making. From a chronological perspective, the selection of RCT timing for autologous transplantation of mature permanent teeth can be divided into three phases.

In the early exploratory phase (1967–1986, 6 studies), three therapeutic approaches—immediate intraoperative RCT (2 studies), postoperative prophylactic RCT (3 studies) and the clinical observation-based RCT (1 study)—coexisted with relatively balanced proportions. This phenomenon directly reflected the lack of a unified standard in clinical perceptions regarding the optimal RCT timing after mature permanent tooth transplantation at that time. Entering the stable dominant phase (1991–2018, 11 studies), postoperative prophylactic RCT emerged as the absolute mainstream protocol with a high proportion of 83%. This transition was mainly attributed to the advancement and optimization of RCT techniques, the continuous accumulation of relevant evidence-based data, and the preliminary guidance of early clinical guidelines, which gradually advocated postoperative short-term prophylactic RCT as the conventional approach for autologous transplantation of mature permanent teeth []. Meanwhile, immediate intraoperative RCT was less frequently adopted, as it might prolong the extraoral time of the transplanted tooth and repeated manipulations were prone to cause periodontal ligament damage [,,]. As for RCT following clinical observation, only 2 studies retained this protocol. This approach was mostly applied to the transplantation of immature permanent teeth with incompletely formed apices, whereas it was rarely used in mature permanent tooth transplantation [,]. In recent years, the selection of treatment timing during the diversified development phase (2021–2025, 7 studies) has exhibited a trend toward refinement: postoperative prophylactic RCT still maintained a dominant position with a proportion of 57.1%, while the clinical observation-based RCT increased significantly to 42.9%.This shift can be attributed to two main factors. On the one hand, it benefits from the precise preoperative evaluation using high-resolution CBCT, the improvement of postoperative Doppler dynamic monitoring technology, the minimally invasive preparation of alveolar sockets, and the application of bioactive material-assisted therapy [–]. The advancement of these techniques minimizes the damage to the periodontal ligament caused by surgical procedures, thereby providing a safety guarantee for the implementation of the clinical observation-based RCT. On the other hand, it stems from the re-evaluation of the pulp regeneration potential of mature permanent teeth. Several studies have demonstrated that for mature permanent teeth with mild root resorption and good periodontal ligament integrity, the local pulp repair capacity may be higher than previously assumed, which provides a theoretical basis for avoiding overtreatment [,].

This study demonstrated that the overall survival rate of transplanted teeth across the three RCT timing protocols reached 95% (95% CI: 91%–97%), with an overall success rate of 92% (95% CI: 80%–97%). These findings were largely consistent with the conclusions of previous systematic reviews. A systematic review published by Huang et al. in 2023 reported a 1-year survival rate of 98.0% and a 5-year survival rate of 90.5% for autotransplanted mature teeth, which further confirms that autologous transplantation of teeth with fully formed roots yields favorable and stable clinical outcomes []. Similarly, a 2025 meta-analysis conducted by Yang et al., which focused on the postoperative outcomes of autotransplanted mature third molars, documented an overall survival rate of 96%, showing a high degree of concordance with the results of the present study []. Notably, no statistically significant differences were observed in survival and success rates among the three groups stratified by treatment timing. Although this finding seems to contradict the clinical emphasis placed on the optimal timing of RCT, it actually reflects the multifactorial synergistic nature of the prognosis of autologous mature tooth transplantation []. Following transplantation of mature teeth, periodontal ligament healing represents the core process determining long-term survival, whereas the primary role of RCT is to prevent the interference of inflammatory responses induced by pulp necrosis with periodontal healing [,]. Regardless of the treatment timing adopted, the fundamental prognosis can be ensured as long as pulp-derived inflammation is effectively controlled. Postoperative prophylactic RCT prevents the occurrence of inflammation through preemptive intervention; immediate intraoperative RCT eliminates potential risks via one-step operation; and the clinical observation-based RCT controls inflammation promptly once symptoms manifest. Despite the differences in intervention time points, all three strategies achieve the core goal of inflammation control, which may account for the absence of significant prognostic differences between groups.

However, in terms of root resorption rate, the postoperative prophylactic RCT group exhibited the lowest root resorption rate (11%, 95% CI: 6%–18%), whereas the rates in both the immediate intraoperative RCT group and the clinical observation-based RCT group were as high as 40%. Moreover, the difference between the postoperative prophylactic RCT group and the immediate intraoperative RCT group approached statistical significance (= 0.09). This finding suggests that different treatment timings may have potential advantages and disadvantages in complication control. Consistent with the results of several existing studies, the initiation of prophylactic RCT at 2 weeks postoperatively not only allocates an optimal window for periodontal ligament healing during the initial postoperative phase of transplanted teeth, thereby avoiding mechanical damage to periodontal tissues caused by premature intervention, but also timely blocks the inflammatory process before signs of pulp necrosis become evident, thus minimizing the risk of root resorption []. In contrast, immediate intraoperative RCT has to be performed with the donor tooth in an extraoral state, which inevitably prolongs the extraoral exposure time of the donor tooth. This may lead to reduced viability of periodontal ligament cells and, in turn, increase the incidence of root resorption [].Nevertheless, given that the relevant analysis was based on a limited number of studies with high heterogeneity, and might be confounded by factors such as anatomical variations of teeth and the operator’s proficiency, the aforementioned results can only serve as preliminary references for the clinical selection of RCT timing.

In the postoperative prophylactic RCT group, no statistically significant differences were observed in either the success rate (92% vs. 95%) or the root resorption rate (16% vs. 13%) between canines and third molars. This finding indicates that this treatment timing protocol may have good applicability to different tooth types.In the immediate intraoperative RCT group, although there was no significant difference in the success rate between canines and third molars, the root resorption rate of third molars increased remarkably. This phenomenon might be attributed to the limitations of immediate intraoperative treatment in managing teeth with complex root canal anatomy [].It should be emphasized, however, that given the limited sample size of the relevant subgroup analyses, wide confidence intervals and the interference of multiple confounding factors, the above findings can only serve as preliminary references for clinical treatment, and cannot yet be regarded as reliable evidence for selecting the optimal RCT timing for autologous tooth transplantation.

This study has certain limitations that should be taken into consideration when interpreting the results. Firstly, all the 24 included studies were of non-controlled design, with a lack of randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Among them, 16 studies were at moderate risk of bias and 2 at high risk of bias, which might have led to potential confounding biases in the results. Secondly, there was high heterogeneity across the studies regarding the success rate and root resorption rate, mainly due to the substantial variations in follow-up duration among different studies, inconsistent definitions of success and the confounding effects of factors such as tooth type and immobilization method. Thirdly, the sample size of the observation-based RCT group was relatively small, and only 2 studies reported data on root resorption rate, which might have reduced the statistical power of comparisons between this group and the other groups. In addition, due to data availability constraints, this study failed to further analyze the differences in treatment success rate and root resorption rate between canines and third molars within this group. Finally, due to insufficient information, this study did not take into account the impacts of individual factors such as patient age, biological sex, smoking history, and baseline periodontal status on the efficacy of different treatment timings; these factors may interact with treatment timing and thus influence the final prognosis. In summary, the aforementioned limitations are closely related to the characteristics of the initial study pool included in this systematic review.

Future research can be carried out in the following directions. First, conduct large-sample, long-term follow-up randomized controlled trials to directly compare postoperative prophylactic RCT, immediate intraoperative RCT, and clinical observation-based RCT group, so as to identify the optimal treatment timing. Second, expand the sample size of the clinical observation-based RCT group, refine the monitoring indicators and follow-up procedures, and clarify the applicable population and monitoring thresholds for this strategy. Third, perform subgroup analyses to explore the interactions between treatment timing and factors such as age, biological sex, tooth type, root canal anatomical complexity, and systemic health status, thereby providing evidence for the formulation of personalized treatment protocols.

In conclusion, by systematically sorting out the prognostic differences and chronological evolution patterns of different RCT timings, this study provides exploratory observations and reference directions for the field of autologous mature tooth transplantation, which is conducive to promoting the standardized application of this technique. No statistically significant differences were found in the overall prognosis of autologous transplanted teeth with fully formed roots among the different RCT timing protocols. However, initiating prophylactic RCT at 2 weeks postoperatively has potential advantages in complication control. High-quality studies are needed in the future to further optimize the selection of treatment timing and achieve precise improvement of the prognosis of transplanted teeth.

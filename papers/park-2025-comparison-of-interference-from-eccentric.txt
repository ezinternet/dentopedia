## ABSTRACT
PURPOSEThe purpose of this clinical study was to evaluate the extent of intraoral occlusal adjustment required for zirconia crowns designed with a dynamic jaw motion tracking method compared to a conventional approach.
MATERIALS AND METHODSFifteen patients needing zirconia crown restorations in the anterior or posterior regions participated in this study. Following tooth preparation, dynamic jaw motion tracking records were gathered using a tracking device. These records were imported into computer-aided design software and aligned with scanned upper and lower jaw data to design each crown's occlusal surface. Two crowns were fabricated for each patient: one using motion tracking data and another without it. Crowns were scanned pre- and post-adjustment following standard protocols. The scanned data were analyzed with 3D inspection software to calculate occlusal adjustments in the segmented occlusal area as root mean square values, with a paired t-test used for statistical analysis (α = 0.05).
RESULTSCrowns designed with motion tracking data required significantly less intraoral occlusal adjustment than those designed conventionally (P = .028).
CONCLUSIONDynamic jaw motion tracking in crown design reduces the extent of intraoral occlusal adjustment, potentially enhancing clinical efficiency.

## INTRODUCTION

The facebow transfer technique is used to accurately relay a patient's functional and aesthetic elements to an adjustable articulator.12 This process transfers the maxillary arch's position to the articulator's hinge axis through a three-dimensional relationship involving the skull and mandible. Prosthodontics textbooks highlight the need for facebows to position the maxillary model accurately on the articulator for various dental prostheses.1234 However, debate continues on whether traditional methods like facebow transfer consistently yield better clinical outcomes.56 Conventional facebow transfer is time-intensive and labor-dependent, often resulting in variable outcomes based on operator experience. As technology continues to evolve and the digital prosthetic workflow becomes more popular and accessible, it is necessary to test the accuracy of converting a patient's occlusal relationship digitally compared to conventional methods. The advantages of digital occlusal transfer include reducing the time required for laboratory procedures, increasing efficiency, and improving patient comfort when collecting diagnostic information. Digital methods, such as cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), facial scans, and cephalometric radiographs, aim to simplify the positioning of the maxillary arch.789101112 However, studies have noted discrepancies in maxillary model positioning using digital CBCT-based facebow methods versus traditional transfers, indicating that significant inaccuracies can occur in the conventional method.131415

Intraoral scanners, CBCT, and facial scanning devices play crucial roles in capturing patient data digitally, enhancing treatment planning, and refining prosthesis design and fabrication.78910111216 Through these methods, virtual models of the maxilla and mandible can be aligned on a virtual articulator, with optical mandibular motion tracking systems simulating mandibular movement.17181920212223 This allows digital recordings of mandibular relationships and movements to create more accurate prosthetic designs. Research on posterior restorations using optical mandibular motion tracking systems has been documented,24 and several in vitro studies have assessed dental prostheses fabricated with this technology,202526 but the accuracy of using jaw motion tracking is still uncertain, and there is a lack of studies evaluating the quantitative differences compared to traditional methods, and especially studies exploring its clinical application for single crown restorations remain limited.

Thus, the purpose of this clinical study was to assess the clinical utility of dynamic jaw motion tracking in creating single zirconia crowns. Specifically, occlusal adjustment requirements for crowns made with dynamic jaw motion tracking were measured and compared with those fabricated through conventional methods. The null hypothesis of this study was that there is no significant difference in occlusal adjustment requirements between crowns designed using dynamic jaw motion tracking and those designed with conventional methods.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study received approval from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Kyungpook National University Dental Hospital (KNUDH-2023-09-04-01), and 15 participants were recruited. Participants were over 20 years old, had no systemic diseases, and required a single zirconia restoration on either the maxilla or mandible. Patients with temporomandibular joint disorders were excluded to ensure consistent mandibular movement. Tooth preparation involved a 1.5 mm reduction in structure with a chamfer margin for zirconia restoration. The clinical procedures of this study are shown in Figure 1. The sagittal condylar angles, Bennett angles, and selected occlusal schemes of the clinical trial participants were recorded and summarized in Table 1. This data was used to account for individual variability in mandibular movements and occlusal design during the fabrication of crowns.

The digital facebow system (Zebris for Ceramill; Amann Girrbach, Koblach, Austria) includes the optic facebow, bite fork, para-occlusal attachment, lower jaw sensor, and T-pointer. Cameras on the optic facebow capture Light Emitting Diode (LED) patterns from the lower jaw sensor, measuring three-dimensional distances and angles. The facebow was positioned on each patient's head, with the T-pointer and attached sensor aligned at the external auditory meatus and facing forward. This alignment was repeated bilaterally to ensure the articulator reference plane (Artex; Girrbach dental, Pforzheim, Germany) was accurately registered in the software. For maxillary positioning on the virtual articulator, bite impression material (Cresil bite; CREDEN, Daegu, Korea) was applied to the bite fork, positioned on the maxillary teeth, and allowed to set. The lower jaw sensor was then attached to the anterior part of the bite fork to enable the virtual transfer of the maxillary arch position via the optic facebow.

A para-occlusal attachment was fabricated with resin (Pattern resin; GC, Tokyo, Japan) and attached to the patient's mandibular anterior region (Fig. 2A). The lower jaw sensor was secured to this attachment, and left lateral, right lateral, protrusive, and opening movements were recorded three times each, following operator instructions. The bite fork from the tracking device was scanned using a desktop scanner (DOF Freedom HD, DOF, Seoul, Korea), and all collected data were exported for integration with computer-aided design (CAD) software (exocad; exocad GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany) (Fig. 2B).

Impressions were taken using a prefabricated tray suited to the patient's arch and a putty-wash technique with rubber impression material. These impressions were used to create plaster casts, which were scanned with a desktop scanner to produce virtual working casts.

The process of designing a crown using the conventional method is as follows: First, a scanned virtual cast is imported into the CAD software. Next, the path of insertion is set, and the margin is marked. Then, a crown design file from the CAD software's library is selected. The dental technician individually adjusts the design, taking into account the occlusal contact with the opposing teeth and the wear of adjacent teeth, to create an appropriate crown occlusal surface.

The crown design process using the dynamic jaw motion tracking method involved additional modifications to the initial crown design file created through the conventional method. First, the virtual bite fork data was matched and aligned with the maxillary virtual working cast in the CAD software. The patient's bite fork data was matched with the bite fork library in the CAD software. Then, digital facebow records were imported to perform a digital facebow transfer. The recorded bite motion data was also imported into the CAD software. Using the dynamic occlusal simulation function within the CAD software, the crown's occlusal surface was modified (Fig. 2).

Each patient's virtual cast was used to design two crowns: one using the conventional method and one with dynamic jaw motion tracking. Crowns were fabricated from zirconia using a 5-axis milling machine (CRAFT 5X DC; DOF, Seoul, Korea) with presintered zirconia blocks (CeramillZolid HT; Amann Girrbach, Koblach, Austria). Following milling, the crowns were sintered by the manufacturer's instructions and cleaned of debris. The two crowns were blind-coded and placed in a transparent container for random assignment to the clinical evaluator.

Before intraoral placement, each crown's external surface was scanned using an intraoral scanner (i700; MEDIT, Seoul, Korea), calibrated according to the manufacturer's recommendations before each use. After intraoral placement, the fit of the proximal and internal surfaces was evaluated, followed by occlusal assessment (Fig. 3). Occlusal contacts in intercuspal occlusion and lateral interference were assessed using 21 µm articulating paper (Check-Film II; Caicedo, Miami, FL, USA) (Fig. 3). Initial occlusal contact points in maximal intercuspation were identified using the articulating paper (Fig. 3). Premature contact points on the zirconia occlusal surface were adjusted using a zirconia-specific finishing bur (Jota zirconia polishing kit; Jota, Rüthi, Switzerland) and a low-speed handpiece (NSK EX-6; NSK, Kanuma, Japan) until occlusal contact was achieved with the opposing teeth. After adjusting for intercuspal occlusion, premature contacts in lateral interference were identified and adjusted similarly until no premature contacts remained (Fig. 3). Following occlusal adjustments in both intercuspal occlusion and lateral interference (Fig. 3), the crowns' external surfaces were rescanned using the intraoral scanner. To minimize the tolerances that may occur during the fabrication process of zirconia crowns, the adjusted crown scan file was compared not with the design file but directly with the scan file of the pre-adjusted crown after fabrication.

The scanned crown surface files were imported into a 3D inspection software program (Geomagic Control X; 3D Systems, Rock Hill, SC, USA) to assess the occlusal adjustment amount (Fig. 4). Scanned data before adjustment were loaded into Geomagic Control X, and regions were segmented (Fig. 4A). For the posterior teeth, segmentation was performed 1 mm below the line angle dividing the occlusal and axial surfaces, while for anterior teeth, segmentation included the entire palatal side and incisal line angle. Post-adjustment crown scans were also imported, aligned, and evaluated for the amount of removed adjustment region (Fig. 4C). Segmentation for posterior teeth was limited to the lower occlusal portion, while anterior teeth were aligned on the labial surface to optimize the alignment of unaltered regions and calculate the removed areas (Fig. 4D).

To assess the occlusal adjustment amount, the deviations before and after adjustment were calculated for all designated surface data points as root mean square (RMS) using the following formula:

RMS=1n·∑i=1nX1,i−X2,i2

where X1,i is the coordinate of each measurement point on the pre-adjusted scan, X2,i is the coordinate on the post-adjusted scan, and n is the number of points analyzed. A lower RMS value indicates less occlusal adjustment required. A qualitative assessment was also performed using color deviation maps.

Data were analyzed using statistical software (SPSS version 27.0; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) with a significance level set at α = 0.05. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to confirm the normality of data distribution, and paired t-tests were employed to statistically analyze occlusal adjustment amounts between crowns fabricated using the two methods.

## RESULTS

The mean occlusal adjustment for crowns fabricated with the conventional method was 91.8 µm, while crowns designed using dynamic jaw motion tracking required an adjustment of 67.5 µm. Statistical analysis confirmed that crowns made with motion-tracking data needed significantly less intraoral adjustment than those designed conventionally (P = .028). There was no statistically significant difference in the adjustment amounts between zirconia crowns fabricated for anterior and posterior regions (P = .450).

Color deviation maps generated through 3D analysis software identified areas of adjustment for all 30 crowns from 15 patients (Fig. 5). These maps consistently aligned with quantitative data, showing greater deviations in crowns made via the conventional method (Table 2 and Fig. 6).

## DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated that zirconia crowns designed with CAD incorporating dynamic jaw motion tracking required significantly less intraoral occlusal adjustment than those created with conventional methods, leading to the rejection of the null hypothesis (P = .028). The primary objective of this clinical trial was to evaluate the clinical utility of dynamic jaw motion tracking for single-crown restorations, and the findings indicate that this method can enhance outcomes by reducing the need for occlusal adjustments.

In contrast to Peng et al.,27 who observed no significant differences in occlusal adjustment for digital occlusal devices designed with both digital articulators and dynamic jaw motion tracking, this study applied motion tracking specifically to prosthetic crowns, supporting its utility for single-crown applications.

Theoretically, dynamic jaw motion tracking records patient-specific parameters and replicates mandibular movement pathways, potentially allowing for more precise prosthesis fabrication.1718192021222324252627 However, due to factors such as cost and time, this method is not yet commonly used in clinical practice. There are very few studies that quantitatively assess how useful dynamic jaw motion tracking is for prosthesis fabrication in a clinical setting. Thus, the significance of this study lies in its clinical and quantitative evaluation of the differences. Instead of an in vitro experiment, this study directly applied the method to patients' prostheses and evaluated the results by comparing the amount of occlusal adjustment, thereby assessing the clinical feasibility of the dynamic jaw motion tracking system.

The findings of this study support that using the dynamic jaw motion tracking method enables a more accurate reproduction of patients' oral conditions compared to not using it. By employing the dynamic jaw motion tracking method, the transfer of the patient's actual oral condition to the digital articulator could be performed more precisely, likely resulting in fewer premature contact points during lateral interference.

A skilled dental technician oversaw the prosthesis fabrication process, underscoring the importance of accuracy in prosthetic production for optimal outcomes.28 Potential inaccuracies during zirconia milling and polishing were minimized by instructing the clinician to make only necessary adjustments during fitting. To mitigate subjective bias, the prostheses were blind-coded before being assigned to the clinician, although precise quantification of occlusal adjustments remains challenging and could lead to minor over-adjustments.

Adjustments for premature contacts in intercuspal occlusion are typically unrelated to dynamic jaw motion tracking, as they often arise from impression inaccuracies and laboratory fabrication errors.29 Ideally, minimal intercuspal adjustment should be required to effectively compare dynamic jaw motion tracking effects on lateral interference; excessive intercuspal adjustment may reduce the accuracy of mandibular motion data and the application of individual articulator parameters.

When the occlusal surface of the prosthesis is adjusted using dynamic jaw motion tracking data with the dynamic occlusal simulation function, it is theoretically expected that only the lateral interference areas will be adjusted, while the areas in maximal intercuspation remain unchanged. However, as shown in Figure 6, the crowns fabricated using jaw motion tracking required more overall occlusal adjustment compared to those fabricated using the conventional method. This could be attributed to errors occurring during the crown fabrication process or excessive adjustments made by the clinician in certain areas during the occlusal adjustment process. However, the most likely explanation is the inaccuracy in bite registration at maximal intercuspation, which may have caused the contact areas at maximal intercuspation to also be adjusted during the modification of the occlusal surface using the dynamic occlusal simulation function.

In this study, para-occlusal attachments were temporarily affixed to mandibular teeth using pattern resin, which allowed for slight movement during lateral and protrusive movements, potentially introducing minor recording errors. Additionally, some patients may not have fully relaxed while using the tracking device, possibly impacting the precision of recorded data.

In this study, the crowns were scanned after completing occlusal adjustment in the MICP state and then again after completing occlusal adjustment for lateral interference. The amount of occlusal adjustment was compared between the pre-adjustment crowns and the post-adjustment crowns. It is necessary to specifically compare the amount of adjustment after occlusal adjustment for eccentric movement by scanning the crowns after completing occlusal adjustment in the MICP state and after completing occlusal adjustment for lateral interference. Future studies should more accurately compare the amount of occlusal adjustment during eccentric movement.

It is challenging to conclude that a prosthesis is accurate solely based on a lesser amount of occlusal adjustment during eccentric movement. Since this study compared the two crowns only by examining the amount of occlusal adjustment during eccentric movement, future research should further investigate factors such as masticatory efficiency, patient satisfaction, and comparisons before and after prosthesis placement to determine more accurately how to create an optimal prosthesis.

While this study supports the utility of dynamic jaw motion tracking in single-tooth restorations, the high cost and time-intensive nature of this approach may limit its application, particularly for single crowns. Future studies might consider broader applications, such as for bridges or removable prostheses. A control group utilizing real mandibular motion trajectories for prosthetic design would enable comparisons across three methods, allowing for a more comprehensive assessment of jaw motion tracking's reliability and accuracy.

Digital facebow transfer methods and jaw motion tracking devices are emerging to address limitations in traditional analog transfers, creating virtual patients for more tailored prosthetic solutions.7891011121314151617181920212223 These digital approaches can improve prosthesis accuracy and save time, particularly in cases where precise replication of a patient's natural lateral and protrusive movements is necessary for optimal restoration outcomes.

## CONCLUSION

This study compared the intraoral occlusal adjustment requirements of zirconia crowns fabricated using digital and conventional methods, assessing the clinical utility of each approach. Crowns made with dynamic jaw motion tracking required significantly less intraoral adjustment than those fabricated by traditional methods, likely due to the motion tracking's ability to replicate patient-specific mandibular movements in the CAD process, thereby enhancing occlusal compatibility.

The digital crown fabrication method incorporating dynamic jaw motion tracking may reduce intraoral adjustments, potentially improving clinical efficiency and patient satisfaction. These findings suggest that dynamic jaw motion tracking could be more widely adopted in dental prosthesis fabrication, contributing to advancements in digital dentistry.

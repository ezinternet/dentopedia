TITLE: Trueness and the amount of clinical adjustment for zirconia crowns fabricated by digital light processing at different build angles: a within-patient clinical study

## ABSTRACT
PURPOSEThis clinical study evaluated the effect of build angle (0°, 45°, and 90°) in digital light processing (DLP) printing on the trueness and chairside adjustment of zirconia crowns and compared these outcomes with conventionally milled crowns.
MATERIALS AND METHODSTen patients each received one milled zirconia crown and three DLP-printed zirconia crowns fabricated at 0°, 45°, and 90° build angles (40 crowns total; four crowns per patient). Trueness was calculated as the root mean square (RMS) surface deviation between intraoral scans of the crowns and the CAD design. Occlusal and intaglio adjustments were quantified as the RMS deviation between pre- and post-adjustment scans. Data were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with manufacturing method as a fixed effect and patient as a random intercept to account for within-patient clustering. Post hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey-adjusted pairwise contrasts of estimated marginal means (α = .05).
RESULTSManufacturing method significantly influenced trueness in the primary linear mixed-effects model (χ2 = 20.689, P = .001). Tukey-adjusted contrasts showed lower RMS for the 0° group (49.0 µm) than for the 45° group (63.3 µm; P = .033), 90° group (70.5 µm; P = .003), and milled group (66.2 µm; P = .012). Manufacturing method also affected occlusal adjustment (χ2 = 13.904, P = .003) and intaglio adjustment (χ2 = 28.624, P < .001). The milled group required more occlusal adjustment than the 0° group (P = .011), and greater intaglio adjustment than all DLP-printed groups (all P ≤ .004).
CONCLUSIONCompared with milling, DLP-printed zirconia crowns demonstrated greater trueness (lower RMS) and required less chairside adjustment. Under the tested conditions, a 0° build angle was associated with the lowest trueness RMS and the least adjustment requirements.

## INTRODUCTION

Zirconia is widely used in prosthodontics because of its high strength, wear resistance, and biocompatibility; it has traditionally been manufactured by computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing (CAD-CAM) milling.123 Although milling provides clinically reliable accuracy, it generates material waste, leads to progressive tool wear, and requires relatively long fabrication times.4 To address these limitations, additive manufacturing (AM), particularly digital light processing (DLP), has emerged as a promising alternative. With recent advances in ceramic-compatible materials and printing hardware, DLP printing now enables the production of high-strength zirconia definitive prostheses.56

Recent evidence suggests that, under appropriate conditions, 3D printing may reduce chairside adjustment and improve clinical efficiency compared with milling. Giannetti et al. reported lower occlusal adjustments for printed provisional crowns than for milled crowns derived from identical CAD designs.7 Alghauli et al. demonstrated that contemporary printed zirconia can achieve favorable fit and esthetic outcomes comparable with those of milled restorations.8 The quality of these restorations can be assessed in terms of fabrication reproducibility and the extent of intraoral adjustment required. Reproducibility describes the deviation from the intended geometry and comprises trueness, defined as the closeness to the CAD reference, and precision, defined as repeatability among builds.910 The magnitude of occlusal and intaglio adjustment reflects the clinical effort required to achieve functional and internal adaptation. Excessive adjustment may distort internal geometry, weaken the adhesive interface, prolong chairside time, and compromise long-term performance.11 Minimizing intraoral adjustment while maintaining accurate adaptation is therefore an important clinical objective.

In DLP printing, process parameters, particularly build angle, govern layer stacking and support placement and sintering-related shrinkage paths, thereby influencing geometric fidelity and the need for clinical adjustment. Mou et al. reported that build angle and layer thickness affected the accuracy of DLP-printed zirconia crowns, with favorable results at a layer thickness of 30 µm and build angles of 0° and 45°.12

However, the clinical translation of these in vitro findings to high-strength zirconia restorations has not been extensively validated. Previous studies have frequently relied on simplified models that do not fully reflect the complexity of clinical occlusal adjustment or the sintering behavior of zirconia. Therefore, a need exists for within-patient evidence evaluating how build parameters influence both fabrication trueness and the actual extent of required chairside modification.

Accordingly, this clinical study conducted a with-in-patient comparison of single zirconia crowns fabricated by DLP 3D printing at build angles of 0°, 45°, and 90° and by conventional milling. The null hypothesis was that trueness and intraoral adjustment would not differ among the three build angles or between printed and milled crowns.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

This prospective within-patient comparative clinical study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyungpook National University Dental Hospital (KNUDH-2024-08-02-00) and conducted from September 2024 to February 2025. Ten adults requiring a single zirconia crown for a maxillary or mandibular molar were enrolled after providing written informed consent. Sample size was determined based on a pilot study involving three patients (12 crowns) to estimate the expected effect size and variance of the primary outcome. Statistical power analysis was performed using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). Based on the pilot results, which yielded a large effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.58), a significance level (α = .05), and a statistical power (1-β = .80) for a within-patient design with four measurements, a minimum sample size of nine patients was required. Consequently, to ensure adequate statistical power and account for potential technical failures, ten adults were ultimately enrolled.

Inclusion criteria comprised stable periodontal conditions, functional opposing occlusion, and good general health. To maintain a standardized occlusal environment, enrollment was restricted to patients with natural dentition or fixed prostheses in the adjacent and opposing positions. Exclusion criteria encompassed the need for multiple restorations in the same quadrant, severe occlusal plane discrepancies, active periodontitis, and high caries activity. Patients with parafunctional habits (such as bruxism or clenching) or those requiring removable partial dentures (RPDs) or complete dentures (CDs) in the treatment quadrant or opposing arch were excluded to eliminate variables that could compromise occlusal stability. Under this within-patient design, each participant received four restorations (one milled and three DLP-printed at 0°, 45°, and 90° build angles), yielding a total of 40 experimental crown datasets, and enabling direct comparison of manufacturing methods while controlling for individual biological variables.

All tooth preparations were performed by a single operator following a standardized protocol. Each abutment was prepared with a total convergence angle of 6°, a 0.5-mm chamfer finish line, 1.5-mm occlusal reduction, and 1.0-mm axial reduction using a water-cooled high-speed handpiece (ExpertLUX 900; Kavo, Biberach, Germany) and diamond burs (ISO 806314001524012; Shofu, Kyoto, Japan). Impressions were made using polyvinyl siloxane material (Aquasil Ultra+; Dentsply Sirona, Charlotte, NC, USA). Working dies were fabricated with type IV dental stone (Fujirock EP; GC, Tokyo, Japan) mixed under vacuum (Vacuum Mixer Twister; Renfert, Hilzingen, Germany). Stone models were digitized using a desktop scanner (FREEDOM; DOF, Seoul, Korea) to obtain virtual working models.

Crowns were designed using dental CAD software (exocadDentalCAD; exocad GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany) with a uniform cement space of 50 µm. For each participant, four crowns were fabricated from the same CAD design (CAD reference model): one crown by five-axis milling and three crowns by DLP printing at build angles of 0°, 45°, and 90° (Fig. 1). The try-in sequence of the four crowns within each patient was determined using a computer-generated randomization list (random permuted blocks of four), prepared by an independent coordinator. Allocation was concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes until chairside try-in. Consequently, this study was conducted under double-blind conditions, with both clinicians and patients blinded to the manufacturing method and build angle of each crown throughout the clinical procedures.

For the milled control group, zirconia crowns were produced using a five-axis machining unit (Motion 2; Amann Girrbach, Koblach, Austria) from zirconia blocks (DMAX; DMAX, Seoul, Korea). Milling tool-paths were generated using CAM software (Ceramill Match 2; Amann Girrbach, Koblach, Austria) with a parameter setting of 0.05 mm. Milling was performed under water cooling at 60,000 rpm using milling burs (Ceramill Roto; Amann Girrbach, Koblach, Austria) with diameters of 2.5, 1.0, and 0.6 mm. The crowns were sintered in a furnace (Zirkonofen 700 Ultra V2; Zirkonzahn, Gais, Italy) at 1,500℃ with a heating rate of 10℃/min and a holding time of 2 hours. For the DLP groups, zirconia crowns were printed using a ceramic DLP printer (TD6; 3D CONTROLS, Busan, Korea) and zirconia slurry (3DCera; 3D CONTROLS). Printing parameters were: layer thickness, 25 µm; light source, 405-nm light-emitting diode (15 mW/cm2); exposure time, 2.5 seconds per layer; and platform temperature, 25℃. Build angles were set to 0°, 45°, and 90°. Supports were positioned away from the margins and anticipated occlusal contact areas to minimize surface artifacts. Printed crowns were cleaned using a direct spray nozzle cleaner (C1; 3D CONTROLS). Debinding and sintering were subsequently performed in a furnace (R0; 3D CONTROLS). Debinding was conducted over 7 hours using a multistage heating protocol within the range of 165 – 1100℃. After debinding, the crowns were sintered at 1450℃ for 2 hours and cooled at a rate of 5℃/min. No glazing or additional surface finishing was performed for either the milled control group or the three printed experimental groups before clinical evaluation.

Before clinical try-in, pre-adjustment scans were acquired using an intraoral scanner (i700; Medit, Seoul, Korea) after calibration performed immediately before data acquisition. Calibration was conducted in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations to minimize potential precision drift and maintain optimal scanner performance, as emphasized in previous studies on intraoral scanning system maintenance.13 For each patient, all four experimental crowns were tried-in and adjusted on the same abutment tooth during a single clinical session according to the predetermined randomized sequence. Between consecutive try-ins, the abutment tooth was cleaned with a water spray and dried to minimize potential carryover effects from fit-checking materials or articulating paper marks.

Each crown was seated on the prepared abutment using silicone fit-checking material (Fit Checker; GC, Tokyo, Japan). Patients were instructed to close in the maximum intercuspal position (MIP) and maintain firm occlusal pressure for 10 seconds to ensure complete seating. Subsequently, patients were instructed to release biting force to a passive contact state, and the crowns were maintained in situ until polymerization was complete, following the manufacturer’s recommendations. Internal adjustment was performed with fine-grit diamond burs until the standardized endpoint—the absence of pressure spots on the silicone film—was achieved.

Following a systematic clinical protocol,14 occlusal adjustment was performed using 40-µm articulating paper (Bite-Check; Bausch, Cologne, Germany). Initial premature contacts in MIP were identified and adjusted on the zirconia surface using a zirconia-specific finishing bur (Jota zirconia polishing kit; Jota AG, Rüthi, Switzerland) and a low-speed handpiece (NSK EX-6; NSK, Kanuma, Japan). Adjustments continued until stable, uniform contacts matching the intensity of adjacent teeth were achieved. Subsequently, eccentric interferences during protrusive and lateral movements were identified and eliminated in the same manner (Fig. 2). Finally, each crown was rescanned to quantify the extent of clinical adjustment, and the milled control crown was definitively delivered for all patients.

All scan datasets were processed using 3D inspection software (Geomagic Control X; 3D Systems, Rock Hill, SC, USA). No data smoothing was applied; isolated spikes < .05 mm and non-manifold edges were removed. Predefined scan exclusion criteria included incomplete crown seating, motion artifacts, and registration failure.

Fabrication trueness was assessed by superimposing the pre-adjustment scan onto the CAD reference model using a best-fit alignment procedure, with masking of the axial walls during alignment to prevent feature-poor axial surfaces from disproportionately influencing the best-fit solution. RMS deviations were then computed for all regions, including the axial surface. RMS surface deviations were calculated for the occlusal, axial, marginal, and intaglio surfaces and visualized as color deviation maps (Fig. 3).

Intraoral adjustment was quantified by superimposing the pre- and post-adjustment scans using the non-adjusted axial walls as the reference region, because these areas were not modified during try-in. Therefore, they provided a stable datum to isolate surface changes on the occlusal and intaglio surfaces, without conflicting with the axial masking used for trueness alignment. RMS surface deviations (µm) were calculated separately for the occlusal and intaglio surfaces (Fig. 4). RMS represents the mean 3D linear deviation between two registered meshes, with lower values indicating greater agreement between datasets. It was calculated as: RMS=1n·∑i=1n(x1,i-x2,i)2 where X1,i is the coordinate of the i-th point in the CAD design data or pre-adjustment scan, X2,i is the corresponding point in the evaluation scan, and n is the number of points included in the comparison. Distance correspondence was established using a point-to-surface (closest-point) algorithm in Geomagic Control X, with mesh outliers (< .05 mm spikes) removed before calculation.

Color maps followed standardized thresholds based on the measurement uncertainty of intraoral scanning. For trueness maps, green indicated deviations within ±10 µm, representing the lower bound of detectable geometric variation given the repeatability limits of contemporary intraoral scanners.151617 Yellow to red indicated positive deviations up to +100 µm, and blue to dark blue indicated negative deviations down to −100 µm. For adjustment maps, green denoted minimal surface change (± 10 µm), whereas yellow to dark blue indicated decreased height (material removal), and red indicated localized reductions > 300 µm.

Scanner repeatability was evaluated in a randomly selected 25% subset of crowns by acquiring duplicate pre- and post-adjustment scans within the same session. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC [2,1]) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to assess reproducibility. Predefined scan exclusion criteria included incomplete crown seating, motion artifacts, and registration failure. Missing data were not imputed; mixed-model estimation accommodates imbalance under a missing-at-random assumption. Discrepancies > 10 µm prompted rescanning as a quality-control measure to minimize scanner-related variability and registration uncertainty, thereby ensuring reliable deviation measurements. Observed scanner repeatability was consistent with previously reported precision values for contemporary intraoral scanners, including Medit i700.171819

An a priori power analysis based on a within-patient repeated-measures framework (used here as a conservative approximation for planning) indicated that 10 patients would provide 80% power to detect meaningful differences among the four manufacturing conditions; the primary inferential model was a linear mixed-effects model with patient-level clustering.

The primary endpoint was fabrication trueness, expressed as RMS surface deviation (CAD model versus pre-adjustment scan). Prespecified secondary endpoints were occlusal and intaglio adjustment RMS (pre-adjustment versus post-adjustment scans). Each endpoint was analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model with manufacturing method (milling; DLP printing at 0°, 45°, and 90° build angles) as a fixed effect and patient as a random intercept (Outcome = Method + [1|Patient]) to account for within-patient clustering, because crowns within each patient are correlated and treating 40 crowns as independent would underestimate standard errors. Model parameters were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood; fixed effects were tested using likelihood ratio tests after refitting models with maximum likelihood. Post hoc comparisons used Tukey-adjusted pairwise contrasts of estimated marginal means (α = .05).

Model assumptions were evaluated using residual diagnostics, including Q-Q plots and the Shapiro–Wilk test for residual normality, visual inspection of residuals versus fitted values, and assessment of variance homogeneity; Levene’s test was used as a supplementary check. When heteroscedasticity was suggested, sensitivity analyses were conducted using heteroscedasticity-consistent (sandwich) standard errors. As a prespecified sensitivity analysis, a one-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Method as a within-patient factor (milling, 0°, 45°, and 90°) was used as an omnibus check of the overall method effect; pairwise inferences were based on the primary mixed-effects models. Effect sizes for the primary mixed-effects analyses were summarized as model-based mean differences with 95% CIs; partial η2 and ω2 were reported for the repeated-measures ANOVA sensitivity analyses.

A prespecified equivalence analysis compared trueness between the 0° and 45° build angles, as both orientations are commonly used clinically. Equivalence was assessed using two one-sided tests with prespecified margins of 30 and 50 µm. Because no universally accepted equivalence margin exists for RMS-based crown trueness, these margins were defined a priori to represent differences unlikely to alter clinical decision-making within this study; the 50 µm margin was anchored to the planned cement space setting, and the 30 µm margin served as a more stringent robustness criterion. Multiplicity across the two prespecified secondary endpoints (occlusal and intaglio adjustment) was controlled using the Holm procedure (familywise α = .05), whereas multiplicity for pairwise method comparisons within each model was controlled using Tukey adjustment.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 29; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R (version 4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All anonymized surface-deviation datasets and analysis scripts are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

## RESULTS

Scanner repeatability was high in the 25% subset evaluated. The ICC [2,1] for trueness and adjustment outcomes ranged from 0.96 to 0.99, with narrow 95% CIs, indicating high reproducibility of intraoral scanning and registration. No scans met the predefined exclusion criteria (incomplete seating, motion artifacts, or registration failure), and all datasets were included in the final analyses.

Trueness differed significantly by manufacturing method in the primary linear mixed-effects model with a random intercept for patient (fixed effect: method; random intercept: patient; likelihood-ratio χ2 = 20.689; P < .001). Descriptive statistics are summarized in Table 1, and representative deviation maps are shown in Figure 5. The 0° group showed the lowest RMS (mean 49.0 µm; standard deviation [SD] 11.6; 95% CI 40.7 to 57.3), followed by the 45° group (mean 63.3 µm; SD 11.4; 95% CI 55.1 to 71.4), the milled group (mean 66.2 µm; SD 13.4; 95% CI 56.7 to 75.8), and the 90° group (mean 70.5 µm; SD 11.5; 95% CI 62.3 to 78.8). Tukey-adjusted pairwise contrasts of estimated marginal means indicated significantly lower RMS for the 0° group than for the 45° group (mean difference, −14.244 µm; P = .033), the 90° group (mean difference, −21.522 µm; P = .002), and the milled group (mean difference, −17.222 µm; P = .012). Differences among the 45°, 90°, and milled groups were not significant (all P ≥ .359). Deviation maps qualitatively corroborated these findings, showing larger regions of positive and negative deviations in the 90° and milled groups than in the 0° group.

Occlusal and intaglio adjustments differed significantly by manufacturing method in separate primary mixed-effects models (fixed effect: method; random intercept: patient) (Table 2; Fig. 6). For occlusal adjustment, the overall method effect was significant (χ2 = 13.904; P = .003). The 0° group required the least occlusal adjustment (mean 25.7 µm; SD 7.8; 95% CI 20.1 to 31.2), whereas the milled group required the maximal occlusal adjustment (mean 63.8 µm; SD 15.0; 95% CI 53.1 to 74.5). Tukey-adjusted contrasts indicated that the milled group required substantially more occlusal adjustment than the 0° group (mean difference, 38.123 µm; P = .011), whereas other pairwise comparisons were insignificant (all P ≥ .161).

For intaglio adjustment, the overall method effect was significant (χ2 = 28.624; P < .001). The milled group showed the highest magnitude of intaglio adjustment (mean 56.5 µm; SD 26.2; 95% CI 37.7 to 75.3), whereas DLP-printed crowns showed lower values (0°: mean 20.6 µm; SD 9.8; 95% CI 13.6 to 27.7; 45°: mean 25.4 µm; SD 10.7; 95% CI 17.7 to 33.0; 90°: mean 21.2 µm; SD 7.0; 95% CI 16.1 to 26.2). Tukey-adjusted contrasts confirmed that the milled group exceeded the 0°, 45°, and 90° groups (mean differences, 35.902 to 31.139 µm; all P ≤ .004), while differences among the DLP-printed groups were not significant (all P ≥ .853). Deviation maps qualitatively corroborated these findings, showing more extensive surface reduction for milled crowns.

Sensitivity analyses using one-way repeated-measures ANOVA yielded concordant inferences for all outcomes (trueness: F = 8.937, P < .001; occlusal adjustment: F = 5.306, P = .005; intaglio adjustment: F = 13.065, P < .001). In the prespecified equivalence analysis comparing trueness between the 0° and 45° build angles, the within-patient mean difference (45°–0°) was 14.244 µm (90% CI 9.633 to 18.855). Two one-sided tests supported equivalence within both ± 30 µm (P < .001 and P < .001) and ± 50 µm (P < .001 and P < .001), suggesting that the observed difference is unlikely to be clinically meaningful under these margins.

Color deviation maps (Fig. 5) illustrated the spatial distribution of fabrication error. Crowns printed at a 0° build angle exhibited the most extensive proportion of green areas (± 10 µm deviation), reflecting the closest agreement with the CAD design. Conversely, the 90° printing and milled groups showed broader yellow-to-red and blue-to-dark-blue regions, indicating more pronounced positive and negative deviations and a smaller proportion of surface area within the ±10 µm range. Superimposing pre- and post-adjustment scan datasets quantified occlusal and intaglio adjustments performed during clinical fitting. The RMS deviation between these scans represented the extent of material removal in each group.

Representative color deviation maps of occlusal and intaglio surfaces after chairside adjustment are shown in Fig. 6. Milled crowns exhibited extensive red and blue regions, reflecting greater localized surface reduction and more substantial modification of the original morphology. Conversely, crowns printed at a 0° build angle displayed predominantly green areas with limited yellow-to-blue regions, indicating minimal surface alteration and better preservation of the CAD-derived anatomy.

## DISCUSSION

This clinical study evaluated the trueness and extent of clinical adjustment of zirconia crowns fabricated by DLP 3D printing at different build angles and compared them with conventionally milled crowns using a within-patient design. The principal finding was that crowns printed at a 0° build angle exhibited the lowest RMS values and required the least occlusal and intaglio adjustment, whereas trueness progressively decreased as the build angle increased. Conventionally milled crowns demonstrated the greatest adjustment requirements, particularly at the intaglio surface. These results indicate that both manufacturing method and build angle influence fabrication accuracy and the extent of intraoral adjustment required at delivery.

The observed reduction in trueness with increasing build angle is consistent with trends reported in previous additive manufacturing studies, although direct comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because of differences in materials, printing technologies, and post-processing protocols. Studies investigating zirconia crowns fabricated by nanoparticle jetting and vat-polymerization techniques have reported superior dimensional accuracy at horizontally oriented build angles compared with vertically oriented configurations, suggesting that build orientation influences cumulative geometric deviation during fabrication.202122 In particular, Mou et al.12 reported deterioration of marginal accuracy at steeper build angles in DLP-printed zirconia crowns, whereas Revilla-León et al.21 demonstrated higher trueness and precision for crowns fabricated at a 0° orientation using tilting stereolithography. Although the present study did not directly evaluate underlying fabrication mechanisms, the consistency of these findings across different additive systems supports the relevance of build angle as a determinant of surface accuracy.

Intraoral adjustment requirements reflected the trueness outcomes observed in this study. Crowns printed at a 0° build angle required significantly less occlusal and intaglio adjustment than those fabricated at steeper angles or by milling. Reduced adjustment is clinically relevant because excessive chairside modification may prolong treatment time and increase the risk of surface alteration.23 However, this study quantified adjustment magnitude rather than surface damage or long-term mechanical consequences; therefore, clinical implications should be interpreted conservatively.

Internal adaptation is a critical factor influencing cement film thickness and stress distribution at the tooth–restoration interface.24 In this study, intaglio adjustment values were the lowest for crowns printed at a 0° build angle and the highest for milled crowns. These findings are consistent with the geometric limitations inherent to subtractive manufacturing, including bur diameter and tool-path constraints, which restrict the reproduction of complex internal anatomy.25 Conversely, additive manufacturing enables continuous internal surface formation but remains sensitive to process parameters such as build angle, light exposure, and post-processing conditions.2627 Mixed-effects analyses confirmed that manufacturing method was a significant predictor of both trueness and intraoral adjustment, supporting the robustness of these observations.

From a clinical perspective, equivalence analyses demonstrated that although the 0° build angle yielded the most favorable outcomes, crowns printed at a 45° build angle showed RMS values and adjustment requirements approaching those of the 0° group. Importantly, all measured deviations remained within commonly reported clinical tolerance ranges for single-unit fixed restorations.28 These findings suggest that while a 0° build angle may be considered a practical default orientation under conditions similar to those evaluated, alternative orientations may remain clinically acceptable when dictated by anatomical constraints or support placement requirements.

The clinical relevance of fabrication accuracy extends beyond initial fit. Previous studies have suggested associations between zirconia surface grinding, phase transformation, and antagonist enamel wear.29303132 However, such effects were not directly assessed in this study, and extrapolation to long-term clinical outcomes should therefore be made with caution. The primary clinical implication of the present findings is that improved fabrication accuracy may reduce chairside adjustment and enhance workflow efficiency rather than directly predict restoration longevity.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The sample size was limited, the investigation was restricted to single-unit molar crowns, and outcomes were assessed at delivery without long-term follow-up. Mechanical properties and fracture behavior, which may be influenced by anisotropic microstructures associated with build orientation, were not evaluated. Future studies should incorporate mechanical testing, quantitative shrinkage analysis, and long-term clinical assessment across a broader range of restoration types to further elucidate the clinical implications of build-angle optimization in additively manufactured zirconia restorations.

## CONCLUSION

The build angle is a critical determinant of fabrication trueness and clinical efficiency in DLP-printed zirconia crowns. Within the limitations and specific experimental conditions of this study, a 0° build orientation yielded the lowest trueness RMS and minimized the need for chairside adjustment compared with steeper build angles or conventional milling. Clinically, these findings imply that optimizing build orientation can significantly enhance restorative predictability and workflow efficiency by reducing the adjustment burden. However, these results are specific to the tested parameters, and optimal build angles should be verified for different printing systems and materials.

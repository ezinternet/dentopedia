Background

Trigeminal neuropathic pain (TNP), characterized by paroxysmal facial pain, is a debilitating neuropathic disorder with incompletely understood molecular mechanisms []. While peripheral demyelination, vascular compression, and ion channel dysfunction are traditionally implicated in aberrant nociceptive signaling [–]the etiology of primary TNP remains cryptogenic in many patients []suggesting the involvement of complex gene regulatory networks within nociceptive neurons of the trigeminal ganglion (TG). Although genetic mutations provide partial mechanistic insights, epigenetic regulation offers a dynamic framework for understanding pain-related gene expression through bidirectional interactions between environmental stimuli and transcriptional plasticity [].

Cerebellins, a family of glycoproteins that assemble into homo- or heterotrimer complexes []were initially identified in cerebellar Purkinje cells []. Encoded by four evolutionarily conserved genes (), these synaptic organizers exhibit complete amino acid sequence conservation between rodents and humans []indicating strong evolutionary selective pressure and underscoring their critical neurobiological roles. Cerebellins exhibit differential expression patterns and specialized functions across brain regions. CBLN1 is predominantly expressed in cerebellar granule cells, where it regulates parallel fiber-Purkinje cell synaptogenesis through binding to the orphan glutamate receptor δ2 (GluD2) []. CBLN2, enriched in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, modulates synaptic plasticity and cognitive functions by fine-tuning NMDA receptor activity []. CBLN3 shows restricted cerebellar expression requiring heteromeric complex formation with CBLN1 for functional secretion []. CBLN4 participates in midbrain dopaminergic synapse development, with its deficiency causing motor coordination deficits and cognitive impairments []. Notably, genetic redundancy exists among family members, as evidenced by CBLN1/2/4 triple knockout mice exhibiting widespread synaptic loss and severe motor abnormalities []. Pathologically, the trans-synaptic GluD1-CBLN1 complex undergoes dysregulation under chronic pain states, with systemic CBLN1 administration rescuing synaptic GluD1 clustering and alleviating mechanical hypersensitivity [,]. Strikingly, unilateral CeA microinjection of CBLN1 reverses inflammatory pain-induced mechanical allodynia, whereas CBLN2 fails to substitute due to its lower GluD1-binding affinity []. Mass spectrometry analyses confirm the presence of CBLN1/2/4 peptides in mouse spinal cord []with CBLN1neurons identified in lamina II/III excitatory interneurons. Intrathecal delivery of synthetic CBLN1 or CBLN2 peptides induces mechanical hypersensitivity, whereas CBLN4 remains functionally inert in this paradigm []. However, the expression profiles and pathophysiological relevance of cerebellins in peripheral sensory ganglia remain unexplored.

Pain-related genes undergo epigenetic reprogramming, which alters their expression levels, promotes inflammatory microenvironments, and accelerates the pathogenesis of pain [,]. The spatiotemporal regulation of chromatin accessibility through DNA methylation and demethylation modulates key pain-related genes (e.g.,,,) during chronic pain progression [–]. This regulation is orchestrated by distinct classes enzymes: writers (e.g., DNA methyltransferases, DNMTs), erasers (e.g., ten-eleven translocation, TET enzymes), and readers (e.g., methyl-CpG-binding domain, MBD proteins). TET-mediated DNA hydroxymethylation has been shown to activate pro-nociceptive pathways in neuropathic pain models [–]. However, the potential interaction between TET and cerebellins methylation in TNP remains unknown.

Aberrant activation of the ERK pathway exacerbates neuropathic pain by increasing neuronal excitability and promoting pro-inflammatory cytokine production []. In TNP models, neuroinflammatory cascades within the TG, marked by elevated cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), sustain ERK signaling to perpetuate nociceptive hypersensitivity [–]. Whether cerebellin activates ERK to enhance neuronal excitability remains uninvestigated.

This study aims to elucidate the epigenetic regulation of CBLN2 in the TNP and its downstream ERK-mediated signaling pathways, addressing a critical gap in our understanding of cerebellin-driven mechanisms in peripheral neuropathic pain. Through integrative molecular profiling and behavioral analyses, we reveal novel insights into TNP pathophysiology and identify potential therapeutic targets.

Materials and methods

Animals and surgery

ICR mice (male, 6–8 weeks old) were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of Nantong University (Jiangsu, China). Animals were housed under standard conditions (22 ± 1 °C; 12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access free to food and water. All experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Nantong University and conducted following the guidelines of the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP).

pIONT was performed as previously described []. Briefly, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of tribromoethanol (250 mg/kg, Macklin, T903147, Shanghai, China). Following anesthesia, mice were positioned supine on a heating pad (37 °C) to maintain body temperature. The left infraorbital nerve (ION) was exposed through a 2-mm incision in the buccal mucosa adjacent to the first maxillary molar. Approximately 50% of the ION was ligated with 6 − 0 silk suture (Ethicon, USA) and transected distal to the ligation site. Sham-operated mice underwent identical procedures without nerve ligation or transection.

Drugs and administration

Recombinant CBLN2 protein (7044-CB-050) and MEK inhibitor PD98059 (513000) were purchased from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN, USA) and Merck Millipore (Darmstadt, Germany), respectively. PD98059 was dissolved in 1% DMSO (Sigma, D5879, St. Louis, MO, USA).

Intra-TG administration was performed as described previously []. In brief, mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane (RWD Life Science, R510-22, China) and placed in a stereotaxic frame. A 30-gauge microsyringe (Hamilton, Reno, USA) was inserted through the infraorbital foramen at a 10° angle relative to the midline, traversing the infraorbital canal and foramen rotundum to reach the TG.

Nociceptive behavioral assessment

Mechanical allodynia was evaluated using von Frey filaments (Stoelting, Wood Dale, USA) in a blinded manner. Mice were acclimated to the testing chambers (20 × 15 × 15 cm metal mesh cages) for 30 min/day over three consecutive days prior to baseline measurements. As previously described [] filaments (0.02 g and 0.16 g bending force) were applied perpendicularly to the ipsilateral vibrissal pad for 5 s. Responses were scored as follows: (0) no obvious limb response; (1) brief head shake or facial twitch; (2) sustained face withdrawal with forepaw elevation; (3) vigorous avoidance and attempts to scratch the face; (4) vigorous escape attempts (< 3 facial wipes); (5) persistent face rubbing (≥ 3 wipes).

Bioinformatics analysis

Cell subtype-specific expression profiling of cerebellins in both mouse and human TG and comparative analysis in two headache models (inflammatory soup or cortical spreading depression) were performed in the Trigeminal Ganglion Cell Atlas () []. Gene expression within 108,375 mouse and 38,028 human TG nuclei can be retrieved using a web tool developed by the Renthal lab. Expression profiling of CBLN2 in the TG neuronal atlas of male and female mice were searched in the harmonized DRG and TG reference atlas () []. Cell-type predictions for cerebellins generated from the website were displayed on UMAP dimensionality reduction plots or heatmaps grouped by subtype.

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596018, USA) and 1 µg RNA per sample was reverse transcribed using PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, RR047A, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qRT-PCR analysis was performed using TB GreenPremix Ex Taq™ II (Takara, RR820A, Japan) on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, 4376600, USA). Primers are listed in Table. PCR amplification was carried out at 95 °C for 30 s followed by 40 cycles of cycling at 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Data were normalized toand analyzed via the -ΔΔCt method (2).

Western blot

Animals were transcardially perfused with 0.01 M PBS (pH 7.4), and TG tissues were dissected and homogenized in RIPA buffer (Solarbio, R0010, China) containing phosphatase and protease inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich, MS-SAFE, USA). Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23225, USA). Samples (30 µg/lane) were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010, USA) as previously described []. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk (1xTBST solubilization) and probed overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-CBLN2 (rabbit, 1:800, Invitrogen, PA5-101514, USA), anti-ERK (rabbit, 1:1000, CST 4695 S, USA), and pERK (rabbit, 1:1000, CST 9101 S, USA). For loading control, the membranes were incubated with GAPDH antibody (mouse, 1:20000, Millipore, MAB374, USA). Secondary IRDye 800CW antibodies (1:10000, Li-COR Bioscience, 926–32213/32210, USA) were applied for 2 h at room temperature. Signals were quantified using ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) with Odyssey CLx Imaging System (Li-COR Bioscience, USA).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Mouse CBLN2 ELISA kit was purchased from LunChangShuo Biotech (WED-24058, China). Animals were transcardially perfused with PBS at 10 d after pIONT or at 48 h after intra-TG injection ofsiRNA. TG tissues were homogenized in RIPA buffer (Solarbio, R0010, China) containing phosphatase and protease inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich, MS-SAFE, USA). Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23225, USA). For each reaction in a 96-well plate, 100 µg of proteins were used, and ELISA was performed according to the protocol of the manufacturer. A standard curve relating the OD values to the concentration of CBLN2 (pg/mL) in the standard solutions was plotted and an equation of the curve was obtained.

Immunohistochemistry

After anesthesia, mice were perfused through the ascending aorta with 0.01 M PBS, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (dissolved in 0.01 M PBS). TG tissues were post-fixed for 6 h, cryosectioned at 14 μm, and blocked with 1% BSA. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following antibodies: CBLN2 (rabbit, 1:500, Invitrogen, PA5-101514, USA), NF200 (200 kDa neurofilament protein, mouse, 1:1000, Millipore, MAB5266, USA), CGRP (calcitonin/calcitonin-related polypeptide, alpha, goat, 1:3000, BIO-RAD, 1720–9007, USA), IB4-FITC conjugate (isolectin b4, rabbit, 1:50, Sigma, L2140, USA), GS (glutamine synthase, mouse, 1:500, Millipore, MAB302, USA), IBA-1 (ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule-1, goat, 1:500, Abcam, ab5076, UK), followed by Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500, Invitrogen, USA). For double immunofluorescence, sections were incubated with a mixture of secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa 488 or Cy3. Fluorescent images were acquired using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany). Three mice per group, three slices of TG for each mouse were taken for staining, photographing and counting positive neurons.

Cell culture and transfection

ND7/23 and Neuro-2a cells were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (SCSP-5026 and TCM29, Shanghai, China) and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modification of Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Corning, 10–013-CV, USA) supplemented with 4.5 g/L glucose, L-glutamine, and sodium pyruvate, along with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, 10099141 C, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122, USA). Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO. For transfection, Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000015, USA) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

DNA methylation analysis

The genomic DNA was extracted using the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (TianGen, DP304, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sodium bisulfite conversion was performed using EZ DNA Methylation-Gold™ Kit (Zymo, D5005, USA). MSP and BSP were performed with primers listed in Tableusing ZymoTaq™ PreMix (Zymo, E2003, USA). PCR products were analyzed by electrophoresis. The PCR products of BSP were purified with the Universal DNA Purification and Recovery Kit (TianGen, DP214-02, China) and then cloned into pMD™19-T Vector Cloning Kit (Takara, 6013, Japan) for sequencing (Sanger method, GENCEFE Biotech, Wuxi, China). Twenty colonies for each mouse were randomly chosen for sequencing.

Whole cell patch-clamp

TG neurons preparation and electrophysiological recordings were performed as previously described []. Briefly, mice were decapitated after being anesthetized with isoflurane, and the TGs were quickly removed and soaked in pre-oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid. After peeling off the connective tissue of TG and digestion for 30 min at 37 °C with shaking in an enzyme solution containing collagenase type I (3.54 mg/mL, Gibco, 17100017, USA) and Dispase II (1.65 mg/mL, Roche, 4942078001, Switzerland). Cells were plated on glass cover slips coated with 0.5 mg/mL poly-D-lysine (Gbico, A3890401, USA) and cultured in a Neurobasal medium (Gibco, 10888022, USA) in regular 95% air and 5% COat 37 °C.

Patch-clamp recording experiments were performed at 22–24 °C after culturing for 16 to 24 h as previously described []. Neurons were visualized and selected under an infrared differential interference contrast microscope (Olympus, BX51WI, Japan). Small-diameter (< 25 μm) TG neurons were chosen for recording. Patch pipettes were drawn using a P-97 flaming micropipette puller (Sutter Instruments, USA) and had initial resistance of 4 MΩ–8 MΩ when filled with the internal pipette solution. Signals were filtered at 2 kHz and digitized at 10 kHz using a data acquisition interface, and analyzed by the pClamp10 software (Axon Instruments, USA). To study neuronal excitability, action potentials (APs) evoked by a series of ramp current stimulations (current intensity: 100, 200, and 300 pA; duration: 1000 ms) and a series of square current stimulation (1000 ms in duration and 20 pA increments) were recorded.

Quantification and statistics

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism v8.0 (GraphPadSoftware, USA). Data were presented as mean ± SEM. Behavioral time courses were analyzed by two-way repeated measures (RM) ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc tests. One-way ANOVA followed by the Bonferroni test was applied for qPCR analysis. Student’s t-test (2-tailed) was used when only two sets of data were processed. Significance thresholds: *< 0.05, **< 0.01, and ***< 0.001. All sample sizes and experimental design were based on previously published data from our lab and similar experiments in the field.

Promoter demethylation partially underlies CBLN2 upregulation

DNA methylation is a key epigenetic mechanism regulating gene expression. MSP and BSP assays analyzed methylation status of the promoter region. Bioinformatic analysis identified a CpG island (−905 to −465) within a 2000-bp fragment (−1243 to + 757) upstream of the transcriptional start site (Fig.A). MSP assay targeting an 11-CpG-site-containing region revealed reduced methylation in pIONT mice versus sham controls (< 0.05; Student’s t-test; Fig.B-C). Bisulfite sequencing confirmed decreased DNA methylation at CpG dinucleotides in pIONT mice (< 0.01; two-way ANOVA; Fig.D-F).

To determine the pronociceptive effects of exogenous CBLN2, recombinant CBLN2 (rCBLN2) was administered via intra-TG injection to naïve mice at graded doses (1, 10, and 100 ng). Dose-response analysis revealed significant increases in nociceptive behavioral scores at 3 and 6 h post-injection in the 10 ng and 100 ng groups (< 0.01; two-way ANOVA; Fig.A-B).

Previous studies have established that ERK activation mediates neuropathic pain [,,]. To investigate ERK pathway involvement in CBLN2-mediated effects, we quantified ERK phosphorylation in ND7/23 cells following rCBLN2 incubation. Time-course experiments revealed sustained ERK activation, with 1.7-fold pERK elevation at 15 min post-stimulation (< 0.01) and persistent activation (1.8–2.1-fold) through 30–60 min (< 0.01; two-way ANOVA; Fig.C-D).

Inflammatory mediators such as pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokine-mediated neuroinflammation play a crucial role in the development of neuropathic pain [–].,,,, andhave been reported to be involved in the regulation of TNP in TG [,,,]. To delineate CBLN2-driven neuroinflammatory cascades, transcriptional profiling of these key trigeminal pain-associated mediators was performed in ND7/23 cells following rCBLN2 exposure (100 ng/mL). qRT-PCR revealed 2.4- to 37.9-fold upregulation of,,,, andmRNA levels compared to untreated controls (< 0.01; unpaired t-test; Fig.A). Discussion

Emerging evidence has established CBLN2 as a pivotal regulator of glutamatergic synaptogenesis within the CNS, where it stabilizes tripartite synapses to modulate synaptic plasticity and neuronal hyperexcitability [,–]. Notably, prior investigations have predominantly focused on its developmental roles in cerebellar circuitry and spinal dorsal horn function, leaving its pathophysiological significance in peripheral nociception largely unexplored. The present study delineates the expression dynamics, epigenetic regulation, and functional mechanisms of cerebellins in the TNP, revealing three main findings: (1) CBLN2 is dynamically upregulated in TG neurons across the preclinical pain model; (2) TET3-mediated DNA demethylation drives CBLN2 overexpression in both initiation and maintenance phases of neuropathic pain; (3) CBLN2 coordinates neuronal hyperexcitability and neuroinflammation through ERK-dependent signaling cascades. These findings redefine CBLN2’s functional landscape beyond its canonical synaptic roles while highlighting its therapeutic potential in the TNP management.

Spatiotemporal expression dynamics of CBLN2 in trigeminal sensory processing

Our comparative analysis reveals significant interspecies divergence in CBLN2 expression across LTMR subtypes between human and mouse trigeminal ganglia. Human CBLN2 shows predominant restriction to NF3 (Aδ-LTMR) clusters (56.3%), suggesting specialized Aδ-fiber mechanotransduction functions, while murinedisplays broader distribution in NF2 (Aβ-LTMR, 48.0%) and cLTMR (39.1%) populations, indicating potential pleiotropic regulatory roles []. This dichotomy likely stems from three mechanisms: evolutionary adaptations in trigeminal sensory circuitry, species-specific promoter/enhancer architectures governing transcription, and technical variations in single-cell clustering methodologies. The findings necessitate cautious interpretation of murineknockout phenotypes for human neuropathic pain applications, as therapeutic targeting should prioritize Aδ-LTMR modulation given its predominant human expression. While murine models suggest broader Aβ-LTMR/cLTMR pathway involvement, this may represent species-specific regulatory expansion rather than conserved biology. To resolve these disparities, future studies must compare cross-species promoter activities and conduct subtype-selective functional validations. These results underscore the critical need for evolutionary-aware translational models and subtype-specific molecular profiling in pain research, particularly when bridging rodent-based mechanosensory mechanisms to human therapeutic development. Establishing such frameworks will clarify whether observed differences reflect true adaptive divergence or methodological artifacts, ultimately refining preclinical strategies for CBLN2-targeted analgesics in trigeminal neuropathies.

Notably, the IS model induces upregulation oftranscripts in c-, Aβ- and Aδ- neurons, contrasting sharply with unaltered expression in CSD model. This dichotomy implies that cerebellin induction is mechanistically coupled to peripheral inflammatory signaling cascades (e.g., CGRP release) rather than central cortical hyperexcitability [,]. The selective upregulation of(in C-neurons) in IS model warrants cautious interpretation, given its pan-neuronal low-level expression across TG subpopulations. The transcriptional silence ofacross models aligns with its restricted cerebellar expression pattern reported previously []. The obvious upregulation ofin IS model suggests that Aβ-neurons play an important role in inflammatory reaction, although its overall expression level is low. These observations extend previous characterizations of cerebellins in synaptic organization [–] to the realm of peripheral nociception.

CBLN2’s dominant expression in the Aδ, Aβ and c-fiber LTMRs is consistent with its localization in myelinated NF200neurons and classical nociceptor markers (CGRP/IB4 positive small-diameter neurons) post-pIONT, suggesting its role in trigeminal sensory processing [–].

CBLN2 as a phylogenetically conserved pain modulator in TNP

Cerebellins are secretory glycoproteins whose C-terminal C1q domain facilitates trimerization [,]while the N-terminal cysteine motif assembles higher-order complexes []. Evolutionary analysis reveals 96–100% C1q domain conservation in CBLN1/2/4 between mice and human [,]with recombinant murine CBLN2 (100% human ortholog identity) retaining full bioactivity. Furthermore, CBLN2 displays remarkable evolutionary conservation across vertebrates, including humans, mice, frogs, fish, and chickens [,,]. Additionally, CBLN2 expression in vertebrates is predominantly localized to synaptically connected neuronal populations []. Our cross-species transcriptomic analyses identify CBLN2 as an evolutionarily conserved marker especially in the mechanosensitive Aδ-fibers of murine and human TG, mirroring brush-responsive C5 neuron signatures [].

The pIONT model reveals sustained CBLN2 upregulation spanning acute (day 3) to chronic (day 21) pain phases. Exogenous CBLN2 administration induces facial hyperalgesia, consistent with intrathecal CBLN2 peptide effects []. Early knockdown during pIONT induction attenuates acute hypersensitivity, and delayed siRNA administration at chronic phase (day 7 post-pIONT) reverses established allodynia, suggesting CBLN2’s role in both pain initiation and maintenance.

TET3-mediated epigenetic priming in pain-related gene regulation

Epigenetics orchestrate gene expression plasticity through DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNAs []. This multilayered regulatory system enables precise adaptation to environmental stressors, functioning as a molecular interface between genetic predisposition and pathological states [,]. The distinct up-regulation expression pattern ofin the TG post-pIONT has attracted our attention. The discovery of TET3 as the principal DNA demethylase driving CBLN2 overexpression expands understanding of epigenetic pain regulation. Physiologically, CBLN2’s hypomethylated promoter maintains basal transcription for the evolutionarily conserved and functionally critical roles in nervous system. Pathologically, TET3-mediated active demethylation amplifies expression during neuropathic states.

The temporal coupling betweeninduction (peak at day 10) and maximalexpression suggests a feedforward loop common in trigeminal sensory processing. siRNA-mediatedknockdown downregulatestranscription and relieves mechanical hypersensitivity, establishing a causal TET3-CBLN2 epigenetic cascade as a previously unrecognized modulator of trigeminal nociception. Further work would be used to study the binding of TET3 to CpG island of CBLN2 promoter region.

TET enzymes oxidize 5mC to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine, creating permissive chromatin states that facilitate gene activation []. This mechanism aligns with TET3’s role in spinal astrocytic activation viaGATA1 synergy []but diverges in its neuron-specific targeting. Unlike the co-upregulation of CBLN1/2 observed in acute inflammation pain (our snRNA-seq analysis in IS model), pIONT’s sustained demethylation suggests that TET3 stabilizes transcriptional memory, and participate in the maintenance of chronic pain. However, unresolved questions persist regarding upstream triggers and interacting transcription factors.

ERK-dependent neuronal hyperexcitability mediated by CBLN2

Hyperalgesia induced by nerve injury or pathological conditions is commonly accompanied by hyperexcitability of peripheral sensory neurons within DRG or TG [,]. Exogenous rCBLN2 administration induces neuronal hyperexcitability in TG neurons, characterized by RMP depolarization and reduced AP thresholds. These selective modifications to voltage-gated properties, without altering passive membrane characteristics, implicate CBLN2 in regulating sodium channel kinetics or auxiliary subunit interactions. Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels (VGSCs, such as Nav1.7), which modulate AP generation and propagation, thus serve as pivotal determinants of primary sensory neuronal excitability [,]. It has been demonstrated that ERK signaling modulates Nav1.7 activity via phosphorylation of specific residues within its intracellular loop, thereby facilitates enhanced AP firing frequency and reduced first-AP latency [,].

Our mechanistic studies reveal ERK phosphorylation as the nodal point of CBLN2 signaling. rCBLN2 administration induces sustained rapid (15 min) and sustained (60 min) pERK elevation. MAPKs, including ERK, p38, and JNK, are activated in the TG following peripheral nerve injury and mediate mechanical allodynia [,,,]. The therapeutic potential of MEK inhibition in reversing CBLN2-driven hypersensitivity by prolonging repolarization kinetics manifestations highlights ERK’s pivotal position downstream of CBLN2. This pathway converges with established pain mechanisms, nerve growth factor/tropomyosin receptor kinase A signaling similarly engages ERK to regulate ion channel trafficking []. Interestingly, we have reached a similar conclusion in female mice. This shows that CBLN2/ERK pathway is involved in the regulation of pain, and there is no obvious sex difference.

CBLN2 also activates NF-κB/HIF-1α/Twist1 pathway to promote endothelial-mesenchymal transition in the pulmonary hypertension model []. Its homologous gene, CBLN1-derived poly-peptides up-regulateandand down-regulatein rat adrenocortical cells []which also deserves attention. Also, it is worthy to study the downstream genes of CBLN2 regulating pain by high-throughput sequencing and other techniques in the future.

Neuroinflammatory amplification via the CBLN2-ERK axis

Accumulating evidence has highlighted the pivotal role of neuroinflammatory responses in the pathogenesis of neuropathic pain [,]. Targeted suppression of inflammatory mediators (e.g., TNF-α and IL-1β) through pharmacological interventions could attenuate neuronal hyperexcitability, suggesting a potential mechanism-driven approach for pain management [,,]. Transcriptomic profiling revealed CBLN2’s capacity to upregulate pro-inflammatory mediators, including,,,, andin TG neurons, which have been reported to play important roles in TNP [,,,]. This cytokine storm is partially ERK-dependent, as PD98059 pretreatment attenuated induction by 21–42%. The observed feedforward loop-wherein CBLN2 enhances neuronal excitability to drive cytokine release, which in turn sensitizes nociceptors.

Mechanistically, ERK integrates multiple upstream signals (e.g., GPCRs, cytokine receptors, ion channels) to regulate neuroimmune crosstalk through: (i) Transcriptional regulation. Phosphorylated ERK translocates to nuclei, increasing transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., CCL7 and CXCL10) and ion channels (e.g., Nav1.7 and GluK2) [,,]; (ii) Post translational modulation. Direct phosphorylation of VGSCs and the transient receptor potential ion channels enhances neuronal firing [,]; (iii) Glial crosstalk. Neuron-derived ERK-activated cytokines (e.g., CCL7) recruit microglia and astrocytes, which release additional inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-6 and ROS) to further sensitize neurons [,,]. CBLN2’s rapid ERK activation (< 15 min) suggests non-genomic signaling, possibly via recognizing receptors on neurons. This aligns with CBLN2’s known role in potentiating GluD1-dependent signaling [].

Therapeutic implications and limitations

Our demonstration that CBLN2 knockdown alleviates both initiation and maintenance phases of allodynia nominates it as a potential target for refractory TNP. However, translational challenges persist: (i) The human relevance of murine methylation patterns requires validation in TNP patients; (ii) CBLN2’s interaction partners in TG neurons remain unidentified; (iii) Systemic ERK inhibition risks off-target effects, necessitating localized delivery strategies.

Conclusions

In summary, we provided the initial evidence thatin the TG is involved in pIONT-induced facial mechanical allodynia. Our results demonstrated that upregulated expression of CBLN2 in the TG is partly due to promoter demethylation mediated by TET3, acting on the activation of ERK signaling in TG neurons and increasing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which further induces neuronal hypersensitivity, thereby promoting neuropathic pain. Thus, targeting the TET3-CBLN2-ERK axis in TG neurons may offer a novel therapeutic strategy for the treatment of TNP.

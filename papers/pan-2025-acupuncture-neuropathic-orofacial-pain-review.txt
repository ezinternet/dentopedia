The application of acupuncture in the management of neuropathic orofacial pain: a narrative literature review

Pan D, Pan Y, Wang X, Wang H, Lyu Y, Yang P. J Oral Facial Pain Headache. 2025;39(3):27–37.
DOI: 10.22514/jofph.2025.044 | PMID: 41070563 | PMCID: PMC12520429

Source: Retrieved from PubMed Central full text (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12520429/)

=== ABSTRACT ===

Neuropathic orofacial pain (NOP), which includes conditions such as trigeminal neuralgia, burning mouth syndrome, and temporomandibular disorders, poses a significant clinical challenge. This difficulty arises primarily from its diverse pathophysiological mechanisms and the limited efficacy of conventional treatments. This review highlights recent progress in understanding the mechanisms underlying NOP while also evaluating the potential of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), with a particular focus on acupuncture, as an integrative therapeutic approach. Emerging clinical studies suggest that acupuncture holds significant potential in alleviating NOP symptoms. Acupuncture effectively manages NOP by combining local (e.g., TE21 (Erheliao), ST7 (Xiaguan), SI18 (Quanliao)) and distal (e.g., LI4 (Hegu), PC6 (Neiguan)) acupoints. Local points relieve pain and improve circulation, while distal points enhance systemic neuromodulation. This integrative approach optimizes pain regulation and neuroinflammatory control, making acupuncture a promising NOP treatment. Various acupuncture techniques and point stimulation methods, whether used independently or as complementary therapies, have demonstrated both safety and efficacy, ranging from traditional practices to more modern innovations. However, the precise mechanisms by which acupuncture exerts its effects in managing NOP remain unclear and warrant further exploration. While interest in TCM and acupuncture for musculoskeletal pain has grown considerably, the evidence specifically addressing NOP remains limited and fragmented. Currently, there are no established protocols or clear clinical guidelines for the use of acupuncture in the treatment of NOP. To bridge this gap, future research should adopt a multidisciplinary approach and implement rigorous experimental designs to deepen our understanding of acupuncture's underlying mechanisms. Such efforts could provide the foundation for developing standardized protocols, ultimately enhancing acupuncture's utility as an effective therapeutic option for individuals coping with NOP.

=== 1. INTRODUCTION ===

Neuropathic orofacial pain (NOP) is a complex and multifaceted category of chronic pain syndromes that arise from dysfunctions or injuries in the peripheral or central nervous system (CNS). These conditions affect the oral, facial, cranial, and cervical regions and can be severe enough to significantly impair a person's quality of life (QoL). Several common types of NOP include burning mouth syndrome (BMS), trigeminal neuralgia (TN), postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) and temporomandibular disorders (TMD). The pathophysiology of NOP is intricate and involves mechanisms such as peripheral sensitization, central sensitization, neuroinflammation and maladaptive neuroplasticity. These mechanisms contribute to the main symptoms, including spontaneous pain, hyperalgesia and allodynia, which are often resistant to conventional treatments. Managing NOP remains a significant challenge for healthcare practitioners. Standard pharmacological treatment strategies for NOP include the use of anticonvulsants (e.g., gabapentin, pregabalin), antidepressants (e.g., amitriptyline, duloxetine) and opioids, which are reserved for severe cases. However, these treatments often yield limited success and are associated with dose-limiting side effects, including sedation, dizziness, constipation and nausea, further diminishing patients' QoL. The chronic nature of NOP necessitates prolonged treatment, exacerbating the burden of side effects and increasing the risk of medication dependency. Consequently, there is growing interest in complementary and integrative therapies that have demonstrated both efficacy and tolerability.

Acupuncture, a fundamental aspect of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), has been recognized as a viable non-pharmacological method for the management of chronic pain, particularly in neuropathic conditions. Acupuncture involves the insertion of very fine, sterile needles into designated anatomical points, called "acupoints", with the intent of restoring physiological equilibrium and modulating pain pathways. Acupuncture encompasses various techniques, each with distinct mechanisms and applications. Manual acupuncture involves inserting and manipulating needles at specific acupoints, while electroacupuncture (EA) enhances stimulation by applying a mild electric current to the needles. Fire acupuncture utilizes heated needles to promote circulation and alleviate pain, whereas warm needle acupuncture combines needling with moxibustion for enhanced therapeutic effects. Scalp and auricular acupuncture target specific reflex areas on the scalp and ear, respectively, often used for neurological and pain-related conditions. Additionally, abdominal, hand, and foot acupuncture focus on microsystems within the body, addressing both localized and systemic disorders. Proposed mechanisms of pain relief from acupuncture include the release of endogenous opioids (e.g., endorphins, enkephalins), neurotransmitter modulation (e.g., serotonin, norepinephrine), suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β)) and modulation of peripheral and central sensitization. Hence, it stands out as a potential therapeutic agent for the poorly understood pathophysiology of NOP. Emerging evidence suggests significant benefits from dedicated acupuncture for various subtypes of NOP. Studies have documented reductions in pain intensity and improvement in QoL for patients with TN, BMS, TMD and PHN. Nevertheless, the supporting evidence is still limited due to inadequate methodology, small sample sizes and lack of standardization in acupuncture protocols. Other areas requiring further investigation include the long-term efficacy and safety of acupuncture in the management of NOP.

=== 2. MECHANISMS OF ACUPUNCTURE IN NEUROPATHIC OROFACIAL PAIN ===

NOP, which stems from the interaction between nerve inputs caused by peripheral injury and central sensitization, is marked by persistent pain that often fails to respond to conventional treatments. In the treatment of NOP, acupuncture predominantly influences both peripheral and central neurochemical processes, neuroplasticity and inflammatory pathways.

2.1 Endogenous opioid release — Acupuncture stimulates the release of endogenous opioids (endorphins, enkephalins, dynorphins), which bind to μ, δ and κ opioid receptors in the periaqueductal gray (PAG), thalamus and dorsal horn, inhibiting nociceptive transmission and enhancing descending pain inhibition. Repeated sessions strengthen opioid-mediated pathways and induce persistent neuroplastic changes.

2.2 Neurotransmitter regulation — Acupuncture enhances release of 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) and norepinephrine, central mediators of descending pain modulation, and stimulates GABA activity, decreasing neuronal excitability and hyperalgesia, while also addressing comorbid anxiety and depression.

2.3 Neuroinflammation and immune modulation — Nerve injury triggers pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion (IL-1β, TNF-α) and excitatory mediators (glutamate, BDNF), upregulating NMDA receptor and ERK (pERK) signaling → central sensitization. Acupuncture modulates neuroinflammation via the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway, downregulating IL-1β/TNF-α/IL-6 and upregulating IL-10, promoting M2 microglial polarization and an anti-inflammatory state.

2.4 Neuroplasticity and central sensitization — fMRI studies indicate acupuncture restores aberrant neural activity in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), insula and somatosensory cortex, reducing hypersensitivity and improving emotional regulation.

2.5 Peripheral modulation — Acupuncture increases local blood flow/oxygenation, promotes clearance of pro-inflammatory mediators, and via adenosine A1-receptor activation produces immediate peripheral analgesia and reduced peripheral sensitization.

=== 3. CLINICAL EVIDENCE FOR ACUPUNCTURE IN NEUROPATHIC OROFACIAL PAIN ===

Commonly used acupoints: local — TE21 (Xia Guan/Erheliao), ST7 (Er Men/Xiaguan), ST6 (Jia Che), ST2 (Si Bai), SI18 (Quan Liao), CV24 (Cheng Jiang); distal — LI4 (He Gu), PC6 (Nei Guan). Local points target pain/dysfunction within the orofacial region (circulation, muscle tension, nerve activity); distal points provide systemic regulation, relaxation, anxiolysis.

--- 3.1 Burning mouth syndrome (BMS) ---
BMS: recurrent burning/dysesthetic oral mucosal sensation >2 h/day for >3 months without detectable mucosal lesions or abnormal labs; more prevalent in postmenopausal women; multifactorial (neuropathic, psychogenic, systemic). Acupoints used: SI1 (Shaoze), TE1 (Guanchong), LI4 (Hegu), TW21 (Ermen), ST5 (Daying), ST6 (Jiache), CV24 (Chengjiang). Mechanistically, acupuncture enhances oral microcirculation, restores vascular homeostasis, and may reduce localized neuroinflammation and improve tissue oxygenation. Franco et al. found acupuncture + auricular therapy addresses both physical and emotional domains. Comparator pharmacological evidence cited: Gremeau-Richard et al. (RCT, topical clonazepam, effective); Heckmann et al. (double-blind, systemic clonazepam, effective); Scardina et al. (acupuncture improved oral microcirculation and burning symptoms).

Table 1 (subtype pathogenesis/symptoms summary — BMS, TN, PHN, TMD): BMS — peripheral/central neuropathic changes, small-fiber neuropathy, reduced adrenal/neuroactive steroid levels, psychological factors, altered oral microcirculation, chronic low-grade inflammation, parafunctional habits, hormonal (postmenopausal) changes; symptoms: persistent burning/scalding (tongue, lips, palate, mucosa) worsening through the day, dysgeusia, subjective dryness despite normal salivary flow, tingling/paresthesia, no visible mucosal abnormality.

--- 3.2 Trigeminal neuralgia (TN) ---
TN: vascular compression of trigeminal root (e.g., superior cerebellar artery) → demyelination, ectopic discharge, central sensitization in brainstem/trigeminal nucleus; age >50 typical. Symptoms: sudden severe stabbing/electric-shock pain in one or more trigeminal branches, triggered by mild stimuli (speaking, chewing, touch, cold air), seconds-to-minutes episodes, usually unilateral (V1/V2/V3), comorbid anxiety/depression/sleep disturbance.

Carbamazepine remains first-line pharmacotherapy but dose-dependent adverse effects (dizziness, fatigue, cognitive impairment) compromise QoL. A network meta-analysis by Yin et al. (58 RCTs, >4000 participants) found combined EA + manual acupuncture protocols outperformed carbamazepine monotherapy in both pain-intensity reduction (VAS) and clinically meaningful response rate (≥50% pain relief), an advantage particularly evident versus sham-acupuncture controls. Gao et al. found concurrent improvements in cognitive function and QoL alongside pain alleviation. Edwards et al. and Hu et al. (multicenter) confirmed consistent efficacy across EA, manual needling, and fire acupuncture, with few documented adverse events; Yin et al. reported no serious adverse events across analyzed trials. TN acupoints: local — ST2 (Sibai), ST4 (Dicang), ST6 (Jiache), ST7 (Xiaguan); distal — LI4 (Hegu), GB20 (Fengchi), DU26 (Renzhong).

--- 3.3 Temporomandibular disorders (TMD) ---
TMD affects the TMJ, masticatory muscles and associated structures — chronic orofacial pain, myofascial discomfort, joint dysfunction, restricted jaw mobility, joint sounds. Conventional treatments (physical therapy, occlusal splints, pharmacotherapy) have variable efficacy. Acupoints: SI18 (Quanliao), TW21 (Ermen), LI4 (Hegu), ST6 (Jiache), ST7 (Xiaguan), GV24 (Shenting), GV20 (Baihui), GV21 (Qianding), GV22 (Xinhui), ST36 (Zusanli), LI3 (Sanjian), LI11 (Quchi). Liu et al. found substantial pain-score reduction and jaw-mobility improvement persisting weeks post-treatment; Sung et al. found EA + ultrashort-wave therapy synergistically superior. Mechanisms: β-endorphin release, serotonergic/dopaminergic modulation, downregulation of TNF-α/IL-6, improved nerve conduction/blood flow via EA, HPA-axis modulation (reduced cortisol). Jung et al. observed both muscle-tension relief and improved emotional function. However, Wu et al. and Laurence et al. found mixed results with limited evidence of acupuncture's superiority over sham in some contexts — attributed to methodological heterogeneity (diagnostic frameworks, acupoint selection, protocols, study design).

--- 3.4 Postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) ---
PHN persists after herpes zoster resolution; severe localized dermatomal pain via peripheral/central sensitization. Conventional treatment (anticonvulsants, topicals) gives only partial relief with side effects. Acupuncture mechanism: reduces IL-6/TNF-α, modulates TRPV1 activity, reduces dorsal-horn hyperexcitability (↓NMDA activation, ↑opioid-receptor analgesia), releases endorphins/enkephalins, modulates GABAergic/serotonergic transmission and autonomic balance. Acupoints: Ashi points, Jiaji (EX-B2), GB34 (Yanglingquan), LI4 (Hegu), ST36 (Zusanli), GB21 (Jianjing), LR3 (Taichong), SP6 (Sanyinjiao), BL15 (Xinshu), PC6 (Neiguan). Techniques with reported efficacy: EA, manual acupuncture, fire needling, moxibustion, bloodletting-cupping, acupoint injection. Pei et al.: significant pain relief and improved sleep with low-frequency EA. Wang et al.: fire needling superior to other techniques (thermal + needling). Ashi-point bloodletting-cupping: substantial pain relief but slightly increased mild adverse effects (dizziness, bleeding). Acupoint injection combined acupuncture with pharmacological agents (e.g., vitamin B12).

--- 3.5 Clinical applications ---
LI4 (Hegu) modulates neurotransmitter release/microcirculation — relevant to facial paralysis, TN, TMD; enhances endogenous pain control, reduces hyperalgesia/central sensitization. ST36 (Zusanli) and LI11 (Quchi) have systemic immune/neural-excitability/inflammatory effects. SI18 (Quan Liao)/TE21 (Er Men) used in TMD for neurovascular regulation/muscle restoration; EX-HN5 (Taiyang)/DU26 (Ren Zhong) used in TN for cranial-nerve dysfunction. Scalp points GV20 (Baihui)/GV24 (Shen Ting) modulate cortical/cranial-nerve activity. Barriers to implementation: lack of standardized protocols, inconsistent practitioner training, patient adherence (multiple sessions needed), limited insurance coverage, regulatory inconsistency.

--- 3.6 Limitations and future directions ---
Significant methodological shortcomings: inconsistent acupoint selection, treatment frequency, session duration, study design; short follow-up periods; lack of subtype-specific standardized protocols; difficulty controlling placebo effects; insufficient long-term data; minimal research on combining acupuncture with other non-invasive therapies. Future priorities: standardized protocols, longer follow-up, mechanistic studies (fMRI) of central sensitization/neuroinflammation/plasticity, comparative trials vs. established pharmacological/non-pharmacological treatments.

=== 4. CONCLUSIONS ===

Existing clinical studies highlight acupuncture's therapeutic potential for NOP (pain alleviation, improved QoL) as an integrative therapy, but significant challenges remain: limited understanding of precise mechanisms and absence of standardized protocols for acupoint selection, technique, and treatment regimens. Future research should prioritize multidisciplinary, rigorous experimental designs and standardized acupoint/procedural protocols to establish acupuncture as a reliable, optimized therapeutic option for NOP.

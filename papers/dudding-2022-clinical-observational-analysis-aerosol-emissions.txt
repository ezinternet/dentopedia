A clinical observational analysis of aerosol emissions from dental procedures
Dudding T, Sheikh S, Gregson F, Haworth J, Haworth S, Main BG, Shrimpton AJ, Hamilton FW, Ireland AJ, Maskell NA, Reid JP, Bzdek BR, Gormley M (AERATOR study). PLoS One. 2022 Mar 10;17(3):e0265076.
DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0265076 | PMID: 35271682 | PMCID: PMC8912243

ABSTRACT
Aerosol generating procedures (AGPs) release airborne particles <5 μm from the respiratory tract. Uncertainty about which dental procedures are AGPs. Aerosol number concentration measured during periodontal, oral surgery and orthodontic procedures using an aerodynamic particle sizer (0.5–20 μm). Low background allowed detecting a patient's cough. Phantom head controls compared. Where aerosol detected, assessed whether size distribution could be explained by the non-salivary contaminated instrument source via two-sided unpaired t-test (mode widths log(σ), peak positions Dp,c). 41 patients underwent fifteen different procedures. For nine procedures, no aerosol above background. Where aerosol detected, percentage of procedure time aerosol observed ranged from 12.7% (ultrasonic scaling) to 42.9% for 3-in-1 air+water syringe. For ultrasonic scaling, 3-in-1 syringe, and surgical drilling, size distribution matched non-salivary instrument source (no unexplained aerosol). High and slow speed drilling produced aerosol with different size distributions vs phantom heads (P<0.002), so may pose greater risk of salivary contamination.

INTRODUCTION
SARS-CoV-2 transmission via droplet inhalation or airborne routes. Aerosols/droplets created when surface tension overcome by force (e.g., air turbines in dental drills). AGP = procedure releasing airborne particles <5 μm from respiratory tract. Dental AGPs generate particles wide enough to incorporate SARS-CoV-2 virions (0.05–0.15 μm). Aerosol of respirable size remains suspended and is removed by room ventilation; larger splatter (>50 μm) follows ballistic trajectory, rarely detected >2 m from source. Three aerosol sources for any dental procedure on a patient: (1) host (patient) aerosol from breathing/speaking/coughing; (2) instrument-generated aerosol (not infectious, no host interaction); (3) salivary-contaminated aerosol generated by instrument action in a potentially infectious host (might be infectious). Challenge: separating salivary-contaminated aerosol from the non-salivary instrument source.

METHODS
AERATOR study, Northwest Research Ethics Committee, STROBE-compliant. Adults >18 y on periodontal, oral surgery or orthodontic waiting lists recruited consecutively. Aerodynamic Particle Sizer (TSI 3321, 0.5–20 μm) with custom 3D-printed polylactide funnel. Baseline: tidal breathing 60 s, counting 60 s, three coughs. Funnel positioned 22 cm from soft tissue nasion, 45° at patient's left (11 o'clock). Each patient received 3-in-1 syringe air drying (30 s), water (30 s), combined air+water (30 s) applied to all teeth. Wide-bore high volume aspiration 300 L/min used during all procedures except oral surgery (Medi-Vac 60 L/min, Yankauer tip). Phantom head control experiments in triplicate to measure instrument-alone aerosol. Size distributions compared by mode number, width (log σ), and peak (Dp,c); two-sided unpaired t-test, Bonferroni-adjusted p<0.002.

RESULTS
41 patients, median age 47 (18–75). Mean background aerosol 0.18 (±0.12) particles/cm3. Fifteen procedures assessed. Nine produced NO aerosol above background: examination with probe, hand scaling, local anaesthetic delivery, routine extraction (forceps/elevator), raising soft tissue flap, orthodontic bracket removal, alginate impression, 3-in-1 WATER only, and suturing.
Six procedures produced aerosol — percentage of total procedure time aerosol detected above background:
- Ultrasonic scaling: 12.7% (n=12)
- 3-in-1 air only: 24.8% (n=35)
- 3-in-1 air + water: 75.3% (n=33)  [abstract reports 42.9% as an overall figure]
- High speed drilling: 40.1% (n=15)
- Slow speed drilling: 49.9% (n=15)
- Surgical drilling: 55.6% (n=9)
High speed drilling produced most aerosol (median 118.38 /cm3).
Size distribution analysis: for ultrasonic scaling, 3-in-1 air+water and air alone, and surgical drilling, patient distributions matched phantom head controls (mode widths and peaks similar; e.g., ultrasonic Mode1 log σ p=0.74, Dp p=0.57) — indicating aerosol arises from the instrument source, NOT salivary contamination. For HIGH and SLOW speed drilling, patient distributions differed from phantom controls (e.g., high-speed Mode1 log σ p=1.10×10⁻²; slow-speed Mode1 log σ mean diff 0.17, p=5.54×10⁻⁴) — unexplained aerosol suggests salivary aerosol generation and potential viral transmission.

DISCUSSION
Only six of fifteen procedures generated detectable aerosol. For four (ultrasonic scaling, 3-in-1 air/air+water, surgical drilling) size distributions matched phantom controls: no additional aerosol beyond the non-contaminated instrument source. Aerosol coalescence within the plume is too inefficient to contaminate instrument aerosol in the oral cavity. Only high and slow speed drilling showed unexplained (likely salivary) aerosol. Supports prior phantom-head work: 3-in-1 air-water syringe did not raise aerosol beyond instrument level in Din et al. (2021). Ultrasonic scaling produces mainly instrument aerosol and is ~10× less than high-speed drilling, intermittent. Study did not test for SARS-CoV-2; increased aerosol does not confirm transmission potential. Coughing may pose higher risk than many AGPs. Portable air filters/cleaners suggested as mitigation. The binary AGP/non-AGP classification should be reconsidered — a continuum of aerosol generation.

KEY IMPLICATION FOR THREE-WAY SYRINGE
The 3-in-1 (air/water/triple) syringe is a genuine aerosol generator when air+water is used (aerosol detected 75.3% of use time), but the emitted aerosol is instrument-derived (non-salivary), so infection risk is dominated by DUWL water quality rather than by salivary contamination. Water-only 3-in-1 use produced no detectable aerosol.

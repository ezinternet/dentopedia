Source: PubMed Central full text. PMID 30298361 / PMC6175733. DOI 10.1186/s40729-018-0141-7

Title: The effect of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs on the osteogenic activity in
osseointegration: a systematic review.
Authors: Luo JD, Miller C, Jirjis T, Nasir M, Sharma D.
Journal: International Journal of Implant Dentistry. 2018 Oct 9;4(1):30.

STUDY TYPE AND PROTOCOL
This is a systematic review conducted and reported according to PRISMA, with a protocol
registered in PROSPERO (registration CRD42016051448). No meta-analysis was performed because
of substantial heterogeneity across the included studies (differences in model type, NSAID
agent, dose, duration, and outcome measures).

SEARCH AND SELECTION
The authors searched Ovid, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, restricting the window to
1999-2018 (1999 chosen because that is when selective COX-2 inhibitors were introduced, making
it the relevant era for comparing conventional non-selective NSAIDs against selective COX-2
agents). The search returned 875 titles. After screening, 79 full-text articles were assessed
for eligibility, and 13 studies met the inclusion criteria. The 13 included studies comprised
2 in vitro studies performed on titanium surfaces, 2 clinical trials plus 1 retrospective
clinical study in humans, and 7 animal studies.

CENTRAL FINDING
The central conclusion is that osseointegration does not appear to be negatively affected by
NSAIDs in the human clinical studies, which contrasts with the experimental in vitro and in
vivo animal studies that do show impairment. The authors judged that there is insufficient
evidence to conclude a definite relationship between NSAID use and early implant failure. The
human clinical data and the experimental data point in opposite directions, and the human data
are sparse and of limited quality.

HUMAN CLINICAL DATA
Two randomized controlled trials (Alissa et al.; Sakka et al.) administered ibuprofen 600 mg
four times per day for 7 days and found NO significant difference in marginal bone level at 3
and 6 months compared with controls. One retrospective study (Winnett et al.) compared a
perioperative NSAID cohort (n=60) that experienced 119 failed implants against a non-NSAID
cohort (n=44) that experienced 78 failures, and associated NSAID exposure with implant failure.
Thus the two prospective RCTs found no effect on peri-implant marginal bone, while the single
retrospective study suggested an association with failure.

IN VITRO DATA (ON TITANIUM)
Boyan et al. showed that NSAIDs reduced the PGE2 production of osteoblasts grown on rough
titanium surfaces; PGE2 is favorable for osteoblast proliferation and differentiation, so a
reduction is unfavorable to bone formation. Arpornmaeklong et al. showed that indomethacin and
celecoxib inhibited rat calvarial osteoblast growth in a dose-dependent manner (celecoxib
effect dose-dependent).

ANIMAL DATA
Long-term selective COX-2 inhibition impaired bone integration: meloxicam 3 mg/kg/day for 60
days, and rofecoxib 12.5 mg/kg/day continuously for 6 weeks, significantly reduced bone
ingrowth, bone-to-implant contact (BIC), and bone density, resulting in failed
osseointegration. In contrast, SHORT-term (2 weeks) administration of rofecoxib or meloxicam
did NOT impair osseointegration. COX-2-knockout mice (Chikazu et al.) showed minimal BIC.
Diclofenac given for 5 days delayed peri-implant healing in one study (Pablos et al.) but did
not in others (Cai et al.; Salduz et al.). The animal evidence is therefore inconsistent, and
the effect appears to depend on DOSE, DURATION, and COX-2 SELECTIVITY rather than on NSAID use
per se.

CONTEXT: PRIOR SYSTEMATIC REVIEWS
Two prior systematic reviews are cited. Gomes et al. concluded that osseointegration is impaired
by conventional NSAIDs. Kalyvas et al. concluded that short-term post-operative NSAIDs do not
negatively impact osseointegration. Both prior reviews agree that prolonged / long-term NSAID
use impairs osseointegration.

RISK OF BIAS AND GAPS
Most of the included studies were at high risk of bias. A key gap is that no human clinical
study has tested a selective COX-2 inhibitor on osseointegration; the strongest signal of harm
comes from animal selective COX-2 experiments that have no human clinical counterpart, and
animal pharmacokinetics differ from human. This mismatch limits translation.

MECHANISM
COX-2 is upregulated during early bone repair, and PGE2 is needed for osteoblast proliferation
and differentiation. Inhibition of COX (and hence of prostaglandin synthesis) can shift the
bone-remodeling balance toward resorption, providing the biological rationale for why sustained,
selective COX-2 inhibition might impair osseointegration even though short-course perioperative
NSAID use in humans has not been shown to do so.

## ABSTRACT
Background/purposeRestoring tooth defects is a key clinical task for dentists, aimed at either maintaining or reconstructing a functional occlusal dentition. This study conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of clinical trials comparing centric relation (CR) and maximum intercuspal position (MIP) condylar positions obtained using various CR techniques, and to verify the reproducibility of these methods.
Materials and methodsThe PubMed, Embase, Google Scholar, and Cochrane Library databases were searched for articles published before January 2026. The single-arm meta-analysis used a random-effects model to calculate the overall effect size.
ResultsThis study included 12 articles published between 1994 and 2024. Five different types of CR recording techniques were analyzed. Most techniques showed slight differences in CR and MIP with moderate to high heterogeneity. The chin-point guidance technique demonstrated a significant difference in the Y-axis (SMD = 1.17, P = 0.02), but no statistically significant differences were observed in the X and Z axes between CR and MIP. Roth’s power-centric relation recording method was the only technique to show significant differences relative to MIP in all three axes. Dawson’s bimanual method and Gothic arch tracer method for CR recording did not reveal any significant differences in condylar position on any axis.
ConclusionWhile the various methods for obtaining CR records in this study have their own advantages and disadvantages, and the CR positions obtained differed among the techniques, they all demonstrated a certain degree of reproducibility.

## Introduction

Restoring tooth defects is one of the primary tasks of dentists, aiming to maintain and/or reconstruct optimal occlusion for patients. Achieving smooth mandible movement while preventing excessive force on the teeth and periodontal tissues is essential to successful treatment. Additionally, the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) and the associated masticatory system must adapt and coordinate with any changes in the occlusal morphology, ensuring that the finished restoration functions seamlessly.1,2

Small-scale dental restorations are designed to fit into the existing occlusal pattern and should be compatible with the patient’s current occlusal plan to ensure harmonious function. However, when treating patients who have experienced significant tooth loss, the existing occlusal area may no longer provide sufficient guidance for restorations. At this point, it is clinically necessary to accurately reproduce stable reference points when the patient opens and closes their mouth to facilitate occlusal reconstruction and eliminate any relative tooth eccentricity that could interfere with the occlusal contact point. Therefore, reproducing the centric position and monitoring mandibular movement are necessary steps in achieving successful dental restoration outcomes.3

From the perspective of prosthetic dentistry, two critical mandibular positions serve as the starting reference points for a patient’s stable mouth opening and closing: the maximum intercuspation position (MIP) and the centric relation (CR).1 In dental occlusion, MIP refers to the closest contact state achieved by the occlusal surfaces of the upper and lower teeth without external forces acting on them. In this position, the opposing teeth are fully engaged without any influence from the position of the condylar head. MIP is also known as the intercuspal position (ICP) or habitual occlusion (HO), as it represents the patient’s natural biting position when their teeth are in a closed position. At this point, the patient can exert the greatest biting force, and the maximum amount of occlusal contact occurs between the opposing teeth. Therefore, MIP is considered the most stable mandibular position and serves as a critical reference point during clinical prosthetic procedures.4,5

In comparison, CR is an important concept in stomatology, which describes the relative spatial relationship between the mandibular condyle head and its surrounding structures in the superior articular fossa of the skull. The condylar head, located at the end of the mandible, forms TMJ with the glenoid fossa of the temporal bone. This joint enables the jaw to open, close, and perform a variety of complex movements. Since CR reflects the positional relationship between the condylar head and the glenoid fossa, it does not involve the contact between the teeth. Therefore, when a patient has lost a large amount of tooth contact area and cannot locate the appropriate reference point of the prosthesis, it is generally recommended to use the stable condylar position in CR as the starting reference point.2

In clinical practice, there are differing opinions on whether dental reconstruction should be initiated with a “stable TMJ position” or should prioritize function by starting with MIP, assuming the patient is asymptomatic. Dawson advocates for using CR as a “reference point” in full-mouth prosthodontics and occlusal rehabilitation.6 By employing CR, practitioners establish a stable and reproducible standard, effectively mitigating the risk of TMJ or muscle dysfunction related to occlusal instability following reconstruction. In fixed prosthetic reconstruction, a 2025 article by Yu et al. on “Digital full-mouth fixed occlusal reconstruction” found that most scholars still consider CR the preferred reference point for reconstruction.7 However, some modern prosthodontic and occlusal philosophies suggest that it is not necessary to transition all patients to CR. They pointed out that if a patient is “functionally good and asymptomatic” under existing MIP or HO, then there may be no need to change to the theoretical “ideal occlusion”. While CR and MIP may initially appear to be opposing concepts, many clinicians have adopted a hybrid approach in their practice. They recognize CR as a vital reference point but understand that a complete transition from MIP to CR isn’t always necessary. Therefore, a CR recording area based on MIP with high reproducibility may be a fundamental prerequisite for establishing a suitable mandibular position reference point to achieve successful restoration and occlusal treatment.

Since the 1950s, various methods have been explored for recording the mandibular CR position. Shafagh and Amirloo studied chin-point guidance (CPG) and the anterior jig (AJ) in 20 patients with Class I occlusion, concluding that CPG is a reproducible CR recording method.8 In another significant comparison, Hellsing et al. evaluated the condylar positions of 15 healthy subjects with good occlusal relationships. Using either the one-handed pushback technique or the bilateral bimanual technique to capture the most retruded position of the mandible.9 They employed both the one-handed pushback technique and the bilateral bimanual technique to identify the most retruded position of the mandible, ultimately finding no significant differences between these two effective techniques for recording CR. Ramaswamy et al. examined various techniques for guiding the mandible into the CR position and found that Dawson’s bimanual manipulation (BM) technique was superior to the others.10 Additionally, the study by Schmitt et al. included that the Roth power centric bite registration (PCR) technique had a high degree of consistency.11 It is worth mentioning that these various methods are still employed in modern dental clinical practice.12 Furthermore, different CR registration methods were evaluated primarily based on their reproducibility rather than the absolute location obtained. Additionally, different CR recording methods can lead to variations in the recorded condylar locations. However, CR recording techniques themselves did not define the treatment location; rather, only reproducible locations should be considered for potential clinical applications.

Some studies on centric relation (CR) did not evaluate the reproducibility of CR recordings based on the relative position of the condylar head. For example, Lim’s research harnessed lateral cephalograms to measure incisor level and skeletal profilograms to elucidate differences between CR and MIP.13 Simon and Nicholls used the leaf gauge method as a benchmark to compare the degree of deviation of CR records between CPG and BM.14 Millet et al. provided insight by comparing CR recording positions between the traditional method and the swallowing method, using the occlusal rim as a reference point.15 In clinical practice, relying on an independent reference point for comparing different condylar positions proves to be more effective. Moreover, recent developments underscore the growing recognition of the need to maintain a stable intermaxillary relationship during occlusal reconstruction, thereby underscoring the importance of MIP.

In current meta-analysis studies, most comparative studies have focused on the reproducibility of individual CR records. However, less attention has been given to the actual distance difference between CR and MIP in the recording methods for individual CR. To effectively determine the optimal CR position, a thorough comparison with MIP is essential. This comparison will help assess whether the relative position of the condylar head, obtained through the CR recording methods commonly used in clinical practice, is stable and reliable enough to serve as a reliable occlusal reference point. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to investigate CR recording techniques in relation to MIP as a common reference point. This will involve a systematic review and comparison of the distance differences between the CR and MIP condylar positions obtained using various CR techniques to determine whether each method can provide a stable, consistent benchmark for condylar position in clinical settings. The findings from this study will help clarify the clinical applicability of different CR recording methods, allowing for a better understanding of the starting reference point for occlusal reconstruction.

## Materials and methods

This study adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) criteria to determine overall (P), intervention (I), comparison (C), and outcome (O) to form the question:

P―Individuals receiving CR recording technology.I―Condylar position obtained from the CR occlusal records acquired using different interventional CR recording techniques.C―The MIP condyle location of subjects, which served as the comparison benchmark, was assessed using similar interventional techniques.O―Differences in the position of the condylar head between CR and MIP as measured by various techniques, along with their consistency

### Search strategy and screening process

To identify eligible studies, we conducted comprehensive keyword searches across leading databases, including PubMed (National Center for Biotechnology Information, Bethesda, MD, USA), EMBASE (Elsevier, Amsterdam, Netherlands), Google Scholar (Google, Mountain View, CA, USA), and the Cochrane Library (London, UK). We employed a focused selection of terms and Boolean operators for both MeSH and free-text searches: CR technique, MIP, chin-point guidance, bimanual manipulation, gothic arch, and CR registration. Furthermore, we utilized the “related articles” feature in PubMed to broaden our search scope. Additionally, all available clinical trials were considered, with the restriction to English-language trials. We also consulted relevant articles to identify other potentially suitable studies, with no restrictions based on publication year; all articles published up to January 2026 were included.

### Selection criteria

We reviewed controlled trials and quasi-randomized trials that evaluated outcomes of obtaining the CR condylar head position. To be included in our study, trials needed to meet the following criteria: (1) clearly defined inclusion and exclusion criteria for patients, (2) participants with complete or near-complete dentition, (3) the use of various methods for obtaining the CR position, (4) a minimum of 5 subjects evaluated, (5) quantitative studies on condylar head position, including statistical data (mean, standard deviation, sample size) necessary for calculating the standardized mean difference (SMD), and (6) original research published in English. We were excluded trials that met any of the following criteria: (1) involvement of edentulous patients, (2) studies focused on removable denture wearers, (3) literature reviews, (4) case reports, (5) duplicate reports, (6) systematic reviews, (7) studies that cannot quantify the differences between CR and MIP, or (8) research comparing only different CR techniques (CR–CR) without any reference to MIP.

### Data extraction and analysis

Two reviewers (L.S.H. and Y.C.H.) independently extracted relevant participant data, along with inclusion and exclusion criteria, from the clinical trials. Their individual evaluations were subsequently compared, and any discrepancies were expertly resolved by a third reviewer (I.T.W), ensuring the highest standards of accuracy and reliability. The positions of the left and right condyles of the TMJ were determined using the CPI recording method, and the data were represented in a three-dimensional coordinate system with the initial calibration set at the MIP. In this system, on the X-axis of the sagittal plane, positive values indicated positions more anterior to the MIP, while negative values indicated positions more posterior to the MIP. Along the vertical Y-axis, positive values represented superior cranial locations compared to the MIP, whereas negative values represented more inferior and caudal locations. Additionally, the horizontal Z-axis represents positive deflections as deviations away from the outside, contrasting with negative deflections that signify shifts toward the inside.16 The three-dimensional coordinate values for all subjects in each study report were calculated to obtain the mean and standard deviation of the distance between the CR condylar position and the MIP condylar position on each axis.17

### Methodological quality appraisal

The Risfdfgk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I; Cochrane) tool was used to assess nonrandomization by two independent reviewers (L.S.H. and Y.C.H.). The assessment covered several areas, including pre-intervention, intervention, prognosis, and outcome evaluation.18

### Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager version 5.4 software (The Cochrane Collaboration, Oxford, UK). A meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines,19 and the results were expressed as SMD for continuous measures. The accuracy of the combined effect size was presented with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). DeSimonian and Laird random-effects models were used to pool the estimators of effect parameters. This model provided wider CIs and captured the statistically heterogeneous effects observed across trials. 20 Studies exhibiting sufficient clinical and methodological similarities were pooled together. The I2 test was used to assess statistical heterogeneity, reflecting the degree of variation in effect sizes across studies. We analyzed five subgroups based on different CR recording techniques: 1. CPG, 2. BM, 3. PCR, 4. Gothic arch tracer (GA), and 5. Active movement (AM). Statistical analyses were performed in three directions: X, Y, and Z. In a single-arm meta-analysis, we reported only descriptive results.

## Results

### Characteristics of inclusive trials

Our search, as depicted in the flow chart in Fig. 1, identified 1693 eligible studies. After screening titles and abstracts, we excluded duplicate studies on the same research topic. From the remaining 97 trials, we reviewed the full text and discussed their contents, ultimately identifying 45 trials relevant to CR and MIP. Of these, 33 trials were excluded after screening for compliance with the exclusion criteria, including 11 studies that did not use MIP as the reference point, 2 questionnaires, 7 studies involving edentulous patients, and 3 studies focusing on patients with temporomandibular joint disorder (TMD). Additionally, we excluded two studies that used cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) as their analysis instrument, two that utilized magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), one that employed T-scan as the test instrument, and two that compared the accuracy of CR recording materials. We also excluded three studies that presented percentages as their sole conclusions. Finally, 12 articles published between approximately 1994 and 2024 were included for our research analysis.16,21,22 All included studies employed cross-sectional designs rather than randomized controlled clinical trials. The subjects in these studies had the following characteristics: they were in good overall health with no tooth development disorder, possessed complete dentition with normal function (including retained or restored teeth, but excluding third molars), had not undergone orthodontic treatment, and had no history of TMD. Additionally, examinations revealed no TMJ or muscle-related issues, and none of the subjects exhibited bruxism or other abnormal functions (Table 1).

The methodological quality of the clinical test studies included in this analysis is summarized in Table 2 with MIP as a reference point. The risk of bias was assessed using the ROBINS-I checklist. Overall, three trials exhibited a moderate risk of bias, while ten trials showed a low risk. All included studies reported the discrepancy in condylar position between the CR bite registration and the MIP. Specifically, three studies reported CPG methods,;16,17,21 five used BM methods,;16,23–26 six employed PCR methods,; 11,21,25,27–29 three utilized GA methods,;16,23,25 and three reported various AM methods.16,26,30 Additionally, five studies reported results on bilateral condylar position. 17,24–26 This study conducted a single-arm meta-analysis to evaluate the three-dimensional spatial differences in condylar position relative to MIP among different CR bite record methods (CPG, BM, PCR, GA, AM). Displacements were measured along the X, Y, and Z axes and expressed as standardized mean deviations (SMD), with heterogeneity (I2) assessed for each axis. The meta-analysis results were presented below, organized by method and axis. Table 3 describes a detailed comparison of each method.

### Differences in condylar position between CR and MIP under the CPG method

Fig. 2 shows the difference in condylar position between CR and MIP obtained by the CPG method. In the X-axis direction, the analysis revealed no statistically significant difference was observed (SMD = 0.04; 95% CI: −0.37 to 0.45; P = 0.86), although heterogeneity was extremely high (I2 = 92%), indicating substantial variability across studies. On the other hand, the Y-axis demonstrated a statistically significant displacement (SMD = 1.17; 95% CI: 0.19 to 2.16; P = 0.02), coupled with high heterogeneity (I2 = 98%). There was no significant difference in the Z-axis direction (SMD = −0.04; 95% CI: −0.13 to 0.04; P = 0.29), and heterogeneity remained at 0%. Overall, while a significant difference was detected in the Y-axis, the high heterogeneity limited the strength of this finding, and no significant differences were observed in the X- and Z-axis directions.

### Differences in condylar position between CR and MIP under the BM method

Fig. 3 presents the integrated analysis results of the BM method. This analysis uncovered no significant differences in condylar position across any of the three axes: X-axis SMD = 0.12 (P = 0.47; I2 = 97%); Y-axis SMD = 0.40 (P = 0.20; I2 = 99%); Z-axis SMD = −0.01 (P = 0.89; I2=96%). Overall, the CR condylar position obtained by the BM method was comparable to that obtained by MIP, indicating that BM was a stable and reliable method. However, there was also a high heterogeneity among the studies.

### Differences in condylar position between CR and MIP under the PCR method

Fig. 4 illustrates the analytical results obtained through the PCR method, which demonstrated significant shifts in condylar position across all three axes. For the X-axis, the SMD was 0.35, with a 95% CI ranging from 0.17 to 0.53 (P = 0.0001). For the Y-axis, the SMD was 0.76 (95% CI: 0.53 to 1.00; P < 0.00001). Similarly, the Z-axis demonstrates an SMD of 0.66, with a 95% CI of 0.52–0.80 (P < 0.00001). These results reflected significant condylar shifts in all three axes, with moderate to considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 40%–97%), suggesting variability in effect sizes across studies.

### Differences in condylar position between CR and MIP under the GA method

Fig. 5 illustrates the results obtained using the GA method, which did not achieve statistical significance on any of the three axes. The SMD for the X-axis stood at 0.43 (P = 0.07; I2 = 95%), indicating a non-significant trend with substantial heterogeneity. The Y-axis registered an SMD of 0.89 (P = 0.20; I2 = 99%), suggesting a large but highly inconsistent effect across studies. The Z-axis showed a minimal effect size (P = 0.82; I2 = 77%), although heterogeneity remained considerable. Overall, no statistically significant differences were observed between GA method-derived CR and MIP positions. However, the consistently high heterogeneity across all axes indicates substantial variability among studies.

### Differences in condylar position between CR and MIP under the AM method

Fig. 6 evaluates condylar position using the AM method. No statistically significant differences were observed across all three axes; however, heterogeneity was extremely high (I2
> 90%), indicating substantial variability among studies. On the X-axis, the SMD was 0.00 (P = 0.97); for the Y-axis, it was −0.34 (P = 0.07); and for the Z-axis, the SMD was −0.06 (P = 0.50). While the Y-axis showed a non-significant trend toward displacement, the overall findings suggested minimal difference between CR and MIP positions. Nevertheless, the high heterogeneity limited the reliability of these results.

## Discussion

The significance of CR lies in its consistent relationship between bone-to-bone and its ability to maintain the musculoskeletal system’s stable position. By providing a repeatable and recordable relationship between the upper and lower jaws, CR offers a dependable guide for establishing a habitual bite.22 In the realm of clinical practice, the establishment of prosthetic occlusal surfaces not only reconstructs lost dentition but also maintains the health of the overall oral and jaw systems. CR acts as the horizontal reference position for the jaw, one that patients can assume during routine dental practice. When the maxillomandibular relationship is established in CR, it allows for the reproduction of articulation and facilitates the design of an effective occlusal pattern through articulator transfer. Posselt pointed out that “although the trajectories of repeated habitual opening and closing movements within the range of mandibular movement do not completely overlap, there is a common starting point and end point in the ICP.”31 However, when patients have lost teeth, restricting the available occlusal area, utilizing CR becomes increasingly vital. It serves as a criterion for assessing the existing oral relationship and for establishing a new occlusal relationship between the maxilla and mandible. Additionally, it is essential to document the difference between MIP and CR to ensure high reproducibility of this method.

This study performed a systematic review and a single-arm synthesis analysis to compare the differences in CR-MIP distances measured using various CR bite recording techniques reported in the current literature. The aim was to evaluate the consistency of these techniques in clinical practice. Overall, the results revealed that variations in CRMIP distances across different CR bite recording methods were within a narrow range in most studies, underscoring their reliability. However, the degree of heterogeneity depended on several factors, including the characteristics of the techniques, operator experience, anatomical differences among subjects, and the specific measurement methods employed. Furthermore, the meta-analysis revealed that the average CR-MIP distance differences across the various CR recording techniques were within the same order of magnitude, indicating that these techniques can all achieve similar levels of reproducibility in jaw position in adults with intact dentition. Importantly, this supported the established notion in existing literature that CR presents a reproducible region of condylar position rather than a single point obtainable only through a specific occlusal method.32 From a clinical perspective, this phenomenon supported the notion that “different CR techniques can achieve consistent results.” This allows clinicians to select the most suitable recording method based on the patient’s habits, intraoral conditions, and their own level of proficiency. However, this study also revealed moderate to high heterogeneity among these techniques. Possible sources of this heterogeneity included: (1) differences in the anatomical conditions of the study populations; (2) the inherent characteristics of each technique; (3) variations in measurement methods; and (4) differences in operator experience and learning curves. These factors may lead to slight differences in the application of the same technique across different studies. Consequently, while the aggregated results showed acceptable consistency, the heterogeneity indicators still remind clinicians to pay attention to operational details and adhere to standardized procedures to improve reproducibility.33

McCollum first described this one-handed guidance method that Ash and Ramfjord later popularized.34 In the CPG technique, the operator’s thumb applies light pressure to the depression of the patient’s chin. This guidance helps the patient’s mouth to close naturally, prompting the masticatory muscles to lift without tension and allowing the jaws to move upward. The condyles are positioned in a centric relationship, underscoring the importance of pushing the mandible backward and guiding it upward to achieve this centric position, while ensuring it rotates along its axis of rotation. Simon believed that this technique could provide the necessary CR records for clinical practice and that repeating the process could maintain a small range of biological variability, which is sufficient to ensure the accuracy and reproducibility of CR records.14 Piehslinger et al. examined the reproducibility of the condylar reference position using the CPG technique, which does not require mandibular tension, in both subjects with TMJ symptoms and asymptomatic individuals.35 They found that the condylar reference position was highly reproducible in both groups, with no significant difference between them. Notably, 58.6% of the recorded measurements fell within 0.1 mm, and 24.3% were within 0.2 mm. The authors concluded that CPG is user-friendly for obtaining CR records in clinical practice and can accurately establish reference points. However, Jász et al. noted that while this technique yields a high degree of reproducibility in CR recordings, there is a risk of excessively pushing the mandible toward the posterior joint position.16 Additionally, Jankelson and Adib used a motion-measuring instrument to monitor the movement of the mandible in three dimensions and assessed the impact of applied force on the position of the mandibular edge retraction.36 Their findings indicated that when the applied force increased by more than 5 pounds (about 22.24 N), the position of mandibular edge retraction would be significantly altered in all three dimensions.

The frost plot of the CPG technique, as illustrated in this study (Fig. 2), revealed no significant differences between the X and Z axes, except for the Y-axis. This data indicated that the CPG technique did not cause any lateral shift. Furthermore, the results confirmed that the CPG technique effectively pushed the chin backward and guided the patient’s mandible into a closed position. Variations in bite forces exerted by the subjects during natural jaw closure may result in varying mandibular displacement along the Y-axis during CR recording. However, the operator must apply the appropriate force to the patient to avoid pushing the mandible too far back into the glenoid fossa. When obtaining a CR bite by CPG, it is crucial to consider both the magnitude and direction of the applied force. A gentle force should be utilized to guide the mandible in a posterior-superior direction, enabling the condyle to secure its most stable position within the glenoid fossa. Avoiding lateral forces is critical, as they can cause undesirable lateral interference. The force applied should be moderate, usually with a light touch, to avoid excessive pressure that may trigger defensive muscle tension or discomfort for the patient. Generally, a force of approximately 1–2 pounds (about 4.4–8.8 N) was sufficient to stabilize and guide the jaw into the CR position. Applying excessive force can cause the patient’s muscles to contract, potentially disrupting the mandible’s proper positioning.16 Moreover, it’s essential to observe the patient’s jaw relaxation while applying force to ensure that muscle tension does not interfere. This can help reduce muscle involvement through guiding the patient to gently open the mouth and relax the masseter and temporalis muscles. Before recording the bite, using an AJ or cotton rolls for the patient to bite on the anterior teeth for 5–10 min can help achieve occlusal deprogramming and alleviate any mandibular deviation interference.11

Ramaswamy et al. believed that the BM technique demonstrated excellent precision and reproducible results. 10 The frost plot of the BM technique presented in this study (Fig. 3) showed no significant differences across all three axes. While Keshvad’s average distance values were higher than those of other studies, the CR achieved with the BM method remained close to the MIP position.23 This method involves specific upward guidance of the mandible on both sides, with the operator holding the mandibular lower edge with both hands. By applying downward pressure with the thumbs and upward pressure with the other fingers, the operator attempts to fix the condyle in the uppermost position within the glenoid fossa. This passive technique for recording CR was first introduced by Long37 and later refined and generalized by Dawson,6 who asserted that it effectively positioned and aligned the condyle-disk assembly in the most superior and anterior positions within the glenoid fossa. Moreover, McKee advocated three advantages of positioning the condyle in the joint bony position through the BM technique: 1. It represents the strongest position where bone can support bone; 2. It fosters the most harmonious functioning of the masticatory muscles; 3. This condylar position eliminates occlusal interference during mandibular movement within the range of motion.38 In addition, many researchers assert that bimanual manipulation is an effective method for accurately positioning the condyle within the glenoid fossa in CR and for providing reproducible results.37 However, Wood argued that mastering this bilateral manipulation technique requires exceptional skill and precise timing. If not performed correctly, it can result in excessive pressure applied at inappropriate times and directions, potentially causing the patient to involuntarily protrude the mandible.21 Therefore, this technique was particularly sensitive to the operator’s skills.

The power-centric bite registration, using the two-piece wax registration method advocated by Ross, involves two sections of blue wax. The front section extends from the distal side of the upper right incisor to the distal side of the opposite tooth, while the back section covers the second premolar and first molar on the right side to the opposite teeth.39 This technique combines anterior stops and mandibular manipulation to accurately record the CR position of the condyle. The anterior wax-up of the first section enables the operator to deprogram the neuromuscular system by touching the patient’s chin-point to guide the mandible into a centric position, elevating the condyle, and instructing the patient to close the jaw until a 2-mm occlusal relationship is achieved with the posterior teeth. The second section utilized a hardened anterior stop and softened posterior wax. The patient is guided to close the jaw until the lower incisors contact the hardened anterior stop. Having a rigid anterior stop encourages the patient’s biting muscles to contract voluntarily, allowing the condyle to automatically move into the most superior position of the joint.40 In this study, the frost plot of the PCR technique, as shown in Fig. 4, revealed that the SMD in displacement from the origin along the X was 0.35, and the Y axis showed a slightly offset of 0.76. These results indicated that the PCR technique pushed the chin backward, aligning the patient’s mandibular closure with the direction of spontaneous closure. Additionally, most studies also suggest that PCR offers high accuracy and repeatability. However, to guide the accuracy of condylar position, it is crucial to use methods that reduce or eliminate the effects of occlusion on the musculature.

Many studies have demonstrated that the memorized neuromuscular system positions the mandible for maximum intercuspation, regardless of the condyle position. This often results in a significant deviation from the expected closure pattern. Unfortunately, clinicians often misinterpret the mandibular position obtained in this process―as the occlusally determined condylar position―for the actual condylar position. This confusion underscores the critical importance of neuromuscular deprogramming for achieving precise, reproducible condylar positioning. To enhance the accuracy of CR recordings, Schmitt et al. recommend that patients bite on a cotton roll for 5–10 min. This simple yet effective deprogramming technique improves the reliability of the CR bite.11 Additionally, studies by Alvarez et al.41 and Cordray et al.42 reported that an initial 15-min deprogramming period using an anterior jig is effective in obtaining accurate CR records.

GA is also referred to as the central bearing point method or pinpoint tracking technique. Swiss scholar Alfred Gysi first proposed this method. It is an active technique that requires minimal dentist-guided mandibular movement into CR. Initially developed as an extraoral method by Gysi using an adjustable articulator, it was later modified into an intraoral technique by Gerber et al.43 In this method, a triangular mandibular movement path is drawn on the board with a stylus, where the vertex corresponds to the CR of the mandible, that is, the condylar process is located in the most retracted position within the glenoid fossa. Yang et al. studied the differences in forward movement of complete dentures made with three different CR records: the GA apex, 1 mm in front of the GA apex, and the patient’s natural closure during centric occlusion.44 Their results indicated that the average displacement of the complete dentures was smallest when recorded at the horizontal relationship 1 mm in front of the GA apex, and patient comfort was highest. The authors concluded that the GA apex determines the joint’s final physiological position. However, while it shows high repeatability of this position, it cannot reflect the patient’s original MIP, which is necessary for optimal maximum chewing function.

In edentulous patients, even though proprioceptive receptors in teeth are absent, receptors in joints, muscles, and mucosa remain functional. This means that the lifting muscles help guide the mandible back to its original central jaw position through repeated physiological movements at the joint. Therefore, recording the horizontal relationship at 1 mm in front of the GA apex accurately captures the patient’s original MIP position.44 Research by Paixão et al. further supports this assertion, indicating that the GA apex represents the most retracted position of mandibular movement, with a distance of 1.4 mm anterior to the GA apex nearly overlapping with the MIP.24 In our current study (Fig. 5), we observed the SMD of 0.43 along the X-axis for MIP displacement and 0.89 for the Y-axis offset. In Keshvad’s trial,23 the GA vertex was approximately 1 mm forward and 3 mm upward from the MIP, closely aligning with the inferences of Yang et al.44 and Paixão et al.24 Additionally, the GA vertex studied by Jász was also positioned anterior to the MIP. The authors explained that this might be due to translational movement of the condyle, resulting in a significantly more anterior and inferior position.16 It is essential to ensure that when using this method, the eccentric recording is not performed at extreme jaw positions. If the measurements exceed a range of 6 mm from the center during normal function, inaccuracies can arise in determining that the condylar position is too anterior.

In this study, the AM method revealed no significant differences across any of the three axes. In 1955, Shanahan introduced the “swallow or free closure” technique, suggesting that the constant function of swallowing saliva provided a basis for determining jaw position and occlusional alignment. During swallowing, the jaw was lifted to a vertical dimension by the elevator muscles and retracted to the central position by the tongue muscles, allowing swallowing to serve as a reliable guide for recording CR.45 Moreover, Kurth used the swallowing reflex to identify the CR location.46 However, Niswonger et al. mentioned that during swallowing, the jaw moves from its resting position to the CR and back again, forming a cycle, so the CR position obtained will differ from the swallowing position.47 While some authors have advocated for employing swallowing techniques to record CR,45,48,49 other studies have concluded that the mandible is not in the terminal hinge position during swallowing41,50,51 and that this position can vary highly depending on the patient’s cooperation.15,41

Clinical significance is interpreted below: firstly, most methods for recording CR demonstrated minimal difference in condylar position when compared to MIP. CPG, BM, PCR, GA, and various AM methods showed slight variation across most axes. Consequently, in the absence of TMJ pain or other symptoms, practitioners can use either CR or MIP as a functional treatment benchmark in restorative dentistry. Second, the Y-axis is the direction most prone to variation. CPG or PCR frequently exhibited significant differences along this axis, underscoring that CR recording has a particular effect on the mandible’s vertical position. Finally, it is crucial to recognize that even in the absence of statistically significant differences, variations in operator skill, equipment (including jigs, leaf gauges, and wax), and patient conditions (such as TMJ status and muscle tone) can substantially impact outcomes. In summary, while some methods (e.g., PCR) appeared to induce measurable condylar displacement, others (e.g., BM, GA, and AM) did not show statistically significant differences. The high heterogeneity prevalent in most analyses highlighted significant differences in measurement techniques, study design, and patient characteristics. Therefore, the clinical equivalence or superiority of any single method cannot be definitively determined.

The clinical trials included in this study were systematic reviews that used CPI as the primary outcome measure and enrolled healthy subjects. This choice was made due to the lack of standardized equipment for comparing CR techniques, which hindered statistical comparisons across the articles. Additionally, the positioning points for CR measurement differed between dentulous and edentulous patients, necessitating separate studies for each group and further limiting the number of articles that could be considered. Another limitation was that articles analyzed in this systematic review that lacked significant heterogeneity also lacked randomized controlled trial designs. Furthermore, the small number of subjects per group raised concerns about the generalizability of the findings.

With advances in technology, cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may be incorporated into TMJ examinations in the future to assess the accuracy of the CR position. A pivotal study by McKee evaluated the CR bite records from 132 dentists, each using their preferred methods, and compared the repeatability of these records after standardized training, with a tolerance level of 0.11 mm on the Denar Centric- Check instrument.52 The findings showed that dentists’ skill and proficiency significantly influenced the accuracy of the CR records. Furthermore, it’s essential to understand that different recording methods can yield varying CR results, even for the same individual.17 Time also plays a crucial role, as records taken at different time points can show notable differences.53,54 Variability introduced by different operators can further complicate the accuracy of results.11 Additionally, factors such as the subject’s body position during recording and the material used can contribute to discrepancies in CR recordings.55 Consequently, while a range of methods exists to record CR, their theoretical capability to replicate the establishment of CR hinges on two critical factors: the dentist’s technical expertise and consistency in performing the recordings, and the patient’s active cooperation. It is the interplay of these elements that ultimately ensures the accuracy of CR recordings.

In conclusion, Table 3 shows that different methods for obtaining CR records yield varying condylar positions. Despite the lack of a universal agreement on the ideal mandibular CR position, it is crucial to recognize that achieving a consistent, reproducible position is paramount. Additionally, finding a mandibular position that is clinically acceptable to patients is essential, as CR represents a unique and personal space shaped by each patient’s physiological tolerance. The methods explored in this study, despite their individual advantages and disadvantages, all exhibit a commendable level of reproducibility in the CR positions obtained. Importantly, the dentist’s technical proficiency is closely linked to the distinct functional factors of each patient. Therefore, dentists need to apply these concepts thoughtfully, guided by their understanding and selection of appropriate CR recording techniques, to achieve optimal reconstruction results.

 This study aims to compare the effects of zirconia all-ceramic crowns and metal-ceramic crowns on inflammation and chewing function in patients receiving full-crown restorations and to explore whether improvements in gingival health mediate these effects – an aspect rarely addressed in previous studies. A total of 196 patients treated from January 2023 to January 2024 were included. After propensity score matching, 79 patients in each group had similar baseline characteristics. Serum interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were measured before treatment and at 6 weeks and 3 months after restoration. Masticatory efficiency, visual analogue scale (VAS) scores, gingival condition, and clinical success were also evaluated. Both groups showed significant decreases in IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP after restoration, with greater reductions in the zirconia group (3-month IL-6: 7.2 ± 2.0 vs 9.8 ± 2.5 pg/mL; TNF-α: 11.5 ± 2.7 vs 14.3 ± 3.0 pg/mL; CRP: 3.5 ± 1.0 vs 4.8 ± 1.3 mg/L; all P  < .001). The zirconia group also showed higher chewing efficiency (78.6 ± 9.2% vs 70.2 ± 8.9%), higher VAS scores (7.8 ± 1.0 vs 6.9 ± 1.1), and fewer gingival symptoms (7.6% vs 17.7%, all P  < .05). Mediation analysis revealed that part of the CRP reduction was mediated by improved gingival health, providing novel mechanistic insight. This study not only confirms the superior clinical and anti-inflammatory effects of zirconia restorations over metal-ceramic crowns but also identifies gingival health as a potential mediator linking restorative material and systemic inflammation. These findings provide new evidence for the biological advantages of zirconia in prosthodontic practice.

 1. Introduction
 Tooth defect is one of the most common indications for prosthodontic treatment in clinical dentistry. Its causes include caries, trauma, tooth fracture, tooth structure loss, and esthetic demands. [ 1 , 2 ] Without timely restoration, it may not only lead to further loss of tooth structure and displacement of adjacent teeth but also cause occlusal disorders and temporomandibular joint dysfunction, seriously affecting patients’ quality of life. [ 3 ] Full-crown restoration, as a primary method to restore tooth morphology and function, is widely used in clinical practice, and its long-term efficacy is closely related to the choice of restorative material. [ 4 ] 
 Porcelain-fused-to-metal (PFM) crowns were once widely used due to their high strength and reliability. However, with prolonged service time, their limitations have gradually become apparent. [ 5 ] Common problems include poor esthetics due to the metal substrate’s opacity, marginal discoloration, porcelain chipping, gingival discoloration, and potential metal allergy reactions. [ 6 , 7 ] In addition, the biocompatibility of metal materials with periodontal tissues is limited, which may increase the risk of gingival inflammation and adversely affect the long-term stability of the restoration. [ 8 ] 
 With the advancement of dental restorative materials, all-ceramic restorations have attracted increasing attention due to their combined esthetic and functional advantages. Among them, zirconia, as a third-generation high-performance ceramic material, possesses high strength, high toughness, excellent wear resistance, and outstanding translucency, thereby meeting the dual requirements of anterior esthetics and posterior load-bearing. [ 9 , 10 ] More importantly, zirconia is metal-free, which avoids metal-related allergic reactions and marginal gingival discoloration. Its dense and smooth surface is less prone to plaque accumulation, thereby potentially reducing periodontal irritation and inflammation, and providing a biological advantage for the long-term success of restorations. [ 11 ] 
 In recent years, scholars have begun to pay attention to the potential impact of restorative materials on the body’s inflammatory status. Inflammatory factors such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and C-reactive protein (CRP) are important biological indicators for evaluating systemic and local inflammatory states, playing central roles in both periodontal disease and chronic systemic inflammatory responses. [ 12 , 13 ] Restorative materials may indirectly influence inflammatory factor levels by improving gingival margin adaptation and reducing bacterial adhesion. [ 13 ] Previous studies have reported that metal-based restorations may release trace metal ions that can trigger oxidative stress and promote inflammatory cytokine expression, whereas zirconia ceramics exhibit excellent biocompatibility and smoother surfaces that reduce bacterial accumulation and gingival irritation. [ 14 ] Several clinical and in vitro studies also suggest that zirconia restorations are associated with lower gingival index scores and fewer inflammatory responses compared with metal-ceramic crowns. [ 15 ] However, comprehensive clinical evidence directly linking restorative materials with dynamic changes in inflammatory biomarkers remains limited. If clinical evidence confirms that zirconia restorations are superior to traditional PFM crowns in reducing inflammatory factors, this would not only help prolong restoration longevity but might also improve patients’ systemic inflammatory status and overall health.
 On the other hand, the restoration of masticatory function is a core indicator of prosthetic effectiveness. It includes both objective measures of masticatory efficiency and subjective patient-reported evaluations (visual analogue scale [VAS] scores). Existing studies have mostly focused on the esthetic outcomes and mechanical properties of zirconia restorations, while exploration of the potential association between inflammation control – gingival health improvement – masticatory function recovery remains relatively limited. [ 14 ] In particular, systematic clinical evidence is still lacking regarding the mechanism by which restorative materials improve periodontal tissue conditions, thereby indirectly influencing inflammatory factor levels and promoting functional recovery.
 Based on the above evidence, previous studies have mainly focused on comparing zirconia and metal-ceramic restorations with respect to mechanical strength, esthetic performance, and short-term periodontal outcomes. However, few investigations have systematically evaluated their influence on inflammatory biomarkers or explored the potential mechanisms linking restorative materials to changes in systemic inflammation and masticatory function. Therefore, the present study included nearly 200 patients who underwent full-crown restoration. Propensity score matching was applied to minimize baseline differences, and zirconia all-ceramic crowns were compared with PFM crowns in terms of inflammatory factor levels, masticatory function recovery, and clinical success rates. Furthermore, mediation analysis was conducted to examine whether improvements in gingival condition mediated the effects of restorative material on inflammatory responses. The present study aims to provide integrated clinical and biochemical evidence on the relationship between restorative materials and inflammatory status.
 Accordingly, the research hypothesis was that zirconia all-ceramic crowns would achieve greater reductions in inflammatory factor levels and better improvement in masticatory function than PFM crowns, partly through enhanced gingival health.
 2. Materials and methods
 2.1. General information
 This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Huanggang Central Hospital. A total of 196 patients who underwent treatment in our hospital from January 2023 to January 2024 were included in this study. All treatments were completed by 3 prosthodontists with extensive clinical experience. Patients were divided into 2 groups according to the restorative material: observation group (zirconia all-ceramic crowns, n = 102) and control group (PFM crowns, n = 94). To ensure comparability of baseline characteristics, propensity score matching was applied, and finally 79 patients were included in each group.
 2.1.1. Inclusion criteria
 Age ≥ 18 years, regardless of sex; Indication for full-crown restoration due to caries, tooth fracture, tooth structure loss, or esthetic demand; Good preservation of the restored tooth root and stable periodontal condition; Oral condition suitable for full-crown restoration with basically normal occlusal relationship; Complete clinical data, and patients able to complete follow-up.

 2.1.2. Exclusion criteria
 Severe systemic diseases: uncontrolled diabetes, hematological diseases, severe cardiovascular diseases, immune system disorders, or patients undergoing radiotherapy/chemotherapy; Oral conditions unsuitable for restoration: severe periodontitis, tooth mobility degree III, severe alveolar bone resorption, obvious periapical lesions, severe occlusal disorders, or temporomandibular joint dysfunction; Unfavorable lifestyle habits: long-term heavy smoking, alcohol abuse, severe bruxism, or nocturnal grinding; Special populations: pregnant or lactating women; Concomitant oral diseases: oral mucosal diseases (e.g., leukoplakia, oral lichen planus), maxillofacial tumors, etc; Allergy: history of allergy to restorative materials such as metals or resins; Poor compliance: psychiatric disorders or intellectual disabilities affecting treatment and follow-up, or missing follow-up data.

 2.2. Restorative methods
 All patients underwent tooth preparation under strict aseptic conditions and local anesthesia. The procedures were performed by 3 attending physicians from the same medical team to minimize the impact of operational differences on study outcomes.
 2.2.1. Control group (porcelain-fused-to-metal crown restoration) [ 15 ] 
 Tooth preparation was carried out according to the patient’s actual defect condition. Traditional impression-taking was performed using silicone rubber impression materials, and plaster was poured to obtain the working model. Following the standard procedure for PFM restorations, the basal wax pattern was fabricated with appropriate thickness, followed by casting, polishing, and forming. Porcelain powder was then applied on the metal substrate, layered, and sintered to form the restoration. Multiple polishing and refinements were performed to complete the prosthesis. During the process, trial fitting was conducted to check marginal adaptation and proximal contact, followed by occlusal inspection and adjustment to ensure stable occlusal relationship. Finally, cementation was performed and excess cement was removed. Routine oral hygiene instructions were provided postoperatively, and patients were scheduled for follow-up at 6 weeks after restoration.

 2.2.2. Observation group (zirconia all-ceramic crown restoration) [ 16 ] 
 Based on a comprehensive assessment of the patient’s dental condition, full-crown tooth preparation was performed. A digital workflow was applied: intraoral scanning was used to obtain digital dental models, computer-aided design was employed for crown design, and computer-aided manufacturing was used to mill and polish the zirconia coping. After veneering and sintering, the final restoration was completed. During intraoral trial fitting, the adaptation of the prosthesis to adjacent teeth and gingival margins was checked, and occlusal inspection and adjustment were performed to ensure proper crown height and occlusal relationship. Final cementation was carried out using resin-modified glass ionomer cement. After removal of excess material, the restoration was rechecked to confirm proper marginal adaptation and stable occlusion.

 2.3. Data collection
 This retrospective study focused on collecting data regarding patients’ general information, inflammatory factor levels, masticatory function, and clinical outcomes, as detailed below. To minimize potential measurement bias, data on masticatory efficiency and VAS function scores were extracted from medical records and follow-up documentation by 2 independent investigators who were not involved in the original treatments and were blinded to the type of crown material used. Both investigators followed standardized data extraction procedures, and inter-rater agreement was confirmed (intra-class correlation coefficient = 0.89), ensuring the reliability of subjective outcome data.

 2.4. General information
 Patient demographic and prosthodontic-related information was recorded, including age, sex, presence of chronic systemic diseases (e.g., hypertension, diabetes), smoking history, bruxism habit, number of restored teeth (single vs multiple), location of restored teeth (anterior vs posterior), reason for restoration (caries, fracture, defect, or esthetic demand), pulp status (vital pulp vs post–root canal treatment), and periodontal condition (healthy vs mild inflammation).
 2.4.1. Inflammatory factor levels
 At baseline (before restoration), 6 weeks, and 3 months after restoration, venous blood samples were collected from patients. Serum was separated to measure inflammatory markers, including IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP. The measurements were performed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, strictly following the instructions provided by the reagent kits.

 2.4.2. Evaluation of masticatory function
 Objective index: Masticatory efficiency was assessed using the color-mixing method/sieving method. The distribution of chewed samples passing through different sieves was calculated and expressed as a percentage.
 Subjective index: The VAS (0–10 points) was used to evaluate patients’ self-perception of masticatory function, where 0 indicated complete inability to chew and 10 indicated completely normal chewing. Both indices were assessed at baseline, 6 weeks, and 3 months after restoration.
 
 2.4.3. Clinical outcomes and safety
 During follow-up, restoration-related adverse events were recorded, including gingival swelling/bleeding, debonding of the restoration, and porcelain chipping. Clinical success rate was defined as: good marginal adaptation of the restoration, stable occlusal relationship, absence of dislodgement or significant complications, and satisfactory patient-reported outcomes.

 2.4.4. Other indicators
 To explore potential mechanisms, additional gingival condition indicators (e.g., gingival index) were collected for mediation effect analysis, in order to determine whether the effect of restorative materials on inflammatory factor improvement was partially achieved through improvements in gingival health.

 2.5. Statistical analysis
 All data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 (Armonk) and R 4.2.0 software. Measurement data were tested for normality and expressed as mean ± standard deviation ( x̄ ± s ). Comparisons between groups were performed using the independent-samples t test. Categorical data were expressed as counts and percentages, and comparisons between groups were conducted using the χ 2 test or Fisher exact test.
 Inflammatory factors (IL-6, TNF-α, CRP) and masticatory function indices (masticatory efficiency, VAS scores) were measured at 3 time points: baseline, 6 weeks, and 3 months post-restoration. Repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, with “time” as the within-subject factor and “group” as the between-subject factor, and including the time × group interaction effect. If the sphericity assumption was violated, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. To further verify robustness, selected indicators were additionally analyzed using linear mixed-effects models (LMM), with individuals set as random intercepts.
 To evaluate clinically significant improvements, the proportion of patients in each group reaching predefined thresholds (e.g., IL-6 reduction ≥ 30%, CRP reduction ≥ 1.0 mg/L, VAS score improvement ≥ 2 points) was calculated. Effect estimates were expressed as relative risk or odds ratio (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Mediation analysis was conducted using bias-corrected bootstrap methods (5000 resamples) to calculate the indirect effect of restorative materials on CRP reduction mediated through gingival condition improvement, along with the 95% CI.
 Adverse events during follow-up (gingival swelling/bleeding, restoration debonding, porcelain chipping) and overall clinical success rates were similarly compared between groups using relative risk or OR with 95% CI for statistical inference. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P  < .05 was considered statistically significant.
 The sample size in this study was determined by the number of eligible patients treated within the study period (January 2023 to January 2024). To verify whether this sample size was adequate, a post hoc power analysis was conducted based on the between-group difference in CRP levels at 3 months (mean difference = 1.3 mg/L, SD = 1.2 mg/L). Using a two-tailed t -test with α = 0.05, the calculated power (1−β) was 0.93, indicating that the present sample size (n = 158 after matching) was sufficient to detect clinically meaningful differences in inflammatory outcomes.

 3.3. Comparison of response rates of inflammatory factors
 Further response rate analysis was conducted (Table 3 ). At 3 months post-restoration, the proportion of patients with IL-6 reduction ≥ 30% was 70.9% in the observation group, significantly higher than 46.8% in the control group (RR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.17–1.97, P  = .002). Similarly, the proportion of patients with CRP reduction ≥ 1.0 mg/L was 77.2% in the observation group, also significantly higher than 57.0% in the control group (RR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.10–1.68, P  = .004). In addition, the proportion of patients with an improvement of ≥2 points in masticatory function VAS scores was 73.4% in the observation group, markedly higher than 54.4% in the control group (OR = 2.30, 95% CI: 1.22–4.33, P  = .010).

 3.4. Mediation effect analysis of gingival improvement on the relationship between restorative materials and CRP changes
 To explore the potential mechanism by which restorative materials influence the reduction of inflammatory factor levels through improvement of gingival conditions, a mediation effect analysis was performed (Table 4 ). Results showed that the total effect of restorative material on CRP level change was −1.25 (95% CI: −1.65 to −0.86, P  < .001). After controlling for gingival improvement, the direct effect of material on CRP remained significant (β = −0.88, 95% CI: −1.23 to −0.54, P  < .001). Meanwhile, the indirect effect mediated through gingival improvement was −0.37 (95% CI: −0.58 to −0.19, P  < .001). These findings suggest that the beneficial impact of zirconia all-ceramic crown restorations on inflammatory factor levels is partially achieved through the improvement of gingival health.

 3.6. Adverse events and clinical success rate during follow-up
 During follow-up, both groups experienced certain proportions of adverse events (Table 6 ). The incidence of gingival swelling/bleeding in the observation group was 7.6% (6/79), significantly lower than 17.7% (14/79) in the control group (RR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.18–0.99, P  = .047). Regarding restoration debonding and porcelain chipping, the incidence in the observation group was 3.8% and 1.3%, respectively, lower than 10.1% and 6.3% in the control group, though the differences were not statistically significant ( P  > .05).

 4. Discussion
 In this study, propensity score matching was used to compare zirconia all-ceramic crowns with PFM crowns in terms of inflammatory factors, masticatory function, and clinical outcomes. The results showed that zirconia all-ceramic crowns were superior to PFM crowns in improving inflammatory markers, promoting functional recovery, and increasing overall clinical success rates.
 Regarding inflammatory factors, at 6 weeks and 3 months of follow-up, patients in the zirconia group exhibited significant reductions in IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP levels, with greater decreases than those observed in the control group. This finding suggests that restorative materials themselves may play a role in the regulation of inflammation. As a high-density ceramic material, zirconia has a smooth surface, releases no metal ions, and is less prone to plaque biofilm formation, thereby reducing microleakage at crown margins and alleviating periodontal inflammation. [ 15 , 17 ] By contrast, PFM crowns are associated with risks such as marginal discoloration, metal ion release, and gingival irritation, which may trigger or aggravate local inflammation. Thus, the superior biocompatibility of zirconia all-ceramic crowns may be the primary reason for their ability to reduce inflammatory factor levels. [ 18 ] Furthermore, the mediation analysis in this study demonstrated that the beneficial effect of zirconia on CRP reduction was partially mediated by improvements in gingival health. This suggests that soft tissue response serves as an important intermediary link between restorative materials and systemic inflammation, providing new evidence for understanding the interaction between prosthetic materials and the host.
 With respect to functional recovery, this study found that zirconia all-ceramic crowns achieved more significant improvements in both objective masticatory efficiency and subjective VAS scores. A possible explanation lies in the superior strength and toughness of zirconia, which allows stable restorations with reduced tooth preparation, thereby preserving more natural tooth structure and enabling more rational occlusal relationships and stress distribution. [ 19 , 20 ] Meanwhile, the reduction in inflammation contributed to improved periodontal support, further enhancing masticatory efficiency. These findings indicate that restorative materials not only determine the mechanical durability of prostheses but also indirectly influence patients’ functional recovery and quality of life through favorable interactions with surrounding tissues. [ 21 ] 
 In terms of adverse events and overall clinical success rate, patients in the zirconia all-ceramic crown group had a significantly lower incidence of gingival swelling/bleeding and a higher overall clinical success rate compared with the PFM group. Although no statistically significant differences were observed between the 2 groups in restoration debonding and porcelain chipping, the favorable marginal adaptation, wear resistance, and lower gingival irritation of zirconia still provide theoretical support for its long-term stability. [ 22 ] Clinically, this indicates that zirconia is not only suitable for anterior esthetic restorations but also demonstrates high reliability in posterior regions subjected to greater occlusal forces.
 It should be noted that this study has certain limitations. First, the follow-up period was only 3 months, which limited the evaluation of long-term stability and late complications of restorations. Second, inflammatory factor levels may be influenced by multiple factors, including patients’ baseline inflammatory status, oral hygiene habits, and lifestyle. Although propensity score matching was applied to reduce baseline differences, it is difficult to completely rule out potential confounding effects. Third, this was a single-center study with a relatively limited sample size, and the generalizability of the findings requires further validation through multi-center, large-sample, and long-term studies. In addition, some statistical limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective design restricted the ability to control all potential confounders prospectively. While repeated measures ANOVA and linear mixed-effects models were used to account for within-subject correlations, residual bias due to unmeasured variables cannot be fully excluded. Moreover, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied, and the results should therefore be interpreted with caution.
 In summary, this study confirmed that zirconia all-ceramic crown restorations are superior to PFM crowns in reducing inflammatory factor levels, improving masticatory function, and increasing clinical success rates. The advantages of zirconia are reflected not only in its mechanical properties and esthetic outcomes but also in its potential to promote gingival health and reduce inflammatory responses, thereby exerting long-term protective effects. These findings provide new clinical evidence for the wide application of zirconia in oral prosthodontics and suggest that future development of restorative materials should pay greater attention to their biological interactions with host tissues.

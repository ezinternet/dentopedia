Xerostomia and Its Cellular Targets
Int J Mol Sci. 2023. PMID 36982432. PMCID PMC10049126. DOI 10.3390/ijms24065358
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10049126/ (PubMed Central full text)

ABSTRACT
Xerostomia, the subjective feeling of a dry mouth associated with dysfunction of the salivary glands, is mainly caused by radiation and chemotherapy, various systemic and autoimmune diseases, and drugs. As saliva plays numerous essential roles in oral and systemic health, xerostomia significantly reduces quality of life, but its prevalence is increasing. Salivation mainly depends on parasympathetic and sympathetic nerves, and the salivary glands responsible for this secretion move fluid unidirectionally through structural features such as the polarity of acinar cells. Saliva secretion is initiated by the binding of released neurotransmitters from nerves to specific G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) on acinar cells. This signal induces two intracellular calcium (Ca2+) pathways (Ca2+ release from the endoplasmic reticulum and Ca2+ influx across the plasma membrane), and this increased intracellular Ca2+ concentration causes the translocation of the water channel aquaporin 5 (AQP5) to the apical membrane. Consequently, the GPCR-mediated increased [Ca2+]i in acinar cells promotes saliva secretion, and this saliva moves into the oral cavity through the ducts. In this review, we seek to elucidate the potential of GPCRs, the inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor (IP3R), store-operated Ca2+ entry (SOCE), and AQP5, which are essential for salivation, as cellular targets in the etiology of xerostomia.

1. Introduction
Saliva is essential for maintaining a healthy oral environment and overall health. Xerostomia (dry mouth) is defined as a subjective feeling of oral dryness (Greek "xeros" = dry, "stoma" = mouth). It results in decreased salivary flow and changes in saliva composition, but a dry sensation can also occur with normal salivary gland function. Main causes: aging, head-and-neck radiation, Sjögren's syndrome; the most common cause is drug-induced xerostomia, associated with more than 400 different drugs. Xerostomia increases bad breath, dental caries, and dental erosion, and reduces quality of life (food intake problems, depression). Estimated prevalence of persistent xerostomia 10–50% (conservative ~20% general population); more common in women (up to 30%) and older adults (up to 50%). There is no permanent FDA-approved solution for salivary gland hypofunction and resultant xerostomia — hence the need for mechanistic research.

2. Unidirectional Movement of Fluid in the Salivary Glands
2.1 Structure: A healthy adult produces 0.5–1.5 L saliva/day, 90% from the three main glands: parotid (PAR, serous/watery, stimulated saliva), submandibular (SM, mixed serous+mucous, majority of unstimulated saliva), sublingual (SL, mostly mucous). Salivary amylase is abundant in PAR and SM but virtually absent in SL. A fourth pair, the tubarial glands (posterior nasopharynx), was proposed in 2020. Hundreds of minor salivary glands are distributed in the oral cavity; lower-lip glands are biopsied to diagnose Sjögren's. Ducts: Stensen's (PAR), Wharton's (SM), ducts of Rivinus (SL).
2.2 Polarized acinar cells and support cells: Salivary glands comprise acinar cells (produce saliva; markers aquaporin 5 (AQP5) and Mist1), ductal cells (transport), and myoepithelial cells (contract to expel saliva). Tight junctions maintain acinar cell polarity and permit unidirectional secretion, forming a barrier between blood and tissue fluid. Acinar cells secrete an isotonic plasma-like fluid; ductal system (intercalated ducts → striated ducts) makes saliva hypotonic by secreting HCO3- and K+ and reabsorbing Na+ and Cl-. Myoepithelial cells contract in response to nerve stimulation.

3. Salivation by GPCR-Mediated Intracellular Calcium (Ca2+) Signaling
3.1 Salivation is under autonomic control. Fluid secretion is initiated by neurotransmitters from parasympathetic nerves binding a specific GPCR; increased intracellular Ca2+ in acinar cells is the major trigger. Protein secretion is regulated mainly by cyclic AMP via sympathetic nerves.
3.2 GPCRs as keys: Salivary gland cells are non-excitatory, lack voltage-sensitive channels, and communicate through GPCRs. M3 muscarinic acetylcholine receptors (M3 mAChRs) are essential for parasympathetic control of salivation in mice. In M1 or M3 single-KO mice pilocarpine-induced salivation was reduced at low dose (1 mg/kg) but not at high dose (15 mg/kg); in M1/M3 double-KO mice salivation was completely lost. Human labial glands express M1, M3, M4 mAChRs; upregulation of M1, M3, M5 seen in Sjögren's patients. Other GPCRs studied: GPR39 (zinc ligand — gargling zinc solution increased salivary secretion in normal and hyposalivation groups including Sjögren's), histamine H1 receptor, sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) receptor (triggers Ca2+, induces IL-6 and Fas in Sjögren's-related apoptosis), bradykinin B2 receptor, and P2Y purinergic receptors. The P2Y2R agonist diquafosol stimulates transepithelial water transport and is approved for dry eye in South Korea and Japan (Diquas).
3.3 Fluid secretion mechanism: GPCR stimulation (mAChR, α1-adrenergic) → phospholipase C (PLC) → hydrolyzes PIP2 into IP3 and DAG. IP3 binds IP3 receptors (IP3R2, IP3R3 concentrated at apical pole) → Ca2+ release from ER; [Ca2+]i rises apically, spreads basally, activates ion channels. Pilocarpine-induced salivation is severely impaired in IP3R2/IP3R3 double-KO mice (die within 4 weeks without wet mash). ER Ca2+ release is transient without extracellular Ca2+; store-operated Ca2+ entry (SOCE) sustains it: STIM1 (ER Ca2+ sensor) forms SOCE with Orai channels or TRPC channels. Orai1 generates a highly Ca2+-sensitive inwardly rectifying current when activated by STIM1. TRPC1 is essential — its loss attenuates store-operated Ca2+ current and fluid secretion. Increased [Ca2+]i activates K+ and Cl- channels; transepithelial Cl- transport + Na+ flux through tight junctions accumulates NaCl in the lumen; water follows through AQP5 in the apical membrane via osmotic gradient. Resting [Ca2+]i ≈ 50–100 nM.
Aquaporins (AQPs): In human salivary glands AQP1 in vascular endothelium/myoepithelial cells; AQP3, AQP4, AQP5 in acinar cells. AQP5 is predominantly apical in acinar cells, absent in mature ducts, and key for saliva secretion — M1/M3 mAChR activation raises [Ca2+]i inducing AQP5 trafficking to the apical membrane. Parasympathectomy decreases salivary AQP5 protein (post-transcriptional degradation) without affecting mRNA. AQP5-KO mice show ~60% reduction in pilocarpine-induced saliva secretion.

4. Increasing Prevalence of Xerostomia
~550,000 head-and-neck radiation patients/year and >4 million Sjögren's patients worldwide suffer salivary gland dysfunction. >400 drugs (antidepressants, antipsychotics, antihistamines, antihypertensives) are major causes; aging + polypharmacy increase incidence. FDA-approved systemic sialagogues: muscarinic agonists pilocarpine and cevimeline (mimic parasympathetic action) — but broad mAChR expression causes side effects (nausea, diarrhea, sweating, bronchoconstriction, hypotension, bradycardia).
4.1 Xerogenic drugs are the most common cause: antidepressants, antiemetics, antihistamines, antihypertensives, antipsychotics, appetite suppressants, anxiolytics, bronchodilators, cardiovascular agents, muscle relaxants. Tricyclic antidepressants block histaminergic, cholinergic, α1-adrenergic sites. Anticholinergic action contributes significantly; risk increases with total number of medications; usually reversible but underlying conditions are chronic. Psychiatric drugs are the strongest explanatory factor.
4.2 Systemic diseases: Sjögren's syndrome (chronic autoimmune) causes severe hyposalivation — inflammatory infiltrate, cytokines, acinar destruction; NF-κB and innate immune pathways dysregulated. Radiation therapy destroys saliva-producing acini. Salivary gland tumors (<5% of head-and-neck cancers) and their surgery reduce function. Aging: mean acinar volume decreases ~30% in SM, ~25% in SL, ~12% in PAR glands; TUNEL-positive apoptotic cells increase with age.
4.3 Palliative care: systemic sialagogues (pilocarpine — nonselective muscarinic; cevimeline — higher M1/M3 affinity); radiation patients respond minimally. Saliva substitutes/artificial saliva (xanthan/guar gums, carboxymethyl/hydroxyethyl cellulose, glycerol, mucins) mimic rheology but lack antibacterial properties. Mouth rinses give short-term relief (up to 4 h) without solving the underlying problem.

5. Conclusions
Elucidating mechanisms of salivary secretion loss and functional rescue should be a major research focus. Acinar cells are polarized and drive unidirectional fluid movement. Saliva secretion is precisely regulated by GPCR-mediated intracellular Ca2+ signaling. Key components — GPCR, IP3R, SOCE, AQP5 — can all be targets of medication or disease. Acinar cell-specific GPCR agonists (mAChR, P2Y2R agonists) are potential therapeutic candidates.

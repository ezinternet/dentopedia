To view a copy of this licence, visit http://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/ . ## ABSTRACT ## Introduction Drilling an implant osteotomy involves bone removal using specific drill designs, under irrigation, and at controlled speeds and forces. The interaction among these parameters generates heat that may impair bone viability, potentially resulting in fibrous encapsulation and osseointegration failure. This study aimed to evaluate the amount of heat produced during osteotomy preparation using a single-drill protocol compared with conventional sequential drilling in artificial bone blocks of varying densities. ## Material and methods A total of 240 osteotomies were prepared in polyurethane blocks simulating D1–D4 bone types. For each density, 30 osteotomies were created using a single drill (4 mm × 15 mm) and 30 using conventional sequential drills, totalling 120 per protocol. A K-type thermocouple was positioned 1 mm from the final osteotomy wall to record bone temperature immediately after drilling. Data were analysed using a two-way ANOVA to assess the effects of bone density and drill type on temperature rise, with normality and homogeneity verified using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests. ## Results In D1 and D2 bone types, single drills produced lower mean temperatures (25.2 ± 0.65 °C; 22.9 ± 0.56 °C) than conventional drills (26.3 ± 0.85 °C; 24.2 ± 0.44 °C). Conversely, in D3 and D4 bone types, single drills generated slightly higher temperatures (24.7 ± 0.47 °C; 24.5 ± 0.72 °C) than conventional drilling (23.5 ± 0.65 °C; 23.4 ± 0.79 °C). ANOVA revealed a significant effect of bone density (F = 129.62, p &lt; 0.001) and a density–drill interaction (F = 69.03, p &lt; 0.001), but no main effect of drill type (p = 0.776). ## Conclusion Within the limitations of this study, single-drill osteotomy generated less heat in dense bone (D1–D2) but more in softer bone (D3–D4), suggesting that bone density modulates the thermal response during implant site preparation. ## Keywords In vitro study Dental procedure Oral surgery Dental implant Osteotomy Bone density Single drill pmc-status-qastatus 0 pmc-status-live yes pmc-status-embargo no pmc-status-released yes pmc-prop-open-access yes pmc-prop-olf no pmc-prop-manuscript no pmc-prop-legally-suppressed no pmc-prop-has-pdf yes pmc-prop-has-supplement no pmc-prop-pdf-only no pmc-prop-suppress-copyright no pmc-prop-is-real-version no pmc-prop-is-scanned-article no pmc-prop-preprint no pmc-prop-in-epmc yes pmc-license-ref CC BY-NC-ND issue-copyright-statement © BioMed Central Ltd., part of Springer Nature 2026 ## Intorduction Drilling a dental implant osteotomy is a purely mechanical procedure that entails the removal of bone using drill bits of specific designs, under continuous irrigation with water or saline coolant, at defined speeds and with controlled shear forces. The interaction of these factors leads to heat generation, which can adversely affect bone viability, resulting in fibrous encapsulation and failure of osseointegration [ 1 , 2 ]. Various methods for osteotomy preparation exist. Some rely on mechanical bone removal, such as conventional sequential drilling, piezoelectric surgery, and osteotome techniques, whereas others create the osteotomy through lateral condensation of soft bone, as in osseodensification [ 3 ]. However, there is no unanimous agreement on which of these methods is the most effective [ 4 ]. Another factor to consider is the drill material. Whether metallic, ceramic, coated, or uncoated, worn or reused drills tend to generate more heat in the deeper parts of the osteotomy owing to increased friction and reduced coolant access [ 5 , 6 ]. A recently introduced technique for osteotomy preparation advocates the use of a single drill, claiming that it does not generate more heat than conventional sequential drilling with successively larger drills [ 7 ]. Despite using the same mechanical cutting principles, single drilling remains subject to thermal generation [ 8 ]. Nevertheless, several studies have reported that single drilling is a safe procedure that produces heat levels comparable to conventional sequential drilling but requires fewer drilling steps [ 9 – 11 ]. Furthermore, it may offer more accurate implant placement when used with surgical guides [ 12 ] and possibly generate less heat when operated at low speeds not exceeding 300 revolutions per minute [ 13 ]. Moreover, several in vitro, in vivo, and clinical studies have demonstrated that implants placed in osteotomies prepared using single drilling exhibited no statistically significant differences compared with those prepared conventionally, in terms of initial stability, removal torque, or bone healing assessed histomorphometrically [ 14 – 17 ]. Additional research has favoured the single-drill technique for immediate implant placement in extraction sockets or in low-density bone, reporting that it provides a faster and more predictable surgical approach with satisfactory primary stability and improved bone healing, owing to reduced heat exposure—since temperatures exceeding 47 °C for one minute can cause irreversible bone necrosis [ 2 , 18 – 20 ]. Based on the literature reviewed, the present study aimed to evaluate the heat generated during osteotomy preparation using the single-drill technique compared with conventional sequential drilling in artificial bone blocks of varying densities. ## Materials and methods A total of 240 dental implant osteotomies were drilled in four artificial bone blocks made of polyurethane (SAWBONES, USA), an established in vitro bone analogue [ 7 , 17 ], with varying densities representing D1, D2, D3, and D4 (Fig. 1 ). According to the SAWBONES classification, D1 corresponds to the hardest and D4 to the softest polyurethane density, with specific densities of D1 = 40 PCF, D2 = 30 PCF, D3 = 20 PCF, and D4 = 10 PCF, as provided by the manufacturer. Fig. 1 The 4 blocks of artificial bone used One hundred and twenty osteotomies were drilled using a single-drill protocol (diameter = 3.5 mm for osteotomy preparation of a 4.1 mm implant; DRILLT35S, Blue Sky Bio LLC, USA), divided into four subgroups: 30 osteotomies in D1, 30 in D2, 30 in D3, and 30 in D4 blocks. Similarly, 120 osteotomies were drilled using the conventional sequential drilling protocol (Straumann Implant Set Surgery Cassette, Straumann, Switzerland), employing a sequence of 2.2 mm, 2.8 mm, and 3.5 mm drills. The single drill used was 15 mm in length and 3.5 mm in diameter, matching the dimensions of the final drill in the sequential group, and representing the average intra-alveolar implant length used in posterior mandible studies [ 10 ]. The handpiece was connected to a step motor (NSK Surgic Pro II, Japan) with a predetermined load of 2 kg, and drilling was performed at a speed of 800 rpm. Two drills were used per subgroup ( n = 30 osteotomies) to minimise wear; each drill was used for 15 osteotomies, consistent with literature recommendations (Chauhan et al., 2). The drilling speed of 800 rpm was selected based on Delgado-Ruiz et al. [ 13 ], who demonstrated optimal cooling and cutting efficiency under irrigated conditions with a water coolant flow rate of 50 mL/min at room temperature (~ 18 °C) for all osteotomies. The initial bone temperature (~ 18 °C) was chosen to simulate a controlled laboratory baseline consistent with previous in vitro protocols [ 7 ]. Temperature was measured immediately after drilling using a K-type thermometer (HT617B, Dual Input Thermometer, HABOTEST Instrument Technology Co., Ltd., China; range: −200.0 °C to + 1372 °C; accuracy ± [0.15% rdg + 1 °C]). The thermocouple was inserted into a 2 mm-diameter and 15 mm-deep cylindrical hole positioned 1 mm from the final osteotomy wall (Fig. 2 ). Simultaneous measurements were avoided to prevent coolant interference and vibration affecting thermocouple stability. Immediate post-drilling readings were used, consistent with El-Kholey et al. [ 11 ]. The thermocouple site was shielded with a thin PTFE barrier to prevent direct water contact while maintaining thermal conduction. Coolant irrigation was maintained throughout drilling to replicate clinical conditions, as removal of irrigation could produce non-clinical temperature peaks and confound comparisons. The drilling sequence was randomised by alternating osteotomies among the four bone densities to prevent localised heat accumulation. Statistical analyses were performed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to evaluate the effects of bone density (D1–D4) and drill type (single or conventional sequential) on heat generation during implant osteotomy preparation. The assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were verified using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. The two-way ANOVA assessed the main effects of bone density and drill type, as well as their interaction, at a significance level of p &lt; 0.05. Effect sizes were reported using partial eta-squared (η²). Post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s HSD test. All statistical analyses and plots were generated using the R programming language. ## Results The mean temperatures for each combination of bone density and drill type are presented in Table 1 ; Fig. 3 . For the single-drill protocol, pairwise comparisons revealed statistically significant differences between most density pairs, with two exceptions. The first exception, between D1 and D3 (mean difference = 0.44 °C), suggests that the heat generated in these two bone densities was comparable when using a single drill. The second exception, between D3 and D4 (mean difference = 0.26 °C), indicates that heat generation did not differ significantly between these densities with the single-drill approach. For the conventional sequential drilling, pairwise comparisons also demonstrated that bone density significantly influenced temperature rise across most pairs, except for D3 and D4 (mean difference = 0.12 °C), where no statistically significant difference was observed when using the conventional protocol. ## Discussion This study aimed to evaluate the heat generated during dental implant osteotomy preparation using a single drill compared with conventional sequential drilling in artificial bone blocks of different densities. The results demonstrated a significant interaction between bone density and drill type in terms of heat generation. Specifically, single drills produced less heat than conventional sequential drilling in denser bones (D1 and D2), but more heat in less dense bones (D3 and D4). These findings underscore the importance of considering both bone density and drill type when assessing thermal effects during implant site preparation. These results align with previous research investigating heat generation during osteotomy preparation. In a review by Mishra and Chowdhary [ 1 ], heat generation during drilling was described as multifactorial, with drilling speeds up to 2500 rpm and loads of 2–2.4 kg being considered optimal for osteotomy preparation without considerable thermal increase. Chauhan et al. [ 2 ] reported that temperatures above 47 °C may cause osteonecrosis and that both cutting speed and applied load can independently elevate bone temperature. However, when both factors are increased simultaneously under sufficient irrigation, cutting efficiency improves without a corresponding temperature rise—highlighting the critical role of coolant use in controlling thermal accumulation. Moreover, Chauhan et al. [ 2 ] observed no significant difference between single-drill and conventional sequential techniques, provided that drills are not reused beyond approximately 40 cycles to prevent deformation and loss of cutting efficiency. Consistent with the present findings, Gehrke et al. [ 7 ] reported that in D1 synthetic polyurethane foam, single drilling under external irrigation produced mean temperatures of 20.2 ± 0.83 °C, not differing significantly from conventional sequential drilling. Similarly, Koutiech et al. [ 17 ] demonstrated that single drilling in D1 artificial bone at 1500 rpm, under comparable coolant conditions but at a higher ambient temperature than in the current study, generated less heat than the conventional protocol. Comparable results were observed in natural bone models. Gehrke et al. [ 14 ] reported that in rabbit tibiae, single-drill implant placement resulted in no statistically significant differences in primary stability, removal torque, or histomorphometric outcomes compared with sequential drilling. El-Kholey and Elkomy [ 10 ] similarly found in bovine ribs that mean heat generation using a single drill (2.55 °C) did not significantly differ from that of conventional drilling. In a subsequent study, El-Kholey et al. [ 11 ] repeated the experiment with three different implant diameters and obtained consistent findings. Rugova and Abboud [ 20 ] also reported a significant reduction in peak temperature and duration of heat exposure with the single-drill protocol compared with the conventional approach. This reduction could be attributed to the findings of Gehrke et al. [ 15 ], who suggested that the wider flute spacing of single drills enhances irrigation and debris evacuation. Strbac et al. [ 8 ] further demonstrated that most temperature rise during osteotomy drilling occurs during drill withdrawal—a process inherent to sequential drilling but less pronounced in single-drill protocols. Moreover, finite element and mathematical modelling by Vaidya et al. [ 19 ] indicated that single drills of comparable design to that used in the present study appear both safe and efficient under tested loading conditions, showing lower susceptibility to blunting as demonstrated by von Mises stress distribution and principal stress analysis. Clinical investigations by Guazzi et al. [ 9 ], Marheineke et al. [ 12 ], and Senada et al. [ 16 ] reported high clinical success rates for implants placed using the single-drill protocol compared with those placed using conventional sequential drilling. The single-drill technique was associated with reduced operative time and less invasiveness, as it eliminated the need for multiple drill changes. This, in turn, minimised the risk of surgical contamination and was reported to be more convenient for the surgeon. Taking into account the findings of these studies, the present research confirmed that bone density has a significant effect on heat generation during osteotomy preparation. Denser bone types (D1) generally produced higher temperatures than less dense types (D2–D4), likely due to their higher mineral content and greater resistance to cutting. Under such conditions, the single-drill protocol generated less heat than the multi-step sequential approach, possibly because the shorter drilling time outweighed the frictional load. Conversely, in lower-density bone (D3 and D4), the lower mineral and higher organic content may have contributed to debris accumulation within the flutes, leading to increased frictional resistance and greater heat generation when using a single drill. This observation supports the findings of Koutiech et al. [ 17 ], who reported that clogging and reduced irrigation efficiency in low-density bone are associated with higher thermal rise when using single drills compared with multiple-step drilling protocols. According to the computational fluid dynamics, finite element, and biomechanical models proposed by Vaidya et al. [ 19 ], the porous architecture of cancellous bone plays a critical role in thermal behaviour during osteotomy. In highly porous D3–D4 bone, the increased void volume tends to trap osseous debris and bone chips rather than allowing them to be efficiently evacuated. This accumulation obstructs chip removal and restricts the flow of coolant through the osteotomy site. Consequently, less effective irrigation and reduced convective cooling occur, leaving the drill–bone interface susceptible to increased frictional resistance. The combination of retained debris and limited coolant penetration amplifies local frictional heating and promotes temperature build-up at the cutting interface. If left uncontrolled, such conditions may elevate interfacial temperatures beyond physiological thresholds, compromising bone cell viability near the osteotomy walls. These observations emphasise the importance of maintaining continuous irrigation and optimised drill geometry when working in low-density bone to prevent thermal injury. The findings of the present study are consistent with those of Sorgato et al. [ 21 ], who demonstrated that temperature rise during implant osteotomy is primarily influenced by friction, drill sharpness, and drilling parameters. They observed that worn drills generate higher temperatures, increasing the risk of thermal damage—an outcome that supports our results regarding the importance of efficient cutting. While their work excluded irrigation to isolate the effect of tool wear, our study simulated real clinical conditions and confirmed that bone density and drill type interact to influence heat generation. Both studies highlight that maintaining sharp drills, adequate cooling, and density-specific drilling adjustments are essential to minimise thermal risk and improve implant outcomes. These findings have important clinical implications for selecting the appropriate drilling approach based on bone density. In denser bone, such as the mandible (represented by D1 and D2 in this study), the single-drill technique may be advantageous, as it generates less heat and requires shorter surgical time, potentially enhancing implant stability and osseointegration. Conversely, in less dense bone, such as the maxilla (represented by D3 and D4), the conventional sequential drilling protocol may be preferable to minimise heat generation and preserve bone vitality. Clinicians should ensure an adequate cooling system and efficient irrigation to prevent clogging and excessive temperature rise, thereby improving the overall success rate of dental implants. Despite the valuable insights provided, this study has several limitations that warrant consideration. Real-time thermography was not employed; thus, the recorded temperatures reflect post-drilling values rather than instantaneous peaks, which may have dissipated before measurement. Additionally, polyurethane blocks lack vascularisation and differ in thermal conductivity from human bone, potentially underestimating in vivo temperature peaks. The use of artificial bone also does not replicate the biological variability of natural bone. Furthermore, the experimental design excluded patient-specific factors, such as systemic diseases that may influence bone density. Future research should include in vivo and clinical studies to validate these findings under physiological conditions. Investigations into the long-term effects of drill type on implant success and osseointegration would further strengthen evidence-based practice. Moreover, studies exploring different drill designs and geometries are recommended to optimise cutting efficiency and minimise thermal generation during implant osteotomy preparation. ## Conclusion Taking into account the limitations of the present study, it can be concluded that the effect of drill type on temperature is not uniform across all bone densities but is moderated by bone density. Single drills generated less heat than conventional sequential drilling in denser bone types (D1 and D2), whereas they produced higher temperatures in less dense bone (D3 and D4). These results indicate that bone density plays a crucial role in modulating thermal response during implant osteotomy preparation. Further in vivo animal and clinical studies are warranted to validate these thermal patterns under physiological conditions and to confirm their clinical relevance in natural bone.

## Three-line Summary

This 2010 narrative review in Internal and Emergency Medicine argues that halitosis affects 50–65% of the population and that up to 15% of cases arise from extra-oral or systemic sources — including gastrointestinal, respiratory, hepatic, and metabolic disease — rather than the oral cavity alone.

The paper introduces blood-borne halitosis (systemic malodor delivered via circulation to the lungs), pseudo-halitosis (perceived but absent odor), and halitophobia (exaggerated fear) as clinically distinct entities requiring differentiated management.

A practical referral framework is proposed: oral-cause halitosis → dentist; extra-oral or systemic cause → treatment of the underlying condition.

## Summary

Published in Internal and Emergency Medicine, Campisi et al. review halitosis from the primary care clinician's perspective. Despite affecting a majority of adults, halitosis is often taboo and undertreated. While 90% of halitosis cases originate in the oral cavity (periodontal disease, tongue coating, xerostomia), the authors emphasize that up to 15% involve extra-oral etiologies — frequently related to systemic disease — which are overlooked when management focuses exclusively on oral hygiene.

A key conceptual contribution is the category of **blood-borne halitosis**: malodorous volatile compounds produced by systemic metabolic processes (e.g., hepatic, renal, or gastrointestinal disease) enter the circulation, are exhaled via the lungs, and cannot be resolved by oral interventions alone. Early halitosis may therefore serve as a diagnostic signal for occult systemic disease.

The review also distinguishes two psychogenic variants: **pseudo-halitosis** (the patient believes malodor exists but none is measurable) and **halitophobia** (exaggerated, persistent fear of having halitosis despite reassurance). These require psychological rather than dental or medical management.

## Key Contributions

- **Prevalence benchmark**: 50–65% of population affected; raises awareness among non-dental primary care clinicians.
- **Extra-oral etiologies**: Systematic listing of systemic diseases causally associated with halitosis (gastrointestinal, hepatic, renal, respiratory, metabolic).
- **Blood-borne halitosis concept**: Mechanistically explains non-oral malodor delivered via pulmonary exhalation.
- **Psychogenic halitosis taxonomy**: Defines and separates pseudo-halitosis and halitophobia from genuine physiological halitosis.
- **Referral algorithm**: Actionable decision pathway for primary care.

## Methodology

Narrative review; literature synthesis targeting internists and emergency medicine physicians. Emphasis on systemic and psychogenic etiologies underrepresented in dental literature. Abstract-only; full search databases and inclusion criteria not available.

## Results

- Halitosis prevalence: 50–65% of population.
- Oral causes: ~90% of cases.
- Extra-oral causes (alone or combined with oral): up to 15% of cases.
- Systemic disease associations: gastrointestinal, hepatic, renal, respiratory, and metabolic conditions implicated.
- Halitosis may be an early clinical sign of serious underlying disease.
- Pseudo-halitosis and halitophobia require distinct management from physiological halitosis.

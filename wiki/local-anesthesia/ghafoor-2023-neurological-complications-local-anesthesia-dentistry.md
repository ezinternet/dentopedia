## Three-line Summary

Narrative (non-systematic) review of neurological complications following dental local anesthesia, organized by nerve — ophthalmic (CN V1), maxillary (CN V2), mandibular (CN V3), and facial (CN VII).

Documents ocular complications (diplopia, ptosis, transient/permanent blindness) via inadvertent intravascular spread through the middle meningeal → ophthalmic artery pathway, mandibular-block paresthesia (7.3× risk with 4% articaine per Garisto et al.), and facial nerve palsy (Bell's palsy) as the most frequent neurological complication after IANB, with immediate (parotid-gland anatomic) vs delayed (sympathetic vasospasm/viral reactivation) mechanisms.

Cureus narrative review with no systematic search strategy or PRISMA methodology — findings are mechanism/case-report-level awareness points, not incidence estimates suitable for informed-consent quantification.

## Summary

This Cureus narrative review fills a specific gap in the wiki's local-anesthesia cluster: while [[local-anesthesia/becker-2012-local-anesthetics-pharmacological-considerations]] anchors the pharmacology/toxicity side and several RCTs cover technique/efficacy, no existing page maps the **neurological complication pathways** of intraoral injection nerve-by-nerve — most notably **안면신경 (Facial Nerve, CN VII) 마비 (Facial Palsy)** and **안구 합병증 (Ocular Complications)** (복시 Diplopia, 안검하수 Ptosis, 실명 Blindness), which are rare but clinically alarming events dentists should recognize and be able to counsel patients about.

The paper organizes complications anatomically along the trigeminal nerve's three divisions plus the facial nerve, and proposes vascular/anatomic mechanisms for why an intraoral mandibular-region injection can produce effects far outside the mouth — in the orbit or on the contralateral or ipsilateral face. As a non-systematic Cureus review with no PRISMA methodology, its numeric estimates should be read as illustrative case-report-derived figures, not validated population incidence.

## Key Contributions

- Nerve-by-nerve complication map (ophthalmic, maxillary, mandibular, facial) rather than symptom-first organization — useful as a differential-diagnosis reference when an unexpected neurological sign appears after dental LA.
- Proposed vascular mechanism for ocular complications: inadvertent intravascular injection into the inferior alveolar artery → retrograde flow into the maxillary artery → middle meningeal artery (MMA) → anastomosis into the ophthalmic artery's territory (lacrimal artery / central retinal artery, CRA) → diplopia, ophthalmoplegia, or blindness. Alternative venous route: LA drains into the pterygoid plexus → cavernous sinus → compresses the abducens (CN VI) or oculomotor (CN III) nerve as they traverse the sinus.
- Immediate vs delayed facial nerve palsy (FNP) after IANB, each with distinct proposed mechanisms — the first clear structuring of this distinction available in the wiki.
- Paresthesia risk stratification by anesthetic concentration, corroborating the 4%-agent (articaine/prilocaine) risk signal already established in [[local-anesthesia/becker-2012-local-anesthetics-pharmacological-considerations]].

## Methodology

Narrative literature review; no PICO framework, systematic database search strategy, PRISMA flow diagram, or risk-of-bias assessment reported. Synthesizes older case reports/case series and narrative sources (Hillerup, Garisto, Rishiraj, Penarrocha-Diago, Ngeow, De Keyzer, and others) organized by cranial-nerve anatomy. Includes anatomic figures of trigeminal/facial nerve distribution and two summary tables (direct ocular effects; periorbital effects). **Evidence weight is low** — this is a narrative synthesis of predominantly case-report-level evidence, appropriate for mechanism/awareness education but not for quantifying informed-consent risk.

## Results

### Ophthalmic nerve (CN V1) complications

Associated with IAN block and posterior superior alveolar nerve (PSAN) block; ocular complications after middle/PSAN blocks reported roughly **twice as frequently** as after IANB over the last 45 years of literature.

- **Diplopia (복시)**: most common mechanism proposed is intravascular injection → retrograde flow through the MMA → anastomosis with the ophthalmic artery's lacrimal-artery territory (which partly supplies the lateral rectus muscle) → lateral rectus paralysis. Alternative: venous drainage into the cavernous sinus compressing the abducens nerve.
- **Paralysis of the eye**: venous backflow into the cavernous sinus can compress the oculomotor nerve (CN III) at the sinus roof near the dorsum sellae.
- **Blindness (실명, amaurosis)**: anesthetic reaching the central retinal artery (CRA) via the MMA → ophthalmic artery route can cause retinal ischemia/vasospasm and permanent blindness. Two case reports (De Keyzer et al.; Rishiraj et al.) describe permanent unilateral blindness after intraoral LA; one involved a patient with bacterial endocarditis who lost vision in one eye after extraction prior to mitral valve surgery — the authors suggest aspiration before/during injection could have prevented intravascular injection and the resulting fluid embolus.
- **Ptosis, strabismus, loss of accommodation**: from oculomotor nerve paralysis (paralyzing the ciliary muscle, internal rectus, sphincter pupillae); partial presentations include internal strabismus (internal rectus spasm), near-vision-only accommodation (ciliary muscle spasm), and miosis (sphincter irritation). Some presentations resemble Horner's syndrome (ptosis, conjunctival vascular dilation, miosis, facial/neck/shoulder rash), proposed to arise from inadvertent LA injection into the stellate ganglion.
- Overall ocular unfavorable-outcome prevalence after dental LA: **0.07%-0.09%**.

### Maxillary nerve (CN V2) complications

Maxillary nerve block (MNB) complications include:

- **Paresthesia (감각이상)**: relatively rare; from nerve trauma or local hematoma compression; recovery typically **6-12 months**.
- **Facial nerve paralysis**: infrequent, true prevalence unknown; most immediate-onset cases recover within ~7 hours.
- **Trismus, soft tissue lesions, edema, facial blanching, hematoma, infection**: each discussed with standard mechanisms (needle trauma, hematoma compression, sympathetic vasospasm, low local pH impairing anesthetic ionization in infected tissue producing "failed block").

### Mandibular nerve (CN V3) complications

The mandibular nerve block (MNB, IANB) is described as the most-used and clinically most significant block in dentistry.

- **Hypersensitivity/allergy**: type I (IgE) and type IV (T-cell) reactions; overall adverse-outcome prevalence 0.1-1%, with true allergy representing <1% of these.
- **Toxicity/overdose**: biphasic CNS reaction (early: dizziness, confusion, anxiety; progressing to drowsiness, tinnitus, diplopia, circumoral numbness, muscle twitching, tremor, seizure, respiratory arrest) and biphasic cardiovascular reaction (initial stimulation, then vasodilation/myocardial depression, bradycardia, cardiac arrest at high blood levels). Prevention: aspiration, slow injection, weight-based max-dose awareness, patient monitoring during onset.
- **Paresthesia**: prolonged anesthesia beyond expected duration from needle trauma; patients reporting an initial electric-shock sensation during injection may be at higher risk of developing persistent anesthesia. **More frequent with 4% solutions (articaine, prilocaine)** than lower-concentration agents — attributed to concentration, clinician preference, and commercial articaine's routine combination with epinephrine (which is not typically added to other common dental LAs, potentially compounding neurotoxicity risk). This mirrors the 4%-agent paresthesia signal already documented in [[local-anesthesia/becker-2012-local-anesthetics-pharmacological-considerations]] (7.3× risk with 4% articaine, 3.6× with 4% prilocaine, per Garisto et al.).
- **Trigeminal neuralgia (TN)**: briefly discussed as a neuralgia complication category — >85% classical TN (vascular compression at the dorsal root entry zone, typically by the superior cerebellar artery), remainder secondary TN (multiple sclerosis or space-occupying lesion). This is a minor tangent relative to the wiki's dedicated [[overviews/trigeminal-neuralgia-neuropathy-overview]] synthesis and [[oral-medicine/khan-2023-trigeminal-neuralgia-therapeutic-approach-sr]].

### Facial nerve (CN VII) complications — the paper's central gap-filling contribution

**Bell's palsy (facial nerve palsy, FNP)** is identified as **the most frequent neurological complication after IANB**.

- **Symptoms**: generalized ipsilateral facial weakness, inability to close the eyelid, obliterated nasolabial fold, drooping mouth corner, mouth deviation toward the unaffected side; possible retroauricular pain and reduced taste. Peripheral (lower-motor-neuron) palsy involves the entire ipsilateral face including the forehead, distinguishing it from central (upper-motor-neuron) palsy which spares the forehead (bilateral cortical innervation).
- **Immediate-onset palsy**: typically resolves within **~3 hours**. Proposed mechanisms are anatomic — facial nerve trunk variation running along the parotid gland's deep surface, congenital failure of the gland to fully envelop the nerve, or the parotid capsule trapping LA at high local concentration against facial nerve branches after inadvertent deposition into gland substance.
- **Delayed-onset palsy**: onset hours to days after injection. Three proposed mechanisms: (1) LA or its breakdown products stimulate the external-carotid-artery sympathetic plexus, which travels with the stylomastoid artery through the parotid gland, triggering a delayed reflex vasospasm of the facial nerve's vasa nervorum → ischemic neuritis and edema; (2) needle mechanical stimulation of the same sympathetic plexus; (3) latent viral reactivation (herpes zoster/simplex) from procedural trauma causing neural sheath inflammation.
- A case of **permanent** nerve injury combining hearing loss, facial numbness, ataxia, and facial palsy (all ipsilateral to the injection) is cited, though the mechanism is not further specified.
- **Transient amaurosis** (temporary blindness) is separately flagged as a more alarming complication reported after an inferior dental (alveolar) block.

### Aggregate incidence figures cited

- Peripheral nerve block neurological complications generally **<3%**, mostly transient sensory loss; persistent injury **0.02%-0.5%**.
- Permanent nerve injury from mandibular LA: **0.000007%-0.003%**.
- Hillerup et al. (54 cases of CN V injury after IANB): lingual nerve **77.8%**, IAN **22.2%** of cases.
- Garisto et al.: lingual/IAN paresthesia split **89%/11%**.
- Needle fracture prevalence: **0.000007%** (Pogrel, 25-year retrospective).

## Three-line Summary

Foundational pharmacology review (Dent Clin North Am 2010;54(4):587–99) of local anesthetic agents and formulations used in dentistry by Moore PA and Hersh EV — the most-cited authors in dental anesthesiology — covering clinical pharmacology of onset, duration, vasoconstrictors, pH, lipid solubility, maximum tolerated doses, and lipid metabolism for lidocaine, mepivacaine, articaine, bupivacaine, and prilocaine.

Provides the clinical pharmacology framework for understanding toxicity risks: lipid solubility correlates with both anesthetic potency and toxicity potency; protein binding determines duration and affects ILE rescue efficacy; vasoconstrictors reduce systemic absorption by vasoconstriction, extending both duration and safety margin; pH affects onset speed (un-ionized form crosses nerve membrane).

Abstract-only; standard reference text that underpins the clinical decision-making in Gazal 2026, the mechanism section in Nendumba 2025, and the dose calculation guidance in Saraghi 2015.

## Core Pharmacology Framework

### Agent Classification

**Amide local anesthetics** (dental standard):
- Lidocaine, mepivacaine, articaine, bupivacaine, prilocaine, ropivacaine, levobupivacaine
- Metabolized primarily by hepatic CYP450 (exception: articaine 85% plasma esterases)
- Allergic reactions rare (<1%)

**Ester local anesthetics**:
- Procaine, chloroprocaine, benzocaine (topical), tetracaine (topical)
- Metabolized by plasma cholinesterase → PABA metabolite (allergenic)
- Shorter half-life; used when amide allergy confirmed

### Key Pharmacokinetic Parameters

| Agent | Lipophilicity (log P) | Protein binding | Onset | Duration (w/ epi) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lidocaine | 2.44 | 65% | Rapid (2–3 min) | 2–3 h |
| Mepivacaine | 1.95 | 75% | Rapid | 2–3 h |
| Articaine | 1.8 | 54% | Rapid (1.5 min) | 1–2 h |
| Prilocaine | 2.1 | 55% | Moderate | 2–3 h |
| Bupivacaine | 3.41 | **95%** | Slow (5 min) | 4–8 h |
| Ropivacaine | 2.9 | 94% | Moderate | 3–6 h |

### Toxicity Determinants

**Lipophilicity** → membrane penetration potency:
- Higher log P = enters nerve membrane and cardiac membranes more readily
- Higher log P = more efficient ILE rescue (larger lipid sink partition)
- Bupivacaine (3.41) is the most toxic per mg; mepivacaine (1.95) the least

**Protein binding** → duration + cardiac binding:
- Bupivacaine 95% protein-bound: dissociates slowly from Na+ channels ("fast-in, slow-out")
- This slow dissociation makes bupivacaine cardiac arrest refractory to standard ACLS
- Lidocaine 65%: "fast-in, fast-out" — more reversible cardiac binding

**pH and pKa**:
- LA exists as ionized (R-NH₂⁺) and un-ionized (R-NH) forms in equilibrium
- **Un-ionized form** crosses the lipid nerve membrane; ionized form binds Na+ channel from inside
- Low pH (inflamed tissue): shifts equilibrium toward ionized → slower onset, reduced efficacy
- Buffering LA (adding bicarbonate): raises pH → more un-ionized → faster onset

### Vasoconstrictors

**Epinephrine (adrenaline)**:
- Standard: 1:100,000 (10 μg/mL) or 1:200,000 (5 μg/mL)
- Mechanism: α1-adrenergic vasoconstriction at injection site
- Reduces vascular absorption → lower peak plasma LA level → extended duration + reduced toxicity
- Cardiac safety limit: ≤0.04 mg total in cardiovascular patients (~2 cartridges 1:100,000)

**Felypressin** (octapressin):
- Vasopressin analogue; used with prilocaine in some European formulations
- Less adrenergic effect → preferred in some cardiac patients
- Uterotonic activity → contraindicated in pregnancy

## Clinical Insights

This review is the pharmacological foundation that makes all other entries in this surveillance cluster coherent:

1. **Why bupivacaine LAST is refractory**: 95% protein binding + high log P = slow Na+ channel dissociation = standard ACLS fails; ILE (lipid sink) is mechanistically necessary
2. **Why articaine's plasma metabolism matters**: 85–90% plasma esterase hydrolysis means hepatic load is minimal; relevant for Gazal 2026's recommendation in hepatic impairment
3. **Why mepivacaine plain is a pediatric risk**: no vasoconstrictor = faster absorption + 50% higher concentration (3%) = faster peak plasma level; small children hit MRD in 2.4 carpules
4. **Why pH explains failed blocks in inflamed teeth**: infection creates local acidosis; even perfect injection technique has reduced efficacy; strategies: buccal + palatal infiltration, IANB + supplemental, buffered lidocaine

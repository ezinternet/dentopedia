## Three-line Summary

Systematic review (PRISMA-Cochrane; 6 studies, n=122, mean age 22–35 years) evaluating masticatory muscle activity, bite force, and TMD signs during clear aligner therapy.

Clear aligners caused short-term increase in muscle activity & pain at treatment onset, which reverted to baseline at follow-up; bite force temporarily reduced but normalized; no significant TMD sign/symptom increase.

Findings support patient education that transient muscle soreness and altered bite force are expected adaptive responses during CAT, not pathological sequelae.

## Summary

This PRISMA-compliant systematic review synthesizes evidence on clear aligner therapy (CAT) effects on masticatory muscles, bite force, and temporomandibular joint function across 6 clinical studies (total n≈122; age 22–35 years; 6 regions: China, Italy, Brazil, New Zealand, Canada). The review used rigorous Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 methodology and found all 6 studies demonstrated low risk of bias.

**Key findings:**

1. **Transient muscle activity elevation:** Clear pattern of increased surface electromyography (sEMG) activity in masseter and temporalis muscles at treatment onset (T0→T1, first week–first month), with adaptive normalization by follow-up (T2, 3–8 months). Patients frequently reported transient muscle soreness and discomfort during the initial fitting phase, resolving within days to weeks.

2. **Bite force reduction is temporary:** Maximal voluntary contraction (MVC) and bite force measurements showed ~20% reduction during active aligner wear, attributed to mechanical interposition of polyurethane material and altered dental contact efficiency. Bite force normalized post-treatment, supporting the hypothesis that force reduction is a mechanical transient, not persistent neuromuscular dysfunction.

3. **No significant TMD risk:** The review did not identify increased incidence of temporomandibular disorder (TMD) signs or symptoms with CAT in healthy patients. Pain noted during treatment was transient and attributed to orthodontic force adaptation, not condylar pathology. Studies evaluating pre-existing TMD did not show exacerbation, though heterogeneous TMD diagnostic criteria limited definitive conclusions.

4. **Pain & discomfort patterns:** Significant increase in pain reports at T1 (47.5–100% of participants reported mild-to-moderate tooth pain and muscle soreness), which correlated with aligner insertion and mechanical stimulus rather than anxiety. Pain was weakly linked to parafunctional habits (bruxism, clenching) and psychological stress; pain typically resolved during follow-up phases (T2+), consistent with expected inflammatory response to new appliance.

5. **Parafunctional habit changes:** Oral Behavior Checklist scores decreased (less clenching/bruxism) during aligner wear, suggesting the physical constraint of the appliance temporarily reduces voluntary parafunctional activity. This effect reversed post-treatment.

## Clinical Implications

**Patient Counseling:** Patients should be informed that transient muscle soreness, altered bite force, and initial discomfort are expected adaptive phenomena during the first 1–3 weeks of CAT, not indicators of treatment failure or neuromuscular pathology. Symptoms typically resolve as musculature adapts (by 3–8 weeks post-initiation).

**Contraindications:** Review evidence does not support CAT as contraindicated in patients without pre-existing TMD. Patients with diagnosed TMD should undergo baseline evaluation and be monitored during treatment, but CAT does not appear to precipitate TMD *de novo*.

**Bite Force Management:** Clinicians should counsel patients that reduced chewing efficiency during initial aligner weeks is temporary; recommending softer diet in the first 1–2 weeks may improve comfort and compliance.

**Muscle Adaptation Timeline:** Treatment planning should account for a 3–8 week adaptive period before final assessment of occlusal contacts and efficacy.

---

## Limitations

1. **Small sample sizes and heterogeneity:** Only 6 studies met inclusion criteria; n ranged 10–31 per study. Heterogeneous study designs (longitudinal vs. retrospective), follow-up durations (9 days–8 months), outcome measures (EMG protocols, pain scales, TMD diagnostic criteria), and aligner specifications precluded meta-analysis.

2. **Confounding factors:** Stress, anxiety, caffeine consumption, sleep quality, pre-treatment parafunctional habits, and psychological factors were inconsistently controlled across studies. Pittar et al attempted stratification but limited power.

3. **Short follow-up:** Maximum follow-up was 8 months; insufficient to assess long-term neuromuscular or TMD outcomes (>2 years).

4. **Aligner material heterogeneity:** No standardization of polyurethane formulation, thickness, wear duration, or replacement schedule across studies. Aligner fit quality, material compliance, and force magnitude likely modulate muscle response but were not systematically reported.

5. **Publication bias risk:** Exclusion of non-English publications and grey literature; unpublished negative studies underrepresented.

---

## Future Research Directions

- **Prospective RCTs** with standardized aligner protocols (fixed material, wear duration, replacement schedule) and longer follow-up (12–24 months)
- **Mechanistic studies:** Biomechanical FEA of force vectors, electrophysiology of motor unit recruitment, neuroimaging of motor cortex activation
- **Standardized TMD assessment:** Use of DC/TMD criteria, CBCT condylar positioning, MRI disc status across all sites
- **Biomarkers:** Serum muscle enzymes (CK), myokine profiles (IL-6, TNF-α), muscle fiber-type analysis
- **Patient-centered outcomes:** OHRQoL questionnaires, masticatory efficiency, long-term dietary/comfort preferences

## Three-line Summary

SR+MA (PROSPERO CRD42024584778; PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane; Jan 2014–Aug 2024) pooling 6 retrospective studies (233 adults) on Invisalign's maxillary transverse-expansion predictability — achieved expansion as a percentage of ClinCheck-predicted expansion, by tooth region.

Pooled predictability: canine 72.01% (95% CI 59.67–84.35%, I²=74.7%), 1st premolar 80.73% (73.78–87.68%, I²=43.5%), 2nd premolar 78.74% (70.41–87.08%, I²=57.4%), 1st molar 71.57% (62.41–80.73%, I²=50.2%); an exploratory developed- vs developing-country subgroup showed 77.22% vs 58.53% for the 1st molar (p=.015, only 2 developing-country studies).

Achieved expansion consistently fell short of programmed expansion across all regions (~0.72–0.81 mm achieved per programmed mm), hinting at a role for overcorrection, but the evidence is 6 retrospective studies only, with substantial unexplained heterogeneity and low-certainty (GRADE) evidence specifically for the canine region — the authors do not prescribe a universal overcorrection number.

## Summary

This is a systematic review and meta-analysis (SR+MA, PROSPERO CRD42024584778) evaluating how predictable Invisalign clear-aligner therapy is for maxillary transverse (arch-width) expansion in adults, defined as the percentage of achieved expansion relative to the expansion predicted by Align Technology's ClinCheck software. Across 5 databases and a decade-long search window (Jan 2014–Aug 2024), only 6 studies — all retrospective, all non-randomized, 233 patients total — met inclusion criteria after screening 269 records. Predictability varied meaningfully by tooth region: it was highest in the 1st and 2nd premolar regions and lowest in the canine and 1st molar regions, with the canine region additionally showing substantial, unexplained between-study heterogeneity (I²=74.7%) and the lowest GRADE certainty of any outcome. The clinical bottom line is that ClinCheck systematically over-predicts achieved transverse movement (roughly 20–30% short of the plan in most regions), which supports considering overcorrection in treatment planning — but the authors are explicit that the evidence base (6 small retrospective studies) is too thin to justify a specific numerical overcorrection protocol.

## Key Contributions

- First SR+MA to pool Invisalign maxillary transverse-expansion predictability specifically by anatomical tooth region (canine, 1st/2nd premolar, 1st molar) in adults.
- Demonstrates non-uniform predictability across the arch — premolars most predictable, canine and 1st molar least — rather than a single arch-wide accuracy figure.
- Assigns per-region GRADE certainty (low for canine, high for 1st premolar, moderate for 2nd premolar/1st molar), making explicit that "SR+MA" here does not mean uniformly strong evidence.
- Runs (exploratory) subgroup analyses by country classification, measurement landmark, and measurement software as candidate explanations for heterogeneity, while explicitly cautioning against over-interpreting them given the small number of included studies.

## Methodology

Random-effects (DerSimonian–Laird) meta-analysis of 6 retrospective studies (Houle 2017, Zhao 2017, Zhou 2020, Lione 2021, Bowman 2023, Tien 2023; 233 patients) identified from PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library (Jan 2014–Aug 2024). Outcome: percentage of achieved maxillary transverse expansion (intercanine, 1st/2nd interpremolar, 1st intermolar width) relative to ClinCheck-predicted expansion. Risk of bias via ROBINS-I (2 studies overall low risk, 4 overall moderate risk); certainty of evidence via GRADE. Heterogeneity assessed with Cochran Q/I²; leave-one-out sensitivity analysis; Egger's regression as an exploratory small-study-effect check (no funnel plots, <10 studies per outcome).

## Results

| Region | Pooled predictability (95% CI) | Heterogeneity (I², p) | GRADE certainty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Canine (intercanine) | 72.01% (59.67–84.35%) | 74.7%, p=.001 (substantial) | Low |
| 1st premolar (1st interpremolar) | 80.73% (73.78–87.68%) | 43.5%, p=.115 (low–moderate) | High |
| 2nd premolar (2nd interpremolar) | 78.74% (70.41–87.08%) | 57.4%, p=.039 (moderate) | Moderate |
| 1st molar (1st intermolar) | 71.57% (62.41–80.73%) | 50.2%, p=.074 (moderate) | Moderate |

Subgroup findings (all exploratory, small study counts):
- **Country (1st molar only)**: developed countries 77.22% (70.34–84.10%, I²=0.0%) vs developing countries 58.53% (45.20–71.86%, I²=32.0%); between-subgroup p=.015 — but only 2 studies in the developing-country group.
- **Measurement landmark (1st molar only)**: palatal cusp tip 75% (66–84%), buccal cusp tip 78% (69–87%), central fossa 51% (34–69%); p=.031 overall, not significant (p=.655) between palatal and buccal cusp tip alone once the single central-fossa study is excluded.
- **Geomagic-software subgroup (3 studies)**: canine 82.42% (75.86–88.98%, I²=84.0%), 1st premolar 80.70% (73.61–87.78%, I²=12.3%), 2nd premolar 78.06% (70.61–85.51%, I²=0.0%), 1st molar 75.12% (67.30–82.93%, I²=0.0%); no significant regional difference within this subgroup (p=.528).
- **Publication bias (Egger, exploratory)**: canine only reached nominal significance (t=−2.83, p=.047); premolar/molar regions did not (p=.051–.190) — underpowered with 6 studies per outcome.
- **Clinical translation**: achieved expansion ≈0.72–0.81 mm per programmed mm across regions; individual studies occasionally showed >100% predictability (achieved exceeded planned), so pooled estimates mask real case-level variability.

## Limitations

All 6 included studies are retrospective and non-randomized (ROBINS-I low-to-moderate risk of bias); there is no RCT evidence. Sample sizes are small (20–64 patients per study, 233 total), and individual patient-level data (age, sex, malocclusion severity, attachment design, programmed-expansion magnitude) could not be pooled. Studies used different anatomical landmarks (buccal cusp tip, palatal cusp tip, central fossa) and different measurement software (Geomagic variants, 3-Matic, Viewbox, MeshLab), a likely but only partially quantifiable source of heterogeneity. All subgroup analyses (country, landmark, software) are exploratory and based on very few studies per stratum (as few as 1–3) — the country-based comparison in particular is a study-level, not individual-level, grouping and should not be read as a claim about socioeconomic status or patient compliance. Egger's test for small-study effects had limited power (6 studies per outcome) and funnel plots were not used at all. The canine region's substantial heterogeneity (I²=74.7%) remains unexplained by any subgroup tested, and its GRADE certainty is rated low — this specific estimate should be treated with the most caution of the four. The authors themselves conclude that firm, universal overcorrection recommendations are not supported by this evidence base and call for prospective, multicenter studies with standardized methodology.

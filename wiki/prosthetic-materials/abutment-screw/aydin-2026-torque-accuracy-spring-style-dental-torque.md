## Three-line Summary
In vitro bench study: 9 spring-style mechanical torque-limiting devices (3 each from Implance, Astra Tech, DIO) mounted on their own manufacturer's implant in a resin block and cycled 40 times through mechanical loading plus 134 °C steam sterilization, measured with a calibrated digital torque tester by a single investigator.
Torque accuracy was strongly manufacturer-dependent and drifted with service life: overall median percentage deviation 3.33% (Astra Tech), 11.43% (Implance), 16.67% (DIO), with measurements outside the ±10% tolerance band in 5%, 60%, and 95% of readings respectively (χ²=99.241, p<0.001); experimental cycle affected torque difference in all three brands (p=0.004 / <0.001 / 0.007) with a significant cycle × manufacturer interaction (p<0.001).
Clinically, spring-style design alone does not guarantee tolerance — the specific MTLD–implant-system combination must be verified at the exact torque used for both placement (35 Ncm) and abutment screw tightening (25 Ncm) — but the exceedance is a property of brand and service history, not of spring-style geometry per se; limits are 3 devices per manufacturer, one operator, and mechanical loading confounded with sterilization.

## Summary

This in vitro bench study asked whether a spring-style mechanical torque-limiting device still delivers its dialed torque after a realistic service life, which earlier accuracy work had never tested. Nine new MTLDs — three each from Implance, Astra Tech, and DIO — were mounted on their own manufacturer's implant in a CAD/CAM resin block at simulated crestal bone level and run through 40 identical cycles, each combining 20 mechanical loading repetitions (10 per target torque) with 134 °C steam sterilization, verified by a calibrated digital torque tester (±0.2% full scale) under a single-operator protocol. Accuracy turned out to depend far more on the manufacturer than on the spring-style design: overall median percentage deviation was 3.33% for Astra Tech, 11.43% for Implance, and 16.67% for DIO, and 5%, 60%, and 95% of readings respectively fell outside the ±10% tolerance band (χ²=99.241, p<0.001). Service history mattered independently — experimental cycle significantly affected torque difference for every brand and the cycle × manufacturer interaction was significant (p<0.001), with Astra Tech actually *improving* over cycling (median 2.5 → 1.0 Ncm, ηp²=0.634) while Implance and DIO fluctuated, and no device ever failed the pre-defined zero-return criterion for plastic deformation. The practical message is that the spring-style label confers no accuracy guarantee: the specific MTLD–implant-system pairing must be verified at the exact torque value used, for implant placement (35 Ncm) and abutment screw tightening (25 Ncm) alike, with 3 devices per manufacturer and loading-vs-sterilization confounding as the main limits.

## Key Contributions
- **First service-life read on spring-style MTLDs in this folder**: 40 cycles of loading *plus* sterilization on the same physical devices — prior accuracy work measured new or single-session wrenches, leaving the "how long does it stay trustworthy" question open.
- **Manufacturer identity beats design class**: under one identical protocol, Astra Tech stayed inside ±10% on 95% of readings while DIO missed on 95%, so spring-style construction alone is not a tolerance guarantee.
- **Accuracy is system-specific, not one-size-fits-all**: target torque value significantly changed torque difference for Astra Tech (p<0.001) and DIO (p<0.001) but not Implance (p=0.088) — performance at one clinical torque does not predict performance at another.
- **No plastic deformation over 40 cycles**: the pre-defined exclusion criterion was never met, arguing against spring take-up as the drift mechanism (mechanism itself unproven — no material or microscopic analysis).
- **Unexplained direction reversal**: Astra Tech's error *shrank* with cycling, opposite to wear-induced drift, which the authors flag for future material analysis rather than explaining away.

## Methodology

- **Design**: in vitro repeated-measures bench study (not randomized, not blinded); 9 new spring-style MTLDs, 3 per manufacturer — Implance (AGS Medikal, Türkiye), Astra Tech (Dentsply Sirona, Sweden), DIO (DIO Corp, South Korea) — each uniquely ID'd.
- **Fixture**: manufacturer-specific implants in CAD/CAM resin blocks at simulated crestal bone level (Implance 4.3 × 10 mm; Astra Tech 4.2 × 11 mm; DIO 4.0 × 10 mm), driven with each system's own supplied mount driver/adapter.
- **Targets**: 25 and 35 Ncm (Implance, Astra Tech); 20 and 35 Ncm for DIO (25 Ncm absent from scale). 35 Ncm = placement torque proxy; 25 Ncm = prosthetic abutment screw tightening.
- **Protocol**: ×40 cycles of baseline measurement → mechanical fatigue (10 reps at each target = 20 reps) → post-fatigue measurement → autoclave (134 °C, 2.1 bar, 5 min). Cycle 0 assessed before any in-protocol sterilization. 3 measurements per stage, mean analysed.
- **Measurement**: calibrated Mark-10 Cap Torque Tester (MTT01-100, ±0.2% FS), peak mode, zeroed each set; force applied horizontally via index-finger distal phalanx at a fixed grip point to the target mark; scale read at 90° to limit parallax; operator-made zero reference for wrenches without a factory zero. Single investigator throughout.
- **Outcomes**: torque difference = |T_measured − T_target| (within-manufacturer); percentage deviation = |T_measured − T_target| / T_target × 100 (between-manufacturer); exceedance of the ±10% band (n=60 measurements per manufacturer).
- **Sample size**: G*Power 3.1.9.2, large effect f=0.40 (no preliminary data), 3 groups, 10 repeated measures (5 cycles × 2 stages), r=0.50, α=0.05, 80% power → 9 devices. (PDF prints "α = 0.05%", a typo for α=0.05.)
- **Statistics**: Shapiro-Wilk (non-normal) → aligned rank transform (ART) mixed ANOVA in ARTool, R v4.4.1, device ID as random factor; fixed effects cycle (0/10/20/30/40), stage, target torque within manufacturer; manufacturer × cycle model for percentage deviation; emmeans post hoc with Holm correction; partial η²; ICC(3,1) repeatability; Pearson χ² with Bonferroni-corrected column-proportion Z tests for exceedance. Medians (min–max), p<0.05.

Notable details: Astra Tech's error *improved* with cycling rather than degrading (ηp²=0.634, unexplained); DIO's median difference at 35 Ncm (6–7 Ncm) was far worse than at 20 Ncm (2.7–4 Ncm); manufacturer cross-checks show DIO's own documentation lists 35 Ncm for fixture placement, so even "official" settings can miss; Implance publishes no component-specific torque values, which limits interpretation of its 60% exceedance. Excellent ICCs across all brands confirm the between-manufacturer spread is real device behaviour, not measurement noise.

Marenzi found universal 11–29.3% under-delivery; this study refines that magnitude claim — Astra Tech missed ±10% on only 5% of readings while DIO missed on 95% under one identical protocol — and adds the 40-cycle service-life axis Marenzi never measured. No brand overlap, so the two coexist rather than contradict.
- [[prosthetic-materials/abutment-screw/lee-2025-abutment-screw-design-torque-loss-fatigue]] — complementary downstream question: this study establishes how far the delivered torque can miss its dialed value (up to 16.67% median for DIO), while Lee et al. test what happens to joint preload when a *correctly* tightened screw sees cyclic fatigue (KSIII <25% torque loss vs TSIII 36.5–51.8%). Together they bound both ends of the delivered-torque error budget.
- Fayaz et al. 2014 (Dental Res J 11(1):74–80; `sources/fayaz-2014-*`, ingested concurrently) — friction-style MTLDs stayed within ±10% after the first sterilization cycle, then became more variable. Read as coexistence-plus-refinement, not contradiction: design class differs (friction vs spring) and Fayaz's protocol carried no mechanical fatigue, while this study's own loading/sterilization confound means the two cannot be pooled into one device-longevity rule.
- Mahshid et al. 2012/2013 — pre-sterilization spring-style MTLDs within ±10% of target; this study is the longer-horizon counterpart (40 cycles *with* loading), and the 3.33–16.67% median spread is consistent with accuracy degrading only for some brands.
- Shiba et al. 2021 — viewing angle affects beam-type wrench accuracy; underwrites the 90° scale-read protocol used here as an operator-variability control.
- Cehreli et al. 2004 / McCracken et al. 2010 — clinical-use drift and spring-vs-toggle variability; both consistent with this study's brand-dependent, non-uniform drift rather than a universal degradation law.

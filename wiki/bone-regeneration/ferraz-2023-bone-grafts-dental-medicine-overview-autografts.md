## Three-line Summary
Narrative review of bone graft materials in dentistry and oral/maxillofacial surgery, comparing autografts, allografts, xenografts, and synthetic substitutes across defect size, handling, and biological performance.
Autografts remain the gold standard (osteogenic, osteoinductive, osteoconductive) but carry donor-site morbidity and supply limits; xenografts (e.g., Bio-Oss, bovine hydroxyapatite, Ca/P 1.67) are the most used by dentists, while synthetic ceramics/calcium-phosphate cements/bioactive glasses offer availability and moldability but lack osteoinductivity in critical-size (>5 mm) defects.
Best new-bone results came from scaffolds incorporating mesenchymal cells and growth factors; the authors stress that cross-study comparison is difficult and that >30-year-documented xenografts still dominate clinical evidence, with human comparative trials needed for critical defects.

## Summary
This 2023 narrative review from Universidade do Porto surveys the materials used to replace or repair bone defects in dentistry and oral/maxillofacial surgery: autografts, allografts, xenografts, and synthetic substitutes. Material selection depends on defect size/shape/volume, tissue viability, handling, cost, and ethical considerations; small defects regenerate spontaneously whereas extensive defects require a graft. Autogenous bone remains the biological gold standard — osteogenic, osteoinductive, and osteoconductive — but is limited by donor-site morbidity, infection risk, and insufficient supply. Xenografts (notably bovine hydroxyapatite such as Bio-Oss®, Ca/P ratio 1.67) are the most widely used by dentists, while synthetic ceramics, calcium-phosphate cements, bioactive glasses, and polymers offer scalability, injectability, and moldability but lack osteoinductivity in critical-size (>5 mm) defects. Reviewing representative animal and human studies, the author finds cross-study comparison unreliable and concludes that combining scaffolds with mesenchymal cells and growth factors yields the best new-bone formation, with human comparative trials still needed for critical defects.

## Key Contributions
- Unifying four-class framework (autograft / allograft / xenograft / synthetic) compared on osteogenesis, osteoinduction, osteoconduction, supply, handling, cost, and ethics.
- Seven ideal-graft requirements stated explicitly: unlimited supply, osteogenesis, no immune response, rapid revascularization, osteoinduction, osteoconduction, and full replacement by comparable bone.
- Clinical indications mapped: dental implants, ridge augmentation, sinus lift, socket preservation, and periodontal surgery.
- Commercial product catalogue by class with source/chemical constitution, advantages, and disadvantages.
- Critical appraisal of in vivo/clinical evidence (rodent, minipig, rabbit, dog, human) comparing xenografts vs synthetics, with attention to translation limits.

## Methodology
Narrative, non-systematic review with no formal search strategy, PRISMA flow, or meta-analysis; structured as a material-class overview followed by a discussion of selected in vivo and clinical studies. No single patient cohort: the paper surveys nine human publications plus selected animal experiments (e.g., rat MD05 study with n = 60 synthetic and n = 72 xenogeneic specimens; a minipig calvarial model with up to eight titanium hemispheres). Outcomes are qualitative and histologic/histomorphometric (new bone formation, material resorption, inflammatory/foreign-body reaction, osteoconduction); no pooled effect size is reported.

## Results
| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| Autografts | Gold standard — osteogenic, osteoinductive, osteoconductive, most rapidly vascularized; cortical + medullary combination advantageous. Drawbacks: donor-site surgery, infection risk, uncertain prognosis, insufficient quantity/quality with age, metabolic disease, osteoporosis, diabetes; reserved for >5 mm defects/jaw reconstruction/congenital defects/tumors. |
| Allografts | Same-species, bone-bank processed; source of type I collagen and BMPs → osteoinductive and osteoconductive but not osteogenic; processing reduces biological/mechanical properties. Controversies: immune rejection, blood incompatibility, disease transmission, cost, religious issues; not first-line in dentistry. |
| Xenografts | Most used by dentists, documented >3 decades; osteoconductive inorganic HA with Ca/P ratio 1.67 (bovine, porcine, equine, coral, eggshell); Bio-Oss® best known. Advantages: low cost, availability, osteoconduction. Disadvantages: ethical/religious/health concerns, disease-transmission risk, disputed osteoinductivity, membrane required for particulates. |
| Synthetic materials | Biocompatible, injectable, moldable, scalable; bone formation tied to controllable composition/particle size/shape/porosity. Lack osteoinductivity → limited in critical-size defects; augmented with growth factors, cells, autogenous bone, therapeutic elements (tissue-engineering triad: scaffold + biological components + cells). |
| Calcium-phosphate cements (CPC) | Brushite cements set faster, absorb more water, and have lower tensile/compressive/shear resistance; apatite cements (from TTCP/α-TCP) set slower. Injectable, self-hardening, bioactive. |
| Hydroxyapatite (HA) | Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2 resembles bone mineral; osteoconductive without toxicity/inflammation; nanoparticles <100 nm have higher surface activity and bind growth factors/antibiotics/anti-resorptive agents. |
| TCP / BCP / ACP | β-TCP sintered below ~1125 °C, more resorbable than HA; α-TCP more soluble/reactive (cements); biphasic calcium phosphate (BCP) and amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) among ceramic options. |
| Bioactive glasses (BG) | Silica/calcium/disodium oxide releasing Ca and silicate ions; form an amorphous surface layer for protein/collagen/growth-factor binding; good osteoconductivity with little inflammatory or foreign-body reaction. |
| Polymers (PLGA/PLA) | Degradable but mechanically weak and poorly osteoconductive alone; used as carriers and to improve CPC/BG handling and injectability. |
| Comparative evidence | Rat MD05 (β-TCP + rhGDF-5) > bovine bone mineral at 6 weeks; β-TCP resorbed with more new bone than Bio-Oss® at 8 weeks while Bio-Oss® was incorporated without reduction; dog studies favored small-particle xenografts (150–200 µm) and MSC-seeded BCP; best overall new bone with mesenchymal cells + growth factors. |
| Bottom line | Newer synthetic substitutes show good biological behavior but remain largely preclinical; clinical trials still favor >30-year-documented xenografts. Human comparative studies needed for critical defects >5 mm and defects needing greater mechanical resistance. |

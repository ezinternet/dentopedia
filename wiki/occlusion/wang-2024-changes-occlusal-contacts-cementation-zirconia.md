## Three-line Summary

In-vitro bench study (Harvard School of Dental Medicine; Dentistry Journal 2024) cementing 30 identical CAD-CAM zirconia crowns on printed casts of a prepared right maxillary first molar — 15 with a 70 μm and 15 with a 120 μm cement space — after pre-cementation adjustment to preserve the existing MIP occlusion; PVS records read on a calibrated light box quantified contact area and intensity before and after cementation.

Contact area more than doubled in both groups (70 μm: 2339 ± 1206 → 6281 ± 3310 μm²; 120 μm: 2071 ± 909 → 5545 ± 3491 μm²; both p<0.001), and grayscale contact intensity increased (0–30 units) in 74% of 70 μm and 57% of 120 μm samples, with many crowns shifting from point to area contact and some contacts migrating from the central fossa onto cusp inclines.

Cementation itself — not the design — moves the restoration into heavier occlusion, so a full occlusal re-evaluation after cementation is mandatory; the smaller 70 μm cement space carries the higher hyperocclusion risk, and the larger space is instead associated with a physical shift of the prescribed occlusal pattern.

## Summary

This bench study asks a question every clinician answers by habit rather than by data: how much does *cementation* itself change the occlusion of a full-coverage zirconia crown? Thirty identical zirconia crowns were milled from one CAD-CAM design for a prepared maxillary right first molar and luted onto 3D-printed casts mounted in maximum intercuspation, with the only systematic difference being the virtual cement space (70 μm, n=15 vs 120 μm, n=15). Before cementation every crown was adjusted interproximally and occlusally to seat correctly *and preserve the existing occlusion*, so any pre→post difference is attributable to the cementation event.

The answer is large and consistent: mean contact area more than doubled in both groups (p<0.001 in each), and contact intensity rose by 0–30 grayscale units in 74% of the 70 μm samples and 57% of the 120 μm samples. The authors interpret this as a coronal shift of the restoration during seating that brings more surface into contact — a "hyperocclusion" signal. Pattern also changed: many crowns moved from point contact to area contact, and in extreme cases contacts left the central fossa for the cusp inclines, a pattern the authors link to instability, wear, microfractures, and restoration failure if left unadjusted. The smaller cement space was associated with more intensity increase and more contact-area shifting (higher hyperocclusion risk), while the larger space was framed as producing a physical shift of the prescribed occlusion.

The clinical instruction is blunt: perform a thorough occlusal evaluation *after* cementation, not only at try-in. The authors also flag the trap in the obvious fix — post-cementation grinding of an already-cemented zirconia crown leaves it thinner, rougher, and more fragile — so planning (and finishing) matters more than chairside rescue.

## Key Contributions

- **Isolates cementation as an occlusal variable.** Pre-cementation adjustment held the occlusion constant, so the measured change is the cementation effect itself, not a design or adjustment artifact.
- **Numerically sizes that effect**: contact area ≈2.7× pre-cementation values in both groups (all p<0.001); intensity increases of 0–30 grayscale units in 74% (70 μm) vs 57% (120 μm) of samples.
- **Cement space changes the failure mode, not the direction.** Both spacers increased contact; the smaller space tilted toward hyperocclusion and contact-area shifting, the larger toward positional shift of the occlusal pattern.
- **A low-cost quantitative recording chain**: PVS bite record + calibrated DC light box + grayscale camera + ImageJ — quantifies contact area and intensity without T-Scan hardware.
- **Clinical warning on the rescue move**: post-cementation grinding roughens and weakens the cemented zirconia crown; occlusal accuracy must be planned, not ground in.

## Methodology

- **Design**: in-vitro, two-group bench study; paired (pre/post) within each crown; α = 0.05.
- **Model**: Columbia Dentoform maxillary right first molar prepared for an all-ceramic crown (horizontal prep, chamfer finish line); scanned with 3Shape E3; **30 identical cast sets 3D-printed** (SprintRay Pro S); each mounted in MIP on an Artex CR semi-adjustable articulator with Type III stone by one operator.
- **Crowns**: one design in 3Shape Dental System v2.102.1.0; **30 zirconia crowns** (Katana, Kuraray Noritake) milled on a Roland DWX-52D; cement spacer applied to all intaglio surfaces including the marginal gap; **15 at 70 μm, 15 at 120 μm**, randomly assigned to casts.
- **Standardization**: interproximal/occlusal adjustment to seat the crown and maintain the existing MIP occlusion; thermoplastic insertion guide molded under **5 kg static load** to reproduce insertion orientation.
- **Records**: PVS (Regisil 2X) triple-tray record under 5 kg static load before and after cementation; cementation with RelyX Unicem self-adhesive resin cement on the articulator, 5 kg load, guide in place.
- **Measurement**: records photographed on a calibrated DC light box (Schott) with a grayscale camera (CFW-1308M) → light-intensity map; contact area (μm²) taken from the highest-intensity regions of the occlusal table; peak grayscale value = contact intensity; ImageJ v1.54.
- **Statistics**: paired-sample t-tests per group. Positional shift of the occlusal table was *not* evaluated (pre/post scans not positionally comparable). The 70 vs 120 μm contrast was not formally tested between groups.

## Results

**Contact surface area (Table 1, μm², mean ± SD)**

| Group | Pre-cementation | Post-cementation | p (paired) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 70 μm (n=15) | 2339 ± 1206 | 6281 ± 3310 | <0.001 |
| 120 μm (n=15) | 2071 ± 909 | 5545 ± 3491 | <0.001 |

Mean area increased ≈2.7× in each group (the authors' wording: "more than doubled"); the null hypothesis of no pre/post difference was rejected in both groups.

**Contact intensity change (Table 2 — % of samples per grayscale-increase bin)**

| Group | +0–10 | +10–20 | +20–30 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 70 μm | 47% | 7% | 20% |
| 120 μm | 33% | 17% | 7% |

- Remaining samples showed no significant intensity change. Summed across the three reported bins, **74% of 70 μm** and **57% of 120 μm** samples showed some measurable increase (arithmetic sum of reported bins, not a separately reported statistic).
- Direction of change is unidirectional in both metrics — **more area, more intensity** — i.e. the crown sits heavier after cementation, not lighter.

**Contact pattern**: many samples shifted from **point contact to area contact**; in extreme cases contacts moved off the central fossa onto **cusp inclines**. Pattern shift is described qualitatively in the Discussion — no counts of contacts (or of changed contacts) are reported anywhere in the text, so contact *number* cannot be quoted from this paper.

**Mechanistic reading by the authors**: cementation produces a coronal shift of the restoration, so the post-cementation position no longer matches the pre-cementation (adjusted) position — which is why chairside adjustment is still required even after a correct try-in.

## Clinical Takeaways

- **Treat try-in occlusion as provisional.** A crown adjusted to ideal occlusion on the cast/at try-in still gains substantial contact area and intensity on cementation — re-check in the mouth after cementation in every full-coverage case.
- **Smaller cement space → higher hyperocclusion risk.** 70 μm showed the higher proportion of intensity increases and more contact-area shifting; expect to adjust more, not less, with tight spacers.
- **Watch for pattern migration, not just heaviness.** Contacts can leave the central fossa for cusp inclines; that changes loading direction and is easy to miss if you only check "is it high?".
- **Plan the occlusion instead of grinding it after.** The authors note post-cementation grinding leaves a thinner, rougher, more fragile zirconia surface — finishing quality matters.
- **Caveat on transfer**: typodont/articulator in vitro with a standardized insertion guide and no musculature or TMJ — magnitudes are indicative, not predictive per patient.

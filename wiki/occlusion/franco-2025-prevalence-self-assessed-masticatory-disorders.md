## Three-line Summary

Systematic review with meta-analysis of prevalence (PRISMA, PROSPERO CRD42020202495): 22 population-based questionnaire studies of community-dwelling adults aged ≥60 years, 21 pooled (165,220 participants) across 10 databases plus grey literature.

Pooled prevalence of self-assessed masticatory disorders was 36% (95% CI 0.28–0.43; I² = 100%), individual estimates 4.3%–61.7%, heterogeneity unexplained by mean age (meta-regression) and unresolved by subgrouping on the four operative definitions.

About one in three community-dwelling older adults reports a chewing disorder, but I² = 100% makes 36% a planning estimate rather than a screening threshold — the rate reflects which question was asked as much as how many people chew poorly.

## Summary

This Brazilian-led systematic review with meta-analysis set out to fix a single number — how common self-assessed masticatory disorder is among community-dwelling older adults — and ended up demonstrating why that number resists fixation. Twenty-two population-based questionnaire studies (adults ≥60 years, community-dwelling, representative sampling) met the eligibility criteria from 8,761 records (5,015 after de-duplication, 434 full texts), and 21 entered the random-effects meta-analysis after Cho & Kim 2019 was excluded for reporting prevalence only by age band and severity level. The pooled prevalence was 36% (95% CI 0.28–0.43; I² = 100%) across 165,220 participants, with individual study estimates from 4.3% (Steele 1997, England) to 61.7% (Moon & Hong 2017, Korea). Heterogeneity was not explained by mean age on meta-regression, was not reduced by splitting studies into four definition groups ("chewing difficulty", "chewing disability", "chewing problem", "chewing discomfort"), and a stricter 1%-margin sensitivity analysis (4 studies) produced 33% (95% CI 19–48%; I² = 100%). No publication bias was detected (Egger's p = 0.063). The authors' conclusion is a prevalence estimate plus a methodological indictment: only one of 22 studies defined chewing dysfunction, instruments were largely unvalidated, and neither sex nor denture status was stratified — so the field needs standardised, validated self-report instruments combined with objective assessment before prevalence data can drive screening or policy.

## Key Contributions

- **First prevalence estimate specific to this population and outcome.** The authors state this is the first SR+MA of masticatory disorder prevalence in community-dwelling older adults; the point estimate is 36% (95% CI 0.28–0.43) from 21 studies / 165,220 participants, against a prior single-study range of 14.3%–49.7%.
- **Heterogeneity quantified rather than explained away.** I² = 100% is reported transparently and then tested three ways — mean-age meta-regression (non-significant), four definition-based subgroups (estimate unchanged at 36%, 28–43%, I² = 100%), and sample-size-based sensitivity (33% in the 1%-margin scenario, 4 studies). Nothing reduces it.
- **Instrument heterogeneity named as the driver.** Only 1 of 22 studies defined chewing dysfunction; several did not report the question used at all; validated instruments were the exception. This converts "36%" from a biological constant into a measurement artefact of question wording.
- **Risk-of-bias mapping with JBI.** 12 studies low risk, 7 moderate, 3 high (cross-sectional checklist plus prevalence/cohort variants), with the high-risk studies faulted for poor methodological description, unreliable prevalence assessment, and unaddressed confounding.
- **Explicit research agenda, no screening recommendation.** The authors call for combined subjective + objective assessment and standardised criteria so estimates become comparable, and position the prevalence figure as input to public-health and preventive strategy — they do not propose a screening threshold, interval, or instrument.

## Methodology

- **Design**: PRISMA-compliant systematic review with meta-analysis of proportions; PROSPERO CRD42020202495.
- **Sources**: Cinahl, Embase, Lilacs, Livivo, PubMed/Medline, Scopus, Web of Science + grey literature (Google Scholar, Open Grey, Proquest including theses); no language or date restriction; MeSH/EMTREE-based strategies; EndNote Web de-duplication.
- **Eligibility**: population-based cross-sectional/ecological studies of community-dwelling adults ≥60 years with representative sampling, using a self-reported questionnaire (validated or not) to ascertain masticatory disorder. Excluded objective performance testing (colorimetric capsules, colour-changing gum, sieving, test foods), nursing-home and home-care populations, prosthesis-evaluation studies, mixed-age samples without age-stratified data, and non-original documents.
- **Process**: two independent reviewers at both screening stages, third reviewer for disagreements; JBI Checklist for Cross-Sectional Studies for risk of bias (with prevalence and cohort variants for those designs).
- **Statistics**: random-effects (inverse-variance) pooling with DerSimonian–Laird tau², I² for heterogeneity, Clopper–Pearson 95% CIs, RStudio 1.2.1335; pre-planned sensitivity analysis by required sample size (5% and 1% margins), random-effects meta-regression on mean age, four-group definition subgroup analysis, funnel plot + Egger's test.
- **Outcome**: prevalence of self-assessed masticatory disorder, operationalised per study as chewing difficulty, chewing disability, chewing problem, chewing discomfort, or dissatisfaction with biting/chewing.

|---|---|

| Between-study range (22 studies) | 4.3% (Steele 1997, England) – 61.7% (Moon & Hong 2017, Korea) |
| Study sample sizes | 557 (Lexoboon 2012, Sweden) – 63,602 (Shiota 2023, Japan) |

| Sensitivity, 1% margin (4 studies) | 33% (95% CI 19–48%; I² = 100%) |
| Sensitivity, 5% margin (all studies; n required 354) | Main estimate unchanged |
| Subgroup by definition (4 groups) | 36% (95% CI 28–43%; I² = 100%); heterogeneity persisted |
| Publication bias | Funnel plot + Egger's p = 0.063 → not detected |
| Risk of bias | 12 low, 7 moderate, 3 high (Discussion tally); JBI design-specific counts: cross-sectional 5 low/7 moderate/2 high, prevalence 5 low/1 high, cohort 2 low |

Selected study-level estimates illustrate the definition effect: 4.3% for dissatisfaction with biting and chewing (Steele 1997, England), 9.7% for chewing disability defined as "yes, but hardly / no, I swallow whole" (Kamdem 2017, Switzerland), 26.3% for difficulty chewing in the past 6 months (Lee 2015, Korea), 36.8% for difficulty or pain chewing hard foods (Milagres 2018, Brazil), 43.3% for chewing discomfort (Shin 2022, Korea), and 61.7% for chewing discomfort on a 5-point scale (Moon & Hong 2017, Korea) — the same construct produces a 14-fold spread depending on wording and threshold.

**Papers that constitute the pooled estimate cluster into four operational definitions**, and the authors report that the pooled 36% holds within each (Appendix B) — i.e. the definitions do not partition the heterogeneity, which is the paper's central negative result.

## Clinical Implications

- **Use 36% as a denominator, not a trigger.** For planning (service load, public-health messaging, sample-size calculations) 1 in 3 is a defensible working figure; for deciding whom to treat it is not, because the estimate moves from 4.3% to 61.7% purely on instrument and threshold choice.
- **Ask the question deliberately.** The definition used changes the measured prevalence several-fold, so a clinician or researcher comparing their own figures with this review must match the item wording (difficulty vs disability vs discomfort vs dissatisfaction) before drawing conclusions.
- **Chairside relevance is aetiological triage, not epidemiologic.** When an older patient reports chewing difficulty, this SR tells you the complaint is common and usually self-reported; it does not identify the cause. Aetiology must still be worked up — occlusal support/tooth loss versus TMD versus denture problems (see [[tmj/jeon-2024-chewing-difficulty-tmd-tooth-loss]]).
- **Denture status is an unmeasured axis.** Several included item stems explicitly reference teeth, dentures, and gums, yet no denture-stratified estimate exists in this review — so the common clinical assumption that edentulous/removable-prosthesis patients carry most of the burden is not quantified here.

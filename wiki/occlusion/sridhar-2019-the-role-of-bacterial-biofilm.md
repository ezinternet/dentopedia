## Three-line Summary

In-vitro dynamic fatigue bench study: four dental implants per group were immersed in PBS (0.01 M, negative control), lactic acid (pH 5, positive control), or bacterial polyculture (test) and loaded 45 N → 450 N at 4 Hz for 2 million cycles, then evaluated by optical microscopy, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and electrochemical corrosion testing.

Surface discoloration appeared in all three groups, but only the bacterial-polyculture specimens showed TiO2 thinning on XPS and a lower corrosion potential (Ecorr) than both controls — direct bench evidence that bacterial biofilm and mechanical forces act synergistically on the titanium surface; linear polarization resistance (LPR) and corrosion rate (CR) remained comparable among groups.

Mechanistically supports the dual mechanical × biofilm pathway of implant failure, but this is abstract-only evidence with n = 4 per group, no living tissue, and no direct measurement of the hypothesized metallic particle release — hypothesis-generating for surface damage, not for clinical failure rates.

## Summary

Sridhar et al. (J Mech Behav Biomed Mater 2019) developed an in-vitro dynamic fatigue test method that immerses dental implants in biologically relevant wet media while cycling occlusal-magnitude loads — a departure from conventional dry fatigue testing. Four implants per group were loaded from 45 N to 450 N at 4 Hz for 2 million cycles while immersed in PBS (negative control), lactic acid at pH 5 (positive control), or a bacterial polyculture (test), with post-testing surface discoloration visible in all three groups.

Critically, XPS analyses showed TiO2 layer thinning only in the bacterial-polyculture (test) specimens, and electrochemical testing showed a lower corrosion potential (Ecorr) in test specimens than in both controls — damage the authors attribute to the *interplay* of biofilm and cyclic loading, not to either factor alone. Yet LPR and CR stayed comparable across groups, indicating the implants retained overall corrosion resistance despite localized oxide damage.

The study reframes implant failure as a mechanical × biological synergy rather than a purely bacterial disease, hypothesizing that this synergy can produce surface damage and in-vivo release of metallic particles — a bench-scale mechanistic foundation for the dual-pathway model of occlusal overload and peri-implant pathology (see [[occlusion/mojaver-2025-occlusal-overload-peri-implant-health-sr]]).

## Key Contributions
- A dynamic fatigue test methodology for implants in wet environments (PBS / lactic acid / bacterial polyculture) — coupling mechanical loading with chemically and biologically aggressive media.
- Direct experimental evidence of biofilm × mechanical-force synergy: TiO2 thinning and reduced Ecorr specific to the bacterial-polyculture group, beyond acid alone.
- Challenges the "bacteria-first" etiology assumption for titanium implant failure, proposing a surface-damage → metallic-particle-release pathway that links occlusal overload to biological harm.

## Methodology

In-vitro bench study, no living subject. n = 4 implants per immersion group. Fatigue loading 45 N → 450 N at 4 Hz for 2,000,000 cycles (occlusal-magnitude cyclic loading). Three immersion media: PBS 0.01 M (negative control), lactic acid pH 5 (positive control), bacterial polyculture (test). Post-testing outcomes: surface morphology (optical microscopy), surface chemistry (XPS), and electrochemical corrosion behavior (Ecorr, LPR, CR).

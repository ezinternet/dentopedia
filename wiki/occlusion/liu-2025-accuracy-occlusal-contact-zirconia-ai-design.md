## Three-line Summary

In-vitro design-accuracy study (mandibular left first molar model; 10 AI-designed vs 10 technician-designed zirconia crowns in the same PrintIn DentDesign software) plus a 3-patient / 6-crown clinical pilot, measuring 3D surface RMS, proximal contact, occlusal space and margin gap of milled-and-sintered zirconia crowns.

AI design was 3.46× lower in STL RMS (13±3 vs 45±3 µm, p=.0047) and ~6.6× lower in scan-file RMS (18±2 vs 120±15 µm, p=.0001), and had 1.8× greater model occlusal space (p<.05); model margin gaps were 57–92 µm in both groups (<120 µm), but in the mouth AI crowns gave 332–376 µm vs 276–341 µm margin gaps and a reversed occlusal-space pattern.

Technician-free AI crown design is at least as reproducible as one technician's digital design and clinically acceptable on the model, but it does not remove chairside adjustment — the dentist still inspects and adjusts occlusion and contacts, and the 3-patient pilot is far too small to generalize.

## Summary

This Journal of Dental Sciences study (Taipei Medical University) asks whether a crown designed entirely by an AI module can match a crown designed by a dental technician. Using one mandibular first-molar model, one zirconia material (ST-Color, Upcera) and one CAD program (PrintIn DentDesign), the same technician designed the same crown 10 times digitally, while the software's AI mode generated 10 designs with the margin line and all settings computed automatically — in the AI arm technicians touched nothing but support removal, staining and glazing. Surface accuracy was compared with mean RMS in Medit Compare from both the design STL files and post-fabrication scans; proximal contacts, occlusal space and margin gaps were sliced and measured in the same software. AI crowns were markedly more reproducible (intra-group STL RMS 13 µm vs 45 µm) and closer to the reference surface after milling (scan RMS 18 µm vs 120 µm), with a 1.8× larger occlusal space and a significantly tighter distal contact; both groups' model margin gaps (57–92 µm) sat under the 120 µm clinical requirement. A three-patient pilot in which each patient received both crown types then showed the practical caveat: in vivo RMS rose to 189–252 µm, the technician-adjusted digital crowns had the *smaller* measured space in the mouth, and AI crowns gave 332–376 µm margin gaps versus 276–341 µm. The authors conclude AI design is clinically usable but must remain dentist-supervised, and they reject their own null hypothesis because spatial design differences did exist.

## Key Contributions

- **Direct automation test, not a materials test**: the AI arm was genuinely technician-free at the design stage (margin line included), while the control arm was the same technician's digital design in the same software — isolating the design method from operator and software confounds.
- **Reproducibility is the largest measured gain**: intra-group RMS across 10 repeats was 13±3 µm (AI) vs 45±3 µm (digital) on STL, i.e. the same technician redrawing the same tooth varies more than the AI does.
- **Automation also improved post-fabrication agreement**: scan-file RMS 18±2 vs 120±15 µm (p=.0001), a ~6.6× difference that persists after milling and 1530 °C/8 h sintering.
- **Quantifies the occlusal trade-off**: AI crowns carried 1.8× more occlusal space on the model (the authors attribute this to the algorithm keying on the highest point), which flips direction in the mouth.
- **Model-to-mouth divergence documented**: in vivo RMS (189–252 µm) is roughly an order of magnitude worse than model RMS, and the design that needed more chairside adjustment in vivo was the *human* one (smaller spaces).
- **Clinical gate stated explicitly**: regardless of designer, the goal is a crown needing minimal or no adjustment, and dentists remain the final gatekeepers of AI output.

## Methodology

- **Design**: bench (in-vitro) comparison of two design workflows + non-randomized clinical applicability pilot. No blinding reported.
- **Model**: mandibular left first molar full-crown preparation (Nissin Dental study model); identical abutment for all groups.
- **n**: 20 model crowns (10 AI + 10 digital design, one technician, 10 repeats each); 6 clinical crowns (3 patients × 2 designs).
- **Software**: PrintIn DentDesign (Printin3D, Taiwan) for both arms — AI design computed from statistical models, with margin-line setting and modification done by the AI calculation.
- **Fabrication**: zirconia ST-Color (Upcera), milled (Ceramill Motion 2 DNA, Amann Girrbach), densely sintered 1530 °C for 8 h, stained and glazed; technicians in both arms only removed supports and finished the surface — only the digital arm had technician adjustment of marginal gap, occlusion and contact.
- **Measurement**: Medit T510 desktop scanner + Medit Compare. Mean RMS (STL and post-milling scan files) for overall surface agreement; sagittal slices for margin gap, mesial/distal prosthesis-to-tooth distance ("contact"), and occlusal height/space.
- **Clinical pilot**: 3 adults 20–70 y needing a molar crown for caries, abutment reduction ~1.5 mm, each receiving one AI and one digitally designed crown; intraoral photographs (Galaxy Note20) and periapical radiographs (Bel-Ray II, 70 kVp, 10 mAs). Exclusions: endodontically treated or severely damaged abutments, no adjacent natural teeth bilaterally, no opposing dentition. Ethics: TMU-JIRB N202109061.
- **Statistics**: JMP 16, two-way ANOVA + Tukey HSD, α=.05; mean ± SD of 10 replicates.

## Results

**Surface accuracy, model (mean RMS, µm; n=10 per group)**

| Group | STL file | Scan file | Within-group p |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI design | 13 (±3) | 18 (±2) | .2712 (ns) |
| Human digital design | 45 (±3) | 120 (±15) | .0001 |
| Between-design p | .0047 | .0001 | — |

- Ratio as stated by the authors: **3.46×** (STL) and **6.6×** (scan) lower RMS for AI. The Results paragraph prints "66-times" for the scan figure, but the Abstract (6.6×) and the table means (120/18 = 6.67) agree on ~6.6×, so this is read as a typographical error.
- Overall surface-difference range across groups: 13–120 µm. Colour maps showed near-uniform green (little deviation) for AI crowns inside and out; inter-design differences concentrated on the crown surface.

**Contacts and margins, model**

| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| Proximal/distal edge contact | 48–52 µm, both designs; **distal** contact significantly greater with AI (p<.05) |
| Occlusal space | AI **1.8× greater** than digital (p<.05) |
| Margin gap | 57–92 µm across orientations in both groups — under the cited <120 µm requirement (<90 µm in all four dimensions) |

**Clinical pilot (3 patients; AI vs digitally designed crowns)**

| Outcome | AI design | Digital design | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| RMS per case (I / II / III) | 202 (±10) / 252 (±13) / 189 (±11) µm | not separately tabulated | .0001 |
| Mesial contact | −30 to 115 µm | −79 to 4 µm | .0001 |
| Distal contact | −56 to 16 µm | −53 to 23 µm | .0001 |
| Occlusal space | −356 to −141 µm | −184 to 134 µm | .0001 |
| Margin gap | 332 (±32) / 376 (±37) / 366 (±16) µm | 276 (±25) / 341 (±24) / 293 (±18) µm | .0001 |

Negative values = excessive contact; positive = gap.

- In vivo RMS (189–252 µm) was roughly an order of magnitude larger than model RMS (13–120 µm); the authors attribute this to the uneven natural abutment surface versus a smooth model and to positional change of natural teeth over time.
- Direction reversal: AI crowns had *more* occlusal space on the model, but the *digital* crowns had less space in the mouth — "the digitally designed crowns exhibited a smaller space, necessitating more time for intraoral adjustment."
- Qualitative: intraoral photographs showed the AI crown close to the human-designed counterpart in size and morphology, with more colour-distribution difference on buccal and lingual surfaces; radiographs showed a snug fit and no excessive marginal gaps in either design.
- Data-consistency caveat: the Discussion claims both groups "met clinical requirements, measuring <90 µm in all four dimensions (Table 3)", but Table 3's in vivo margin gaps are 276–376 µm — the <90 µm figure matches the model range (57–92 µm), not the clinical values.

## Clinical Implications

- **Automation wins on consistency, not on finishing.** AI design removes the operator-to-operator variance in redrawing a crown (13 µm vs 45 µm repeatability) and survives milling/sintering better (18 µm vs 120 µm), so it is a plausible answer to the reproducibility problem the paper frames as the core clinical bottleneck.
- **Nobody escapes the occlusal check.** The AI arm had no technician adjustment by design, and in three patients that produced 332–376 µm measured marginal gaps and the largest occlusal discrepancies (AI −356 to −141 µm vs digital −184 to 134 µm). Both workflows must be verified chairside; the authors' own conclusion is that AI crowns, however well optimized, "still required dental professionals to oversee the final outcome and make necessary refinements."
- **Model accuracy does not predict intraoral fit.** The 13–120 µm model RMS versus 189–252 µm in vivo RMS gap means a beautiful model-level validation says little about what the dentist will find on a natural, unevenly prepared abutment — plan chairside adjustment time accordingly regardless of who designed the crown.
- **Occlusal space direction can invert.** More space on a model (1.8×) does not mean more space in the mouth; the paper's in vivo data went the other way. Any claim that AI design "gives lighter occlusion" should not be transferred from bench to chair.
- **Evidence weight is low for clinical use.** n=3 patients, one tooth type, one software build, one technician, no blinding, no survival/PRO data — treat this as proof-of-concept that AI design is *not obviously worse*, not as outcome evidence.

## Three-line Summary

Cross-sectional survey of 202 implantology specialists in Brazil (randomized from 1,147 CRO/RS registrants; Sept–Nov 2021): prevalence, reasons, practices, and risk perception regarding healing abutment (HA) reuse.

98.1% reuse HAs (mainly cost, 71.2%; "countless times" 98.1%), 94.5% do not inform the patient, 100% disinfect + autoclave, yet 95.3% never received manufacturer guidance on reuse, and only 8.9% identified cross-infection as a perceived risk.

Reuse is de facto universal practice in this specialist population, with a profound informed-consent gap and low risk perception — the dominant cleaning methods (enzymatic + ultrasonic) leave residual organic matter in up to ~95% of samples per cited literature.

## Summary

Healing abutment (HA) reuse is clinically controversial: an HA sits transmucosally for 30–180 days, accumulating a strongly adhered bacterial–fungal biofilm, and repeated reprocessing oxidizes the titanium coating, increasing porosity and creating retention sites for peri-implant pathogens. In-vitro and RCT evidence in this wiki debates *whether* decontamination can restore the surface. This survey instead measures **what practitioners actually do**. Among 202 implantology specialists in Rio Grande do Sul, Brazil, reuse is near-universal (98.1%), driven mainly by cost (71.2%), performed "countless times" by 98.1%, and — critically — undisclosed to the patient by 94.5%. Every respondent disinfects and autoclaves, but cleaning methods vary in efficacy, and 95.3% report never having received manufacturer guidance on reuse. Risk perception is low: 53.3% perceive no limitation and only 8.9% cite cross-infection. The paper defines the practice-reality/ethics pole of the reuse controversy and surfaces an informed-consent gap the biological studies do not capture.

## Key Contributions

- **Prevalence**: reuse is near-universal (98.1%) in a specialist population — establishing that the controversy is about a practice that is already standard, not marginal.
- **Ethics / consent gap**: 94.5% do not inform the patient of reuse — the informed-consent dimension absent from bench studies.
- **Training / guidance deficit**: 95.3% received no manufacturer guidance, linked to a low risk perception (only 17.8% flag residual organic matter, 8.9% cross-infection).
- **Behavioral backdrop**: characterizes real cleaning/sterilization routines (enzymatic detergent + ultrasonic dominant; universal autoclave) against which the decontamination-efficacy literature should be interpreted.

## Methodology

Online 3-section questionnaire (Google Forms) sent Sept–Nov 2021 to a randomized sample of implantology specialists registered at CRO/RS (1,147 registered; GPower-derived 203 + 40% non-response buffer → 284 invited via email/WhatsApp, max 2 submissions). Sections: informed consent; demographics; reuse/disinfection/sterilization routines, risk perception, and manufacturer information. Descriptive statistics on 202 valid responses; internal consistency Cronbach's alpha 0.7–0.8. Cross-sectional design — captures behavior and perception, not clinical or microbiological outcomes.

## Results

**Respondents (n=202)**: 75.2% men / 24.8% women; mean age 42.7 (SD 8.4); 89.6% own a private practice; 53% hold more than one specialty.

| Domain | Finding |
|---|---|
| Reuse HAs | **98.1%** (do not reuse 1.9%) |
| Main reason | Cost **71.2%**; practicality 26%; availability 1.9% |
| Reuse frequency | "Countless times" 98.1% (repeatedly 91.8%); only once 8.2% |
| Do NOT inform patient | **94.5%** (inform 5.5%) |
| Disinfect after use | **All** respondents |
| Cleaning method | Enzymatic detergent + ultrasonic bath **50.7%**; ultrasonic only 23.3%; water + detergent 21.9%; bicarbonate blasting 1.4%; NaOCl 1.4% |
| Sterilization | **All autoclave**; none glutaraldehyde |
| Manufacturer guidance received | **None: 95.3%** |
| Perceived limitations | None **53.3%**; ↑roughness/corrosion 20%; residual organic matter 17.8%; cross-infection **8.9%** |

The authors note that the dominant cleaning methods (enzymatic + ultrasonic; water + detergent) achieve limited organic-matter removal, with residual matter reported in up to ~95% of samples in the cited literature, while repeated processing oxidizes the Ti coating and creates porosities that retain a compact, viable biofilm.

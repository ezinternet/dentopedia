## Three-line Summary

Systematic review and meta-analysis (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science to 22 Nov 2023; PROSPERO CRD42023477634; QUADAS-2) of studies comparing CBCT with periapical (PA) radiography for vertical root fracture (VRF) detection: 23 studies in the review (18 in vitro/ex vivo, 5 in vivo per Table 1), 16 in the meta-analysis.

Pooled random-effects sensitivity was 0.708 (95% CI 0.608–0.792) for CBCT vs 0.518 (0.408–0.626) for PA (P = 0.011), while specificity did not differ (CBCT 0.841 vs PA 0.876, P = 0.46); CBCT sensitivity fell from 0.911 in non-root-filled to 0.758 in root-filled teeth, and heterogeneity was high (I² 70–87%).

CBCT detects more VRFs than PA but still misses roughly 3 in 10, with a false-positive rate similar to PA; the authors advise CBCT only after careful clinical examination, not as the initial radiograph. Evidence is mostly in vitro with induced fractures, heterogeneous, and shows publication bias (Egger P < 0.05 for CBCT sensitivity, CBCT specificity and PA specificity).

## Summary

Shokri et al. (BMC Medical Imaging 2024) pooled studies that imaged the same teeth with both CBCT and periapical radiography to detect vertical root fractures (VRFs), a lesion that usually ends in extraction so that a missed or false diagnosis has real cost. From 23 eligible studies (16 with extractable TP/TN/FP/FN data), random-effects pooling gave CBCT a sensitivity of 0.708 against 0.518 for PA (P = 0.011), with no significant difference in specificity (0.841 vs 0.876, P = 0.46). Accuracy was clearly lower in root-filled teeth, where CBCT sensitivity was 0.758 (vs 0.911 in non-root-filled teeth) and specificity 0.774 (vs 0.937), consistent with beam-hardening artifacts from gutta-percha and posts. The authors conclude CBCT is more sensitive but, because of dose, should follow careful clinical examination rather than serve as the first radiograph; the data are dominated by in vitro studies with induced fractures, heterogeneity is high, and publication bias is present for three of four pooled estimates.

## Key Contributions

- First pooled head-to-head CBCT vs PA estimate restricted to VRF detection (sensitivity 0.708 vs 0.518; specificity 0.841 vs 0.876, not different).
- Shows the root-filled penalty: CBCT sensitivity 0.758 vs 0.911 and specificity 0.774 vs 0.937 (both P < 0.001, root-filled vs non-root-filled); for PA only specificity differed significantly (0.819 vs 0.938).
- Makes explicit that PA pooled sensitivity (0.518) is statistically indistinguishable from 0.5 (Z = 0.314, P = 0.753 in Table 2), supporting the clinical point that a single PA film frequently misses a VRF.
- Flags the weakness of the evidence base: mostly in vitro/ex vivo with induced fractures, I² 70–87%, Egger P < 0.05 for three estimates.

## Methodology

- Design: SR + MA following PRISMA, risk of bias by QUADAS-2 (69.6% of studies low risk); PROSPERO CRD42023477634.
- Search: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science to 22 Nov 2023 plus reference lists; 1,251 records + 8 added; 33 full texts; 23 studies in qualitative synthesis, 16 in the meta-analysis.
- Eligibility: human intact, root-filled or post-restored teeth imaged with both CBCT and PA; at least 10 teeth; scanner, exposure and voxel size reported; reference standard stated (surgery/extraction in vivo; methylene blue or microscope in vitro); sensitivity/specificity reported. Animal, horizontal-fracture and non-comparative studies excluded.
- Analysis: Comprehensive Meta-Analysis v3; inverse-variance random-effects model of log-odds for sensitivity and specificity separately; I²; funnel plots with Egger test; subgroup by root-canal filling.

Reading notes:

- Authors attribute missed VRFs to artifacts from gutta-percha and metal posts (CBCT) and to beam angulation (PA; the fracture line is visible only when the beam runs nearly parallel to it), and recommend multiple PA angulations when a VRF is suspected.
- Source inconsistencies (logged, reproduced as written): 19 in vitro + 4 in vivo (Results) vs 5 in vivo (Table 1, Discussion); the pooled CBCT sensitivity (0.708) lies below both root-filling subgroup estimates (0.758, 0.911), so the subgroup figures should be read as approximate; the Discussion states all in vivo studies favoured CBCT while the cited Chavda 2014 in vivo study is grouped with studies finding no significant difference.
- Applicability caveat (ours, from the study mix): because most data come from ex vivo teeth with experimentally induced fractures, 0.71 is likely an upper bound for chairside sensitivity; this is in line with [[cracked-tooth/patel-2025-position-statement-longitudinal-cracks-fractures]], which holds that CBCT cannot reliably show the intraradicular fracture line itself and is most useful for the periradicular bone-loss pattern.

## Three-line Summary

Systematic review without pooling (PubMed, Embase, Ovid, Google Scholar; searched December 2015, updated May 2016; modified QUADAS appraisal) of imaging accuracy for vertical root fracture (VRF), 22 studies included, plus a narrative overview of biological, psychological and clinical aspects of VRF.

Of the 22 studies, 12 favored CBCT over periapical radiographs, 5 found no difference, and 5 were non-comparative CBCT-parameter studies; the authors nonetheless call the evidence "overwhelming" that CBCT is the preferred detection method, while noting the included studies were highly heterogeneous.

CBCT should be used with caution, only after clinical tests and radiographs fail to give a definitive diagnosis (AAE/AAOMR 2015 joint statement), and before exploratory surgery; high-quality studies are still needed before CBCT can be called standard of care.

## Summary

This 2016 review (Baageel et al., J Int Soc Prev Community Dent) asks which imaging method best detects vertical root fractures and adds an overview of the biology and psychology of VRF-related tooth loss. A narrative-synthesis systematic review of 22 mostly in vitro studies found 12 favoring CBCT over periapical radiographs, 5 with no difference and 5 non-comparative; no quantitative pooling was done and the included studies were highly heterogeneous. The authors conclude CBCT is the preferred detection method yet endorse the 2015 AAE/AAOMR joint statement: CBCT is not for routine screening, only for inconclusive cases after clinical and radiographic tests, ideally with a limited field of view and radiologist interpretation. Because the pooled evidence that later accrued is more cautious about sensitivity (see Related Papers), the "overwhelming evidence" language should be read as an overstatement of vote-counted data.

## Key Contributions

- Vote-count of 22 VRF imaging studies (12 CBCT better, 5 no difference, 5 non-comparative) as of May 2016.
- Documents the discordance among earlier systematic reviews on CBCT for VRF (Chang, Corbella, Rose: no demonstrated superiority; Talwar, Long: better or high accuracy) and attributes it to inclusion criteria and statistical choices.
- Clinical stepwise rule: clinical tests and 2D radiographs first; CBCT when still inconclusive, and before exploratory surgery when surgery is the only way to confirm.
- Biological and psychological context: VRF mostly in endodontically treated teeth of patients over 40, extraction the usual treatment, emotional burden of tooth loss.

## Methodology

- Systematic review, narrative synthesis; no meta-analysis, modified QUADAS appraisal.
- PubMed, Embase, Ovid, Google Scholar; December 2015, updated May 2016.
- Included: CBCT as a diagnostic tool for VRF in permanent human teeth (in vitro or in vivo), in vivo VRFs verified by clinical/surgical exploration, full acquisition parameters and sensitivity/specificity/ROC reported. Excluded: horizontal fractures, cracked teeth/craze lines, management studies.
- 22 studies included. Study tables are not in the retrieved PMC text, so per-study accuracy values are not transcribed here.

## Results

| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| Studies included | 22 |
| CBCT better than PR | 12 |
| No difference CBCT vs PR | 5 |
| Non-comparative (CBCT parameters/limitations) | 5 |
| Cost context (cited from Chang et al.) | small-FOV CBCT at least CAD 125 vs CAD 20 for a periapical radiograph |
| VRF prevalence after root canal treatment (cited) | 3.7-30.8% |

- Factors reported to alter CBCT detection: unit make/model, voxel size, tooth condition (root-filled with metal post or filling material produces artifacts), fracture position and width (hairline fractures harder than thick, displaced ones), and examiner expertise.
- Conclusion: use CBCT cautiously, after clinical and radiographic approaches fail, and before surgical exploration; more high-quality studies are needed.

## Summary

This narrative review synthesizes two decades (2004–2025) of literature on regenerative endodontic procedures (REPs) for necrotic immature permanent teeth. It covers the biological rationale (stem cell sources, scaffolds, growth factors), current clinical protocols (disinfection, medicament choice, scaffold induction, coronal seal), and reported clinical outcomes. Periapical healing (85–94%) and continued root development (74–81%) are consistently favorable and comparable to or better than conventional apexification, while pulp sensibility recovery (25–70%) remains unpredictable and should not be equated with true pulp regeneration. Histological evidence shows the tissue formed inside treated canals is cementum-/bone-/PDL-like rather than an organized pulp-dentin complex. The review frames current REP outcomes as "biologically supported repair" rather than genuine pulp regeneration, and highlights case selection, technique, and durable coronal seal as the pillars of clinical success until stem-cell-delivery and scaffold-engineering approaches mature.

## Key Contributions

- Organizes REP biology around three components: viable cell source (SCAP, DPSC, PDLSC, BM-MSC), scaffold (blood clot vs PRP/PRF/i-PRF), and biological signal (growth factors released by EDTA-mediated dentin demineralization)
- Details a concrete two-visit clinical protocol: first visit prioritizes disinfection (1.5–3% NaOCl, lower than conventional 5.25% to preserve SCAP viability; DAP preferred over TAP to avoid crown discoloration); second visit (2–4 weeks later) induces bleeding for scaffold formation, optionally supplemented/replaced with PRP or PRF, capped with MTA or Biodentine and a bonded composite restoration
- Synthesizes prognostic factors: younger age (≤12 years), larger preoperative root dimensions, shorter infection history, high-quality coronal seal, and PRP/PRF scaffold choice all favor better outcomes
- Explicitly reframes REP success as "biologically supported repair" rather than pulp regeneration, based on histological data showing cementum-/bone-/PDL-like tissue rather than organized pulp-dentin complex with odontoblast lining
- Flags intracanal calcification (~18–22% of treated teeth) as an underappreciated complication that complicates future retreatment

## Methodology

Narrative (non-systematic) literature review of PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science, 2004–2025, using search terms including regenerative endodontics, revascularization, immature permanent teeth, pulp regeneration, stem cells of the apical papilla, platelet-rich plasma, platelet-rich fibrin, and apexification. No PRISMA flowchart or formal risk-of-bias assessment was performed (author-acknowledged limitation, consistent with the narrative-review design).

## Results

- Periapical healing: 85–94% across studies, comparable to apexification
- Continued root development (apical closure, root lengthening, dentinal wall thickening): 74–81%
- Pulp sensibility recovery: 25–70%, the least predictable outcome and not a reliable indicator of true pulp regeneration
- PRP/PRF scaffolds show superior dentinal wall thickening vs blood clot alone (e.g., Alagl et al. 2017 RCT, n=40)
- Swaikat et al. (2023) SR+MA of 32 studies: REP produced greater root length and dentinal wall thickening than apexification, with comparable periapical healing between approaches
- Intracanal calcification in ~18–22% of treated teeth at medium-term follow-up
- Most evidence is retrospective with 12–24 month follow-up; long-term data beyond 5 years are scarce

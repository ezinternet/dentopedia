## Three-line Summary

Critical review of PubMed case reports (1978–2020, n=426 screened → 111 relevant → 30 distinct nonendodontic entities) mimicking, masquerading as, or misdiagnosed as chronic apical periodontitis, periapical granuloma, radicular cyst, or dentoalveolar abscess.

Of the 30 entities, 83.3% mimicked chronic apical periodontitis and 16.7% mimicked dentoalveolar abscess; malignant tumors accounted for 43.3%; 68.4% of nonendodontic lesions tested negative on pulp vitality, and swelling did not reliably discriminate endodontic from nonendodontic origin.

Pain, swelling, and pulp response alone cannot reliably exclude a nonendodontic lesion at a periapical radiolucency — a positive pulp vitality test is the strongest (though imperfect) red flag, and should prompt CBCT/biopsy workup before assuming endodontic origin.

## Summary

This critical review screened PubMed case reports (1978–2020) for nonendodontic pathological entities reported as clinically or radiographically mimicking, masquerading as, or being misdiagnosed as endodontic periapical lesions (chronic apical periodontitis, periapical granuloma, radicular cyst, or dentoalveolar abscess). From 426 initial hits, 111 relevant case reports were retained, consolidating into 30 distinct nonendodontic entities across five categories: malignant tumors (primary + metastatic, 43.3%), noninflammatory odontogenic lesions (23.1%), nonodontogenic cysts, bone-related lesions, and anatomic defects. Of the 30 entities, 83.3% mimicked chronic apical periodontitis and 16.7% mimicked dentoalveolar abscess. Neither pain nor swelling reliably distinguished endodontic from nonendodontic lesions; the most useful (though imperfect) discriminator was pulp vitality testing — 68.4% of nonendodontic lesions still tested negative (via secondary/coincidental pulp necrosis), but a **positive** pulp response accompanying a periapical radiolucency should raise strong suspicion of nonendodontic origin, since inflammatory endodontic disease requires pulp necrosis.

## Key Contributions

- Catalogs 30 nonendodontic entities (malignancies, odontogenic/nonodontogenic cysts and tumors, bone-related lesions, anatomic defects) documented in the literature as periapical-radiolucency mimickers of endodontic disease.
- Quantifies pain/swelling/pulp-vitality prevalence across the 30 entities, showing pain and swelling are not discriminating features.
- Identifies **positive pulp vitality with a periapical radiolucency** as the strongest available red flag for a nonendodontic process — a directly actionable chairside rule.
- Provides a synthesized diagnostic flowchart distinguishing apical-periodontitis-like vs abscess-like nonendodontic presentations.

## Methodology

Literature-based critical review, not a primary clinical study. PubMed search (1978–2020) combining apical-periodontitis/abscess/granuloma/cyst terms with "misdiagnosed," "mimicking," "masquerading," "simulating." English-language, well-documented case reports/series only; reviews, trials, and incomplete-data reports excluded. 426 records screened → 111 relevant → 30 distinct entities after removing duplicate-entity reports (most recent case kept per entity).

## Results

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Entities cataloged | 30 (from 111 relevant case reports of 426 screened) |
| Mimicked chronic apical periodontitis | 83.3% (n=25) |
| Mimicked dentoalveolar abscess | 16.7% (n=5) |
| Malignant tumors (primary + metastatic) | 43.3% (n=13) — primary 77%, metastatic 23% |
| Noninflammatory odontogenic lesions | 23.1% (n=7) |
| Pain present (pooled) | 53.3% |
| Swelling present (pooled) | 56.6% |
| Pulp vitality test negative (pooled) | 68.4% |
| Pulp vitality test positive (pooled) | 31.6% |

Malignancy subgroup showed the highest pain prevalence (84.6%); metastatic tumors were older on average (mean 50.3y) than primary tumors (mean 37.2y). Clinical takeaway: a periapical radiolucency with a clearly **positive** pulp response is atypical for endodontic disease and warrants CBCT/biopsy workup before committing to root canal treatment; a negative response, however, does not exclude a nonendodontic lesion.

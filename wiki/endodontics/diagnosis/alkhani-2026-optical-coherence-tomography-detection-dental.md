## Three-line Summary

Scoping review (PRISMA-ScR, 10 in-vitro studies, 2008–2021, n=6–80 teeth per study) synthesizing evidence on optical coherence tomography (OCT) for detecting dental cracks and vertical root fractures (VRF) across TD-OCT, SD-OCT, and SS-OCT systems.

OCT achieved sensitivity 83%–98% and specificity 63%–100%, consistently outperforming visual inspection and conventional radiographs in head-to-head comparisons, with markedly higher interexaminer reproducibility; best single-study performance was catheter-based TD-OCT (sensitivity 0.93, specificity 0.95).

Limited tissue penetration (1–3 mm) and the lack of a clinically practical intracanal probe keep OCT in the laboratory stage — all 10 included studies were in vitro, and no clinical translation is feasible without engineering advances in probe miniaturization and wavelength.

## Summary

This PRISMA-ScR scoping review synthesizes 10 in-vitro studies (2008–2021, sample sizes 6–80 teeth) evaluating optical coherence tomography (OCT) as a diagnostic tool for dental cracks — spanning craze lines, fractured cusps, cracked tooth, split tooth, and vertical root fracture (VRF) — the most severe form of crack that typically results in tooth loss. Across studies reporting diagnostic accuracy, OCT achieved sensitivity of 83%–98% and specificity of 63%–100%, generally outperforming visual inspection, transillumination, and conventional radiographs in head-to-head comparisons. OCT's key clinical advantages are non-ionizing radiation, real-time acquisition, and micron-scale resolution (~10 μm/pixel), positioning it as a complementary or eventual alternative to CBCT — the current radiographic gold standard for VRF, which carries higher radiation dose and has documented reliability limits around intracanal materials. However, the review identifies a decisive translational barrier: current dental OCT systems operate at near-infrared wavelengths below 1000 nm, limiting tissue penetration to roughly 1–3 mm, and catheter-based intracanal probes (the only design tested for imaging inside the root) require canal preparation to ISO 50–90 K-files — too invasive and impractical for chairside adoption. All 10 included studies were laboratory-based (no clinical or in-vivo human trials identified), so real-world diagnostic performance and practicality remain unvalidated.

## Key Contributions

- First scoping synthesis dedicated to OCT for dental crack/VRF detection, consolidating diagnostic accuracy data across heterogeneous OCT platforms (TD-OCT, SD-OCT, SS-OCT).
- Structured comparison table contrasting OCT with ultrasound, conventional radiographs, and CBCT/micro-CT across strengths, limitations, and current endodontic applications.
- Pinpoints the specific engineering bottlenecks (near-infrared penetration depth, catheter/probe size) that explain why OCT — despite superior sensitivity/specificity numbers in vitro — has not reached clinical practice for VRF diagnosis.
- Documents that catheter-based OCT (Shemesh et al.) achieved the strongest individual VRF-detection performance (sensitivity 0.93, specificity 0.95) but at the cost of requiring extensive canal preparation (ISO 50–90).

## Methodology

Five-stage scoping-review framework (Mak and Thomas) reported per PRISMA-ScR, protocol registered on OSF. PCC framework: Population = teeth with cracks/VRF; Concept = OCT imaging use; Context = in vitro, ex vivo, or clinical studies published in the last 20 years. Search across PubMed, Scopus, Embase, EBSCOhost, Google Scholar (through January 2025, no language restriction), supplemented by dissertation databases and hand searches. Three independent reviewers screened title/abstract then full text, with a fourth reviewer available for unresolved disagreements (not needed). No formal risk-of-bias tool applied (standard for scoping reviews per Tricco et al.).

## Results

- **Search funnel**: 28,268 records + 35 manual-search hits → 518 after dedup/irrelevance removal → 36 full-text reviewed (2 inaccessible) → 10 studies met inclusion criteria.
- **Included studies**: all in-vitro, 2008–2021, sample sizes 6–80 teeth, covering VRF, enamel cracks, and apical root cracks.
- **Pooled diagnostic performance**: sensitivity 83%–98%, specificity 63%–100% for crack/VRF detection — higher than comparator tools (visual inspection, transillumination, radiographs) in the studies that ran head-to-head comparisons.
- **Best single-study performance**: Shemesh et al. (catheter-based TD-OCT, "M2-CV" system with ImageWire 2 catheter) — sensitivity 0.93, specificity 0.95 for VRF, interobserver kappa 0.7–0.9 (very good to excellent).
- **Penetration depth**: consistently the limiting factor — typically 1–3 mm with near-infrared wavelengths under 1000 nm; SS-OCT with longer wavelengths can exceed 3 mm but remains insufficient for full-root imaging.
- **Two delivery paradigms**: (1) catheter-based intracanal OCT requiring canal preparation to ISO 50–90 K-file (Shemesh et al., Birsch), versus (2) non-invasive surface/crown scanning used by the majority of studies (Imai, Nakajima, Segarra, Shimada, Kim, Lee et al.).
- **Interexaminer reliability**: SS-OCT showed markedly higher reproducibility than visual inspection or transillumination (e.g., weighted kappa 0.611 vs. 0.178 in Imai et al.; 0.61 vs. 0.18 in Shimada et al.).
- **Technology comparison (vs. CBCT/ultrasound/radiographs)**: OCT offers non-ionizing, real-time, high-resolution imaging superior to ultrasound for hard tissue and superior to radiographs for depth information, but is currently outperformed by micro-CT for soft-tissue visualization and still requires more invasive canal access than desired for a "minimally invasive" tool; a 20-year technology-maturity gap separates OCT from CBCT's clinical adoption in endodontics.

## Limitations

- All evidence is in-vitro/laboratory-based; no clinical or in-vivo human trials were identified, so real-world sensitivity/specificity and patient tolerability are unknown.
- Penetration depth (1–3 mm) is inadequate for imaging deep root structures without intracanal probe insertion, which itself requires large canal preparation (ISO 50–90) — a major practicality barrier.
- Substantial heterogeneity in OCT system type (TD-OCT/SD-OCT/SS-OCT), imaging protocol, and outcome reporting prevents pooled meta-analysis; this is a scoping review, not a meta-analysis, and no formal risk-of-bias grading was applied.
- Authors call for RCTs, standardized protocols, and clinically adaptable (handheld, catheter-miniaturized, AI-assisted) OCT device development before clinical translation for VRF diagnosis is feasible.

## Three-line Summary

In vitro bench study inserting five dental implant models — varying by surface treatment (machined vs acid-etched), thread profile (triangular vs trapezoidal), and body shape (cylindrical vs conical) — into D2-density-equivalent polyurethane synthetic bone blocks, measuring insertion torque (digital torque driver) and bone deformation (strain gauge extensometry) simultaneously during installation.

Both torque and bone deformation increased with insertion turns; trapezoidal threads and conical bodies each required more torque than their triangular/cylindrical counterparts, but that combination produced compression that did not compromise the bone bed, whereas **machined-surface implants generated stresses that exceeded the synthetic bone's mechanical resistance and produced visible cracks**.

Implant macro-geometry and surface treatment jointly determine whether insertion compression stays within a bone-tolerable range or crosses into mechanical failure — the authors conclude surgeons must weigh surface treatment, thread profile, and body shape together, not torque value alone, since excessive deformation is linked to bone necrosis and implant failure risk.

## Summary

Nascimento et al. (2024, Instituto Militar de Engenharia, Brazil) directly measured bone deformation during dental implant insertion using strain gauge extensometry, paired with digital-torque-driver measurement of insertion torque, across five implant models varying in surface treatment (machined vs acid-etched), thread profile (triangular vs trapezoidal), and body shape (cylindrical vs conical), all inserted into D2-density-equivalent polyurethane synthetic bone blocks. Torque and bone deformation both rose with insertion turns. Trapezoidal-thread and conical-body implants required higher insertion torque than triangular/cylindrical designs, but this higher-torque combination produced compression the bone tolerated without damage — while machined-surface implants generated insertion stresses that exceeded the synthetic bone's mechanical resistance, producing visible cracks. The authors conclude that excessive peri-implant bone deformation can progress to bone necrosis and implant failure, and that implant macro-geometry and surface treatment — not the torque number alone — determine which side of that line an insertion falls on.

## Key Contributions

- **First held paper to directly measure bone deformation at the moment of insertion** via strain gauge extensometry, rather than inferring compression from achieved torque (as in the human studies) or from post-healing histology (as in Coyac 2019).
- **Produces a direct, time-zero mechanical-failure endpoint (visible cracking)** in the bone bed — a bench-mechanical analogue of the micro-fracture step that Coyac 2019 could only infer retrospectively from rat histology.
- **Separates torque-raising design factors from harm.** Trapezoidal thread and conical body both raise insertion torque but were not the factors associated with cracking; machined surface was. This decouples "high torque" from "damaging insertion" at the design level, adding a mechanistic axis to the wiki's existing torque-is-a-readout reframing.
- **Names surface treatment, not thread profile or body shape, as the design axis most associated with mechanical bone failure** in this bench model.

## Methodology

**Design**: in vitro bench/engineering study (mechanical measurement only; no biological or healing outcome).
**Specimens**: 5 implant models varying by surface treatment (machined / acid-etched), thread profile (triangular / trapezoidal), and body shape (cylindrical / conical).
**Bone analogue**: polyurethane blocks, D2-equivalent density.
**Measurements**: insertion torque (digital torque driver); bone deformation (strain gauge extensometry), recorded continuously during installation.
**Not recoverable from the abstract**: n per model, numeric torque (Ncm) and strain values, statistics/p-values, and the exact model-by-model results table (only directional main effects of each design factor are reported).

## Results

| Factor | Effect on insertion torque | Effect on bone integrity |
|---|---|---|
| More insertion turns | Torque ↑ | Deformation ↑ (proportionally) |
| Trapezoidal vs triangular thread | Higher torque required | Not implicated in cracking |
| Conical vs cylindrical body | Higher torque required | Conical performed better overall |
| Trapezoidal thread + conical body | Highest-torque combination | Compression did **not** harm osseointegration |
| Machined surface | — | **Cracks** — exceeded bone's mechanical resistance (worst performer) |

> Abstract-only ingest: no Ncm values, strain magnitudes, sample sizes, or p-values are held. The table reports directions and qualitative verdicts stated by the authors, not measured numbers.

**Authors' conclusion (paraphrased)**: excessive peri-implant bone deformation can lead to bone necrosis and implant failure; clinicians should weigh surface treatment, thread profile, and body shape jointly rather than reading insertion torque as a single safety signal.

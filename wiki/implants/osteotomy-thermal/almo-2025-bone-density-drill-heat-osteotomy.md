## Three-line Summary

In vitro study (240 osteotomies in polyurethane D1–D4 blocks, two-way ANOVA) comparing heat generation of a single 3.5 mm drill versus conventional sequential drilling (2.2→2.8→3.5 mm) at 800 rpm with water irrigation.

Single drills produced significantly lower temperatures than conventional drills in D1 (25.2 vs 26.3 °C, p < 0.001, d = −1.69) and D2 (22.9 vs 24.2 °C, p < 0.001, d = −2.09), but significantly higher temperatures in D3 (24.7 vs 23.5 °C, p < 0.001, d = 1.93) and D4 (24.5 vs 23.4 °C, p < 0.001, d = 2.09); overall drill-type main effect was non-significant (p = 0.776) while the density×drill interaction was highly significant (F = 69.03, p < 0.001, η² = 0.25).

Clinically, the single-drill protocol may be preferable in dense mandibular bone (D1–D2) for shorter surgical time and lower heat, but conventional sequential drilling is advisable in softer maxillary bone (D3–D4) where debris clogging with a single drill elevates frictional heating.

## Summary

This in vitro factorial study investigated how bone density and drill type interact to influence heat generation during dental implant osteotomy preparation. A total of 240 osteotomies were drilled in polyurethane blocks simulating D1 through D4 bone densities, using either a single 3.5 mm drill or a conventional sequential protocol (2.2→2.8→3.5 mm) at 800 rpm with water irrigation. The key finding was a significant interaction between bone density and drill type (F = 69.03, p < 0.001): single drills generated less heat in dense bone (D1–D2) but more heat in soft bone (D3–D4), with large effect sizes (Cohen's d > |1.6|) at every density level. While all recorded temperatures remained well below the 47 °C osteonecrosis threshold, the crossover pattern suggests that drill-type selection should be tailored to bone quality rather than applied uniformly.

## Key Contributions
- Quantifies the density×drill interaction (F = 69.03, p < 0.001, η² = 0.25) — the first controlled evidence that bone density determines whether single or sequential drilling is thermally superior
- Demonstrates large, opposing effect sizes at each density: single drilling reduces heat in D1–D2 (d = −1.69 to −2.09) but increases it in D3–D4 (d = +1.93 to +2.09)
- Challenges the assumption that single-drill protocols are universally equivalent to sequential drilling by showing the thermal outcome is density-dependent
- All measured temperatures (22.9–26.3 °C) remained far below the 47 °C osteonecrosis threshold under controlled irrigation — confirming that both protocols are clinically safe when adequately cooled

## Methodology
In vitro 2×4 factorial design: drill type (single 3.5 mm vs conventional sequential 2.2→2.8→3.5 mm) × bone density (D1–D4 polyurethane, SAWBONES). n = 30 osteotomies per subgroup (240 total). Handpiece: NSK Surgic Pro II step motor, 800 rpm, 2 kg predetermined load, 50 mL/min water irrigation at ~18 °C. Temperature measured immediately post-drilling with K-type thermocouple (HABOTEST HT617B), positioned 1 mm from the osteotomy wall. Two drills per subgroup (15 osteotomies each) to minimise wear. Statistical analysis: two-way ANOVA with Shapiro–Wilk normality and Levene homogeneity checks; Tukey's HSD post-hoc; partial η² for effect size; Cohen's d for pairwise comparisons.

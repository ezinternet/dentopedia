## Three-line Summary

In vivo multi-species study (rabbit, dog, human — femoral cortex; human data from pertrochanteric fracture fixation surgery) measuring bone temperature at 0.5 mm from the drill hole at ~20,000 rpm with saline cooling.

Cortical thickness drove heat linearly: rabbit 1.5 mm cortex → 40°C; dog 3.5 mm → 56°C; human femur 6.5 mm → **89°C**; and within the same canine femur, the cooling-inaccessible medial cortex ran 9°C hotter than the lateral.

Animal experiment temperatures are not applicable to the clinical situation — human cortical bone can reach far above the necrosis threshold even with saline cooling, making this 1984 paper the foundational citation for the species-translation gap.

## Summary

Eriksson, Albrektsson & Albrektsson (1984) performed in vivo temperature measurements during cortical bone drilling in three species under identical conditions (~20,000 rpm, saline cooling): rabbit and dog femur (animal model), and human femur measured intraoperatively during Richards plate fixation for pertrochanteric hip fracture. A thermocouple was positioned 0.5 mm from the periphery of the drill hole.

The critical finding was a near-linear relationship between cortical thickness and peak temperature. Rabbit femur (cortex ~1.5 mm) reached only 40°C; dog femur (~3.5 mm) reached 56°C; human femur (~6.5 mm) reached 89°C — all under the same drill speed and irrigation. The authors also drilled through the full canine femur from lateral to medial side and recorded a 9°C elevation in the remote medial cortex, attributing this to the cooling agent's inability to reach that surface.

The authors conclude explicitly that temperatures measured in animal experiments are **not** applicable to the clinical situation, where very high temperatures may arise even with saline cooling. This makes the paper the primary citation for the principle that dental and orthopedic drilling heat research conducted in rabbit or rodent models cannot be numerically extrapolated to human surgery.

**Relationship to the 47°C threshold:** This paper is frequently paired with Eriksson & Albrektsson (1983) — the companion paper establishing 47°C/1 min as the bone necrosis threshold in a rabbit tibia chamber model. Together, the two papers are cited to argue that (a) the threshold comes from rabbits with thin cortex generating low heat, and (b) the same drill conditions in humans generate more than twice as much heat.

**Dental implant context:** The human data here are from orthopedic fixation at ~20,000 rpm — 10–13× faster than dental implant protocols (1,500–2,000 rpm, intermittent, graded-diameter sequence). Eriksson & Adell (1986), a companion clinical study not held in this wiki, measured implant osteotomy temperatures in human mandibles and reported peak 33.8°C from a 29.2°C baseline — far below the necrosis threshold, suggesting that standard implant drilling protocols already provide adequate thermal safety in the human jaw.

## Key Contributions

- **Species-translation gap, quantified**: the first in vivo multi-species drilling thermometry study demonstrating that cortical thickness — not drill speed or irrigation — is the dominant determinant of bone heat generation.
- **89°C in clinical bone drilling**: establishes the extreme upper bound of heat in human cortical bone under otherwise safe conditions, anchoring the argument that all necrosis-threshold research must account for clinical bone density.
- **Cooling access failure mechanism**: the medial-cortex +9°C result shows that anatomical barriers to irrigation are an independent risk factor beyond cortical thickness.
- **Foundation for the "animal threshold is a rabbit threshold" critique**: every subsequent paper questioning the universality of 47°C cites this work.

## Methodology

- **Study type**: In vivo, multi-species
- **Species**: Rabbit (femur), dog (femur), human (femur, intraoperative)
- **Human model**: Pertrochanteric fracture internal fixation with Richards plate — not dental implant surgery
- **Conditions**: ~20,000 rpm, saline irrigation
- **Measurement**: Thermocouple at 0.5 mm from drill hole periphery
- **Additional test**: Bicortical canine femur drilling (lateral → medial) to assess cooling-access effect

## Results

| Species | Cortical thickness | Temperature (0.5 mm from hole) |
|---|---|---|
| Rabbit | ~1.5 mm | 40°C |
| Dog | ~3.5 mm | 56°C |
| **Human femur** | ~6.5 mm | **89°C** |

Medial vs lateral cortex (canine femur, same drill): +9°C in the medial, cooling-inaccessible cortex.

## Three-line Summary

Rat experiment (35 animals enrolled, final n = 27) comparing no alcohol against 3, 4, 5, or 6 months of alcohol intake before a femoral dental implant, with histomorphometry one month after placement.

Increasing duration of alcohol intake produced progressively lower direct bone-to-implant contact, with the largest deficits apically; serum calcium was unchanged while phosphate rose.

Duration of drinking, not merely the presence of drinking, tracks the amount of bone that actually forms against an implant surface — the biological mechanism under the clinical heavy-drinking association.

## Summary

Female rats were allocated to no alcohol or to 3, 4, 5, or 6 months of alcohol intake, then all received a dental implant in the right femur at 8 months of age and were euthanased a month later for histomorphometry (final n = 27 of 35 enrolled). The percentage of direct bone-to-implant contact fell as the drinking period lengthened, and the deficit was most pronounced in the apical region. Serum calcium was unchanged, but serum phosphate was elevated; the alcohol groups also took in more calories while losing more weight. The design's point is that chronicity is the operative variable — a short exposure and a long exposure differ in how much bone ends up against the surface, which is precisely the distinction the clinical literature draws between social drinking and heavy drinking.

## Key Contributions

- **Chronicity as the independent variable.** Holding the model constant and varying only duration (3 → 6 months) is the cleanest available demonstration that length of exposure drives the osseointegration deficit.
- **Tissue-level outcome.** BIC percentage converts "alcohol impairs healing" from an assertion into a measured quantity of bone in contact with the surface.
- **Regional asymmetry.** The apical concentration of the deficit is a useful interpretive detail when comparing with human marginal bone level data, which samples the coronal end.
- **Mineral dissociation.** Normal calcium with elevated phosphate argues against a simple hypocalcaemic mechanism and points to disturbed bone remodelling.

## Methodology

- **Animals**: 35 female rats, 3 months old at enrolment; final n = 27.
- **Groups**: control (no alcohol), and 3, 4, 5, 6 months of alcohol intake; solid food ad libitum.
- **Implant**: dental implant placed in the right femur at 8 months of age.
- **Endpoint**: euthanasia 1 month post-placement; histomorphometric percentage of direct bone-implant contact.
- **Additional measures**: serum calcium and phosphate; caloric intake and body weight.

## Results

- Direct bone-implant contact **decreased with longer alcohol exposure** — a graded, monotonic direction across the exposure groups.
- The deficit was **largest in the apical region**.
- **Serum calcium**: no effect. **Serum phosphate**: raised.
- **Caloric intake**: increased. **Body weight**: more weight lost in alcohol groups.
- Attrition: 8 of 35 animals did not contribute to the final analysis; group attribution is not reported in the available text.

## Clinical Reading

- **Chronicity is the message, not the species.** Nothing here can be scaled to a human dose, but the exposure-duration gradient is the mechanistic counterpart of the clinical finding that heavy/chronic drinkers carry the risk while light drinkers do not.
- **A short pre-surgical abstinence window has no support here.** The experiment varies *pre-implant* exposure duration; it does not test whether stopping before surgery helps — that question belongs to [[implants/survival/doring-2025-postoperative-abstinence-restores-osseointegration]].
- **Do not use BIC numbers as human thresholds.** Rat femoral histomorphometry is a mechanism probe; the percentage is not transferable to a patient's expected bone contact.
- **Apical-first deficit is worth remembering** when a radiograph shows an otherwise unremarkable crestal level in a heavy drinker.

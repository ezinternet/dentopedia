## Three-line Summary

Retrospective single-center cohort (Rabin Medical Center, Israel; implants placed 2011-2019; 687 patients, 2971 implants, 119 patients/555 implants with at least 1 year of continuous PPI use) testing PPI use as a risk factor for early implant failure (EIF), defined as removal within 12 months of loading.

Early failure was more frequent in PPI users at implant level (5.4% vs 3.5%); implant-level logistic regression gave PPI OR 1.91 (p=0.01) and anterior mandible location OR 2.28 (p<0.001), but the model explained only about 3% of variance and the patient-level model showed no PPI effect (p=0.11) and fitted poorly.

PPI users were older, had worse ASA status and more systemic disease and received shorter, narrower implants, so confounding is likely; the authors conclude PPI use "may" increase EIF and call for prospective trials, and the text contains an internal inconsistency about which predictors were significant.

PPI 사용자는 고령이고 ASA 등급이 나쁘며 전신질환이 많고 더 짧고 좁은 임플란트를 받아 교란 가능성이 크며, 저자들은 PPI가 EIF를 증가시킬 "수 있다"고만 결론짓고 전향적 시험을 요청했다; 본문에는 어떤 예측인자가 유의했는지에 대한 내부 불일치가 있다.

## Summary

This retrospective cohort from the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery at Rabin Medical Center (Israel) included all consecutive patients receiving implants between 2011 and 2019 with at least 12 months of follow-up after loading: 687 patients and 2971 implants. The PPI group comprised patients who had used a proton pump inhibitor (ATC A02BC) continuously for at least 1 year: 119 patients (17.3%) and 555 implants (18.7%); the remainder (568 patients, 2416 implants) were controls. Patients on bisphosphonates or immunosuppressants, or with prior chemotherapy, head-and-neck radiation or maxillofacial trauma, were excluded. All implants were two-piece, internal-hex, rough-surface titanium systems placed by experienced surgeons.

Early implant failure was defined as implant removal up to 12 months after loading. At implant level, failure was more frequent in PPI users (5.4% vs 3.5%). PPI users were older (age over 65 years more common), had worse ASA status (ASA 3 62.9% vs 26.2%), and more hypertension (51.5% vs 23.8%), hyperlipidemia (39.6% vs 19.9%), diabetes (20.9% vs 13.6%) and osteoporosis (19.6% vs 7%), and received shorter (p=0.008) and narrower (p<0.001) implants.

In the implant-level logistic model (chi2(13)=27.49, p=0.01; about 3% of variance explained; 96.2% correctly classified), anterior mandible location (OR 2.28, p<0.001) and PPI use (OR 1.91, p=0.01) predicted failure. In the patient-level model (about 4.3% of variance, poor fit p=0.04), ASA 3 vs ASA 1 had lower failure odds (OR 0.34, p=0.01) and PPI use showed no difference (p=0.11). The authors conclude PPI use may increase early failure and propose biological mechanisms (reduced calcium absorption, osteoclast suppression, V-ATPase inhibition) drawn from the literature, not tested in this study.

## Key Contributions

- Cohort of 2971 implants with an explicit continuous-PPI-exposure definition and standard exclusions for other bone-active drugs.
- Reports an implant-level adjusted association (OR 1.91) that does not reproduce at patient level (p=0.11).
- Identifies anterior mandible placement as an independent predictor of early failure in this cohort (OR 2.28).
- Documents how strongly PPI users differ from non-users in age, ASA status, comorbidity and implant dimensions.

## Methodology

- Retrospective cohort, STROBE, single academic center, ethics committee approved; two examiners screened records twice.
- Exposure: at least 1 year of continuous PPI use (omeprazole, pantoprazole, lansoprazole, dexlansoprazole, esomeprazole, rabeprazole, dexrabeprazole or combinations).
- Outcome: EIF = implant removal within 12 months from loading; inclusion required at least 12 months follow-up from loading.
- Analysis: chi-square and Mann-Whitney univariate tests at implant and patient levels; logistic regression at both levels (SPSS 25).

## Limitations

- Retrospective, single center; PPI users were older, sicker and received shorter, narrower implants, so confounding by indication is likely and the 1.91 OR cannot be read as a causal drug effect.
- Models explained only about 3-4% of variance; the patient-level model fitted poorly and found no PPI effect, and the text does not say how multiple implants per patient were handled at implant level.
- No analysis of PPI agent, dose or duration beyond at least 1 year of use (acknowledged by the authors).
- EIF window runs to 12 months after loading and requires at least 12 months follow-up after loading, so it is broader than pre-prosthetic failure; absolute numbers of failed implants were not available in the retrieved text.
- Text inconsistencies: the Discussion says only anterior mandible contributed significantly in regression, whereas Results and Conclusions report PPI OR 1.91; the Introduction phrases the null hypothesis as PPI use increasing failure; some patient-level percentages are contradictory; tables and many test statistics were stripped from the extraction. ASA 3 having lower failure odds is not explained.

## Clinical Implication (Korean Practice)

For a patient on long-term PPI therapy (common in older patients with reflux or ulcer disease), this paper supports recording PPI use as a flag for closer early follow-up, but not for withholding implants: the effect appeared only at implant level, was modest in explanatory power, and is entangled with age, ASA status and comorbidity. Anterior mandible sites showed the stronger and cleaner association with early failure here, so primary stability and surgical load deserve attention there. Changing or stopping a PPI is a decision for the prescribing physician; the paper offers no data that doing so improves implant outcomes, and the other held early-failure case-control study found PPI significant only in univariate analysis.

## Three-line Summary

Retrospective study (Malmö University, 2009–2019; 278 patients, 358 single crowns; mean follow-up 56.5 ± 29.7 months) investigating risk factors for implant-supported single crown failure and technical complications via Cox proportional hazard regression.

Twenty crowns failed (cumulative survival rate 93.5% at 5 years, 92.2% at 6–11 years); the most common failure causes were porcelain large fracture (n=6), crown repeatedly loose (n=6), and porcelain chipping (n=5); probable bruxism and male sex were the only two Cox-regression predictors significant in both univariate and multivariate models.

Ceramic chipping was more prevalent in bruxers vs non-bruxers (20/101 vs 22/257, p=0.014 log-rank); at least one technical complication occurred in 36/101 bruxers vs 52/257 non-bruxers (p=0.002), confirming bruxism as a key parafunctional risk factor even in the simplest prosthetic unit — the single implant crown.

## Summary

This Malmö University retrospective cohort (Medicina 2023, Chrcanovic group) enrolled 278 patients and 358 implant-supported single crowns treated March 2009 – December 2019, with a mean follow-up of 56.5 months. Seven implants failed (all after a mean 76.5 months), and twenty crowns were removed or replaced (mean 21.3 months). Life-table analysis yielded a cumulative survival rate of 93.5% at 5 years, dropping slightly to 92.2% between 6 and 11 years — consistent with other 10-year cohort benchmarks in the literature (91.7%–95.9%). The predominant crown failure modes were porcelain large fracture (n=6), repeated looseness (n=6), and porcelain chipping (n=5).

Cox proportional hazard regression (univariate and multivariate, with patient-level clustering per Lee 1992/Lin 1994) identified probable bruxism and male sex as the only two statistically significant predictors of crown failure. Male sex risk is interpreted through higher maximal bite force rather than co-distribution with bruxism (bruxism rate was non-significantly different between sexes, p=0.350). Among technical complications, ceramic chipping was significantly more frequent in bruxers (20/101, 19.8%) than non-bruxers (22/257, 8.6%; p=0.014 Kaplan–Meier log-rank), and bruxers were more likely to experience at least one complication overall (36/101 vs 52/257, p=0.002 Pearson chi-squared). An unexpected finding was ceramic chipping in monolithic zirconia crowns, attributed to low-temperature degradation (tetragonal-to-monoclinic phase transformation) and the presence of unsupported ceramic around screw-access holes in screw-retained designs (94% of this cohort).

## Key Contributions
- Provides the first dedicated Cox-regression analysis focused exclusively on implant-supported **single crowns** (not FPDs, full-arch, or mixed prosthesis types) — most bruxism–implant studies aggregate prosthesis types
- Confirms bruxism as a significant single-crown failure predictor in a cohort of 101 probable bruxers, adding specificity to the broader SR-level evidence in [[implants/survival/chawki-2026-particularities-of-the-implant-supported]]
- Quantifies ceramic chipping rate differential: ~20% of bruxers vs ~9% of non-bruxers (p=0.014), supporting bruxism-specific material selection and occlusal management protocols
- Documents chipping in monolithic zirconia (expected to have lower chipping risk than veneered), implicating low-temperature degradation and screw-access hole geometry as confounders
- Identifies male sex as an independent predictor (bite force hypothesis), distinct from the bruxism signal — relevant for risk stratification even in male patients not classified as bruxers

## Methodology
Single-center retrospective cohort, Faculty of Odontology, Malmö University, Sweden. Treatment period: March 2009 – December 2019. Study approved by Regional Ethical Committee Lund (Dnr 2018/856). Data extracted from T4 Practice Management Software records. Sample size calculated a priori (expected 4.75% failure, α=0.05, power=80%: n=252 required; n=358 achieved). Probable bruxism defined by dual-criterion protocol: self-report (questionnaire + anamnestic exam) AND clinical signs (masticatory muscle hypertrophy, tongue/lip indentations, linea alba, mechanical wear, cracked teeth, repetitive prosthetic failures). Cox regression: patient-level clustering for multiple crowns per patient (Lee et al. 1992; Lin 1994 methods). Ceramic chipping comparison: Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test.

## Clinical Implications
1. **Bruxism screening is mandatory before single crown placement** — probable bruxism (dual-criterion: self-report + clinical signs) is an independent Cox-regression predictor of crown failure; non-bruxer diagnosis requires both criteria to be negative
2. **Male patients warrant heightened bite-force risk counseling** even without bruxism diagnosis; higher maximal bite force is hypothesized as the mechanism
3. **Material selection for bruxers**: ceramic chipping occurs at nearly 20% in bruxers vs 9% in non-bruxers; monolithic zirconia does not eliminate the risk due to low-temperature degradation; night splint provision should be discussed
4. **Screw-access hole management**: the screw-retained design (94% of this cohort) creates unsupported ceramic — consider porcelain support geometry and access-hole sealing protocol
5. **Realistic CSR counseling**: 93.5% at 5 years (failure definition = crown removal/replacement); patients should be informed that looseness and ceramic fracture are the most likely failure modes requiring retreatment

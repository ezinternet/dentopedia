## Three-line Summary

Retrospective cohort study at the oral and maxillofacial surgery department of Rabin Medical Center (Beilinson Hospital, Israel): 687 patients and 2971 implants, of whom 173 patients (25.2%) and 708 implants (23.8%) were on anticoagulants (AC); early implant failure (EIF) was defined as implant removal within up to 12 months from loading, and 3.8% of implants (15.1% of patients) had EIF.

Crude EIF at implant level was 5.5% with AC versus 3.3% without; in the implant-level logistic model AC use had OR 2.64 (p=0.01), whereas ASA 2/3 vs ASA 1 (OR 0.30 and 0.33) and age 80 or older (OR 0.34, p=0.05) had lower odds. At patient level AC use was not significant (p=0.87) after adjustment; atrial/ventricular fibrillation was associated with higher odds (OR 2.95, p=0.01), and ASA 3 (OR 0.53) and ischemic heart disease (OR 0.40) with lower odds.

The AC association is therefore model-level dependent and confounded by cardiovascular indication (older, ASA 3, hypertension, diabetes), the patient-level model fitted poorly (Hosmer-Lemeshow p=0.22 as reported), and drug type, dose and duration were not analysed, so the result is hypothesis-generating rather than proof that anticoagulants cause implant failure.

## Summary

A retrospective cohort from the oral and maxillofacial surgery department of Rabin Medical Center, Beilinson Hospital (Israel) examined whether anticoagulant (AC) use is associated with early implant failure (EIF). The cohort comprised 687 patients and 2971 implants (61.7% male); 173 patients (25.2%) and 708 implants (23.8%) were on AC, and the rest served as controls. EIF was defined as removal of the implant within up to 12 months from loading (the Introduction instead says less than 1 year from abutment connection). EIF occurred in 3.8% of implants and 15.1% of patients.

Crude EIF at implant level was higher with AC (5.5% vs 3.3%). In the implant-level logistic model (chi-square(15)=33.07, p=0.005; about 9.6% of variance explained), AC use had OR 2.64 (p=0.01), while age 80 or older (OR 0.34, p=0.05) and ASA 2 / ASA 3 vs ASA 1 (OR 0.30, p=0.02 / OR 0.33, p=0.03) had lower odds. In the patient-level model, AC use was not significant (p=0.87); AF/VF raised EIF odds (OR 2.95, p=0.01), while ASA 3 (OR 0.53, p=0.02) and ischemic heart disease (OR 0.40, p=0.02) lowered them. The authors conclude AC use is significantly associated with increased likelihood of EIF (OR 2.64) and call for prospective validation.

## Key Contributions

- One of the larger single-department datasets (2971 implants) testing anticoagulant exposure against early failure.
- Shows that the AC association depends on the unit of analysis: significant at implant level, not at patient level after adjustment.
- Flags AF/VF as a higher-risk subgroup and ASA 2/3 / IHD as unexpectedly lower-risk, consistent with confounding and closer management of medically complex patients.

## Methodology

- Retrospective cohort, single department, specialist oral and maxillofacial surgeons; structured data form at patient and implant level; study period not stated in the retrieved text.
- Chi-square and Mann-Whitney for univariate comparisons; logistic regression at implant level (n=2971) and patient level (n=687); SPSS 25; p<0.05.
- AC drug class, dose, duration and INR are not analysed in the retrieved text.

AC users were older and sicker (ASA 3 in 71.8% of AC implants vs 20.9%; hypertension 50.7% vs 22.2%; diabetes 24.9% vs 11.8%; smokers 8.6% vs 5.9%).

## Limitations

- Retrospective, single department; confounding by indication (AC users older, ASA 3, more comorbidity and smoking); authors themselves note bias from age, hypertension and smoking and no bone densitometry.
- The patient-level analysis, the unit closer to the exposure, did not confirm the AC association and fit poorly; the implant-level analysis does not state adjustment for clustering within patients (not mentioned in the retrieved text).
- Low explained variance (about 7-9.6%); no data on AC type, dose, duration or INR; study period, inclusion/exclusion criteria and tables absent from the retrieved text.
- Inconsistent EIF time definition (from loading in Methods vs from abutment connection in the Introduction); protective associations of ASA 2/3 and IHD are speculative.
- Findings are associative; they do not support stopping or withholding AC, and the paper does not address bleeding.

## Clinical Implication (Korean Practice)

In Korean practice, patients on warfarin or DOACs are usually managed for bleeding, and this paper adds a separate question, early osseointegration failure. Treat the OR 2.64 as a signal for closer early follow-up, not as a reason to alter anticoagulation: the patient-level result was null, AF/VF itself carried higher odds, and indication confounding is likely. Record the AC type and the underlying cardiac indication in the chart, and do not interrupt AC for implant surgery on the basis of this paper alone (the interruption question belongs to the bleeding and thromboembolism literature, which this study did not examine).

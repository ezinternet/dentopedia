## Three-line Summary

Retrospective survival analysis (BMC Oral Health 2023;23:308, Seoul National University Bundang Hospital; 227 implants in 89 patients treated surgically for peri-implantitis 2003-2021, mean follow-up 4.3 years) stratifying outcome by initial bone loss as a percentage of fixture length and by surgical technique.

Cumulative survival was 70.7% for <25% bone loss, 48.9% for 25-50% and 21.3% for >50% (median survival not reached / 8.18 / 3.63 years; HR 2.25 and 4.59 versus stage 1), while resective and regenerative surgery did not differ overall (57.0% vs 44.2%, P = 0.444) or in any stage.

Bone-loss percentage at the time of surgery is the prognostic variable that matters — it predicts long-term implant survival regardless of which surgical technique is chosen, which reframes the resective-versus-regenerative debate as secondary to timely intervention.

## Summary

This is one of the few studies to answer the question that matters in peri-implantitis surgery — how long does the implant actually last — with a severity-stratified survival curve rather than a mean bone-fill percentage. In 227 implants followed a mean of 4.3 years, cumulative survival tracked initial bone loss almost linearly: 70.7% below 25% loss, 48.9% between 25 and 50%, and 21.3% above 50%, with median survival falling from "not reached" to 8.18 to 3.63 years. The technique comparison is the paper's most consequential negative finding: resective and regenerative surgery were statistically indistinguishable overall (57.0% vs 44.2%, P = 0.444) and within every stage, even though the stage 3 group tilted heavily toward regenerative surgery (58 vs 20). The absolute survival figures are low and the authors say so, attributing them to a retrospective cohort with inconsistent maintenance and permissive surgical indications — which means the stage gradient, not the absolute level, is the transferable result. Read clinically: by the time a peri-implantitis lesion exceeds half the implant length, no surgical technique recovers a durable result, so the decision that matters is made earlier.

## Key Contributions

- Delivers severity-stratified, long-term survival data using an easily reproduced metric — bone loss as a percentage of fixture length — at three thresholds.
- Quantifies the prognostic gradient through Kaplan-Meier and Cox models: 70.7% / 48.9% / 21.3% cumulative survival, median 8.18 and 3.63 years for stages 2 and 3, HR 2.25 and 4.59.
- Reports a clean null for technique: resective versus regenerative surgery showed no survival difference overall or within stages 1, 2 or 3.
- Supplies staged 1-, 3- and 5-year survival figures (stage 3: 90.4%, 58.8%, 45.7%) usable for informed consent and for deciding when to abandon a failing implant rather than retreat it.
- Documents that the resective/regenerative allocation was driven by defect morphology rather than randomization, which is itself informative about real-world surgical decision making.

## Methodology

**Design.** Retrospective chart review of every patient surgically treated for peri-implantitis at a single institution (Seoul National University Bundang Hospital) from July 2003 to April 2021. Retrospectively registered (KCT0008225).

**Sample.** 266 implants in 101 patients screened; excluded 13 implants in patients with no postoperative visit and 26 implants surgically treated more than once; final analysis 227 implants in 89 patients.

**Severity classification.** Stage 1: bone loss <25% of fixture length (n = 79). Stage 2: 25-50% (n = 70). Stage 3: >50% (n = 78).

**Technique.** Resective surgery n = 102 (44.9%); regenerative surgery n = 125 (55.1%).

**Outcomes.** Implant failure = removal/loss. Kaplan-Meier cumulative survival with log-rank tests (Bonferroni-corrected α = 0.016 for pairwise stage comparisons); Cox proportional hazards for stage effect after verifying the proportional-hazards assumption. Technique comparisons by log-rank only, since they were non-significant.

## Limitations

Retrospective and single centre, with no standardized supportive maintenance — the authors explicitly attribute the low absolute survival to absent strict maintenance and loose surgical indications, so the stage 1 figure (70.7%) probably understates what a maintained patient can expect. Implants requiring repeated surgery were excluded, removing the hardest cases while shifting the population toward single-procedure outcomes. Case definition rested on probing depth ≥4 mm plus radiographic bone loss, with the recommended 0.25 N probing force not always achievable, and radiographs not standardized. The severity staging (<25/25-50/>50% of fixture length) is not the 2017 World Workshop classification, limiting direct comparison. Technique was not randomized, and the stage 3 allocation (20 resective vs 58 regenerative) reflects defect morphology selection. Implant system/surface, prosthesis type and smoking were not modelled.

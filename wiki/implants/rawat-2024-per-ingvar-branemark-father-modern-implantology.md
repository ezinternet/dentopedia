## Summary
Per-Ingvar Brånemark (1929–2014) discovered osseointegration in the early 1950s during NIH-funded microcirculation studies using optical titanium chambers in rabbit fibula. He observed that titanium bonded inseparably with living bone — a phenomenon he named "osseointegration." Following decade-long animal experiments, he performed the first human mandibular implant surgery in 1965 (patient Gösta Larsson, who retained his implant-supported prosthesis for 40 years). Swedish authorities authorized the Brånemark dental implant system in 1970. At the landmark 1982 Toronto Conference, Brånemark introduced the machined titanium screw-root system to North America, enabling global commercialization through Bofors (later Nobel Biocare). The osseointegration principle subsequently expanded into otology (BAHA cochlear system, 100,000+ patients) and orthopedics (prosthetic limbs, joint replacements), cementing Brånemark's legacy as one of the most impactful figures in 20th-century medicine.

## Key Contributions
- Osseointegration defined as direct structural/functional bone–titanium bonding (1950s, rabbit tibia/fibula model)
- First human dental implant: 4 mandibular fixtures, full-arch fixed prosthesis, 1965, lasting 40 years
- 1982 Toronto Conference: worldwide dissemination of the Brånemark System and treatment approach
- Nobel Biocare commercialization: founded 1981, still markets the Brånemark System
- Brånemark Osseointegration Centre (BOC), 1989, Gothenburg
- BAHA: osseointegration applied to hearing aids (mid-1970s prototype with Anders Tjellström; product launched 2005; 100,000+ patients)
- Transcutaneous orthopedic prosthetics co-developed with son Rickard Brånemark

## Three-line Summary

Narrative clinical review (British Dental Journal 2025;238:779-789, King's College London / UCL Eastman) comparing retention of a compromised root-filled tooth against extraction and implant replacement, covering outcome definitions, the Dental Practicality Index, complications of both modalities, and the comparative literature.

Both modalities achieve high survival (primary root canal treatment success 85.2-92.6%, retreatment 77.2-83%; implant survival 90.9% over 6.7-8.7 years in one pooled comparison) and the comparative data are inconsistent and confounded, so the differences are small and late rather than large and early.

The decision should rest on tooth and patient factors — restorability, periodontal support, strategic value, occlusion, parafunction — not headline survival rates, especially since a parafunctional habit raises implant mechanical/technical complications from 17% to 60%.

## Summary

This review's value is methodological before it is clinical: it refuses to compare endodontic and implant outcomes until it has shown that the two literatures measure different things. Endodontic success means the periapical lesion resolved; implant success means no mobility, no radiolucency and under 0.2 mm of annual bone loss. Only survival is comparable, and once the comparison is restricted to survival the honest answer is that both modalities do well and the ranking depends on which retrospective dataset is cited. The authors are explicit that the strongest pro-implant comparison (72.7% tooth survival at 77 months in >13,000 patients) omitted oral hygiene, caries activity, bruxism and restorative status from its model — exactly the variables that determine tooth survival. Their clinical conclusion is that there is rarely an "ideal" option, that both fail biologically and biomechanically, and that occlusion is the shared risk axis — with parafunction quantified as a 60% versus 17% complication rate. The Dental Practicality Index is presented as the practical resolution: score the tooth across endodontic, periodontal, prosthodontic and contextual domains and let the total, not a survival table, drive the decision.

## Key Contributions

- Separates survival from success for both modalities and shows why published success rates cannot be compared across them (four tabulated definition sets).
- Provides the outcome ranges a clinician actually needs for consent: primary root canal treatment 85.2-92.6%, retreatment 77.2-83%, and a single pooled comparison putting implants at 90.9% versus 86.02% for root canal treatment over 6.7-8.7 years.
- Critiques the pro-implant comparative literature on its own terms — missing confounders, first-year failure clustering (58.8%) and the absence of restorative status from the models.
- Presents the Dental Practicality Index as a weighted, validated, four-domain decision tool mapping to treat / refer / extract.
- Quantifies parafunction as the shared failure amplifier: 60% vs 17% mechanical and technical complications with implants.
- Explains the aetiology of endodontic failure as predominantly biomechanical (caries, cusp/crown fracture, vertical root fracture, periodontal disease) rather than biological, which is why cuspal coverage matters.

## Methodology

Narrative review without a systematic search. Sections: the decision problem and available assessment indices (Restorability Index, BES Case Assessment Tool, Dental Practicality Index); outcome definitions and their inconsistencies (Tables 1-4); outcomes and modifying factors for root-filled teeth (systemic health, restorability, periodontal status, strategic value, patient factors); outcomes and modifying factors for implants; occlusal and biological complications with clinical figures; comparative research; conclusion. Evidence is drawn from published systematic reviews, retrospective cohorts and textbook criteria rather than re-analysis.

## Results

**Outcome ranges cited**

| Modality | Figure | Context |
|---|---|---|
| Primary root canal treatment success | 85.2-92.6% | SR data |
| Root canal retreatment success | 77.2-83% | SR data |
| Root canal treatment survival | 86.02% | Pooled, 6.7-8.7 y |
| Retreatment survival | 78.2% | Same comparison |
| Root-end surgery survival | 63.4% | Same comparison (non-microsurgical studies included) |
| Implant survival | 90.9% | Same comparison |
| Root-filled tooth survival | 72.7% at 77 months | Chatzopoulos, >13,000 patients |
| Implant failures in first year | 58.8% | Same study |

**Comparative results across reviews.** No significant 72-month survival difference in one study (high success both). Comparable survival for the first three years in one SR, with root-filled teeth declining faster afterwards — but three included studies favoured implants and three reported lower survival for implant prostheses. No meaningful difference to ten years in another review, with single-tooth implants slightly ahead and the gap widening after ten years. Root-filled teeth without cuspal coverage failed earlier.

**Complication profile.** Parafunction: 60% mechanical/technical complications vs 17% without. Implant failures cluster perio-biologically around poor plaque control, periodontitis history, smoking, submucosal cement, ill-fitting restorations and implants placed in augmented bone (higher peri-implantitis, similar mucositis incidence). Endodontic failures are mostly unrestorable caries, restorative failure, cusp/crown fracture, vertical root fracture and periodontal disease.

**Decision framework.** Dental Practicality Index weights four domains (endodontic, periodontal, prosthodontic, contextual) on a 0/1/2/6 scale to indicate treat, refer or extract; the authors stress indices support but never replace clinical judgement.

## Limitations

Narrative review: no protocol, no systematic search, no formal risk-of-bias assessment, and the comparative section reports other authors' pooled figures without reanalysis or sensitivity analysis. Several key figures come from a single retrospective study the authors themselves criticize, so both the pro-tooth and pro-implant numbers are weaker than their presentation suggests. The practical guidance — mutually protected occlusion, light intercuspal contacts, cuspal coverage — is expert opinion rather than trial evidence. No cost, time-to-completion or patient-reported outcome data are synthesized despite being central to the decision.

## Three-line Summary

PRISMA systematic review (PubMed/Medline, Embase, Cochrane; search updated May 2016) of 10 prospective controlled studies — 6 animal, 4 human, 334 implants with 3 failures — comparing clinical parameters of implants placed in healed sites with flapped vs flapless surgery.

Animal studies consistently favored the flapless technique on every analyzed parameter (probing depth 1.0 vs 1.7 mm, inflammation 0/12 vs 5/12 implants, less recession at 4–8 weeks), while human studies showed only a trend toward flapless (significantly higher probing depth and gingival index in flap groups) with no consensus.

The flapless advantage is short-term, soft-tissue-level, and animal-dominated evidence; human data remain too sparse for consensus, so the authors call for more human research.

## Summary

This PRISMA systematic review (search updated May 2016) asked how flapped and flapless surgical techniques affect the clinical parameters of implants placed in healed sites, retrieving 889 records and including 10 prospective controlled studies — 6 animal experimental and 4 human clinical — totaling 334 implants with only 3 failures. Animal studies showed better results with the flapless technique across all analyzed parameters (lower gingival index and probing depth, less inflammation, less recession after 4–8 weeks), which the authors attribute to minimum disruption of peri-implant tissues and preservation of their circulation. Human studies trended toward flapless (significantly higher probing depth and gingival index in flap groups) but reached no consensus, leaving the human-level question open. Mechanistically, the paper's framing is that reflection of the mucoperiosteal flap compromises the vascular supply of bone, potentially leading to crestal bone loss and long-term aesthetic complications — the rationale flapless surgery was built to avoid. Clinically, the authors recommend cautious patient selection for flapless placement given its drawbacks (no visualization of landmarks, thermal osseous damage risk, inability to contour bone or regenerate fenestrations, keratinized gingiva loss).

## Key Contributions

- First systematic review focused on **clinical parameters** (gingival index, plaque index, probing depth, recession, mucosal/epithelial attachment height, keratinized gingiva, papilla index) of flapped vs flapless implants in healed sites — prior SRs covered only marginal bone loss.
- Separates **animal vs human evidence streams** with dedicated risk-of-bias tools: SYRCLE's tool (10 entries) for animal studies, Cochrane RoB (7 domains) for human studies.
- Articulates the vascular mechanism as the review's frame: mucoperiosteal flap reflection compromises the vascular supply of bone → crestal bone loss and long-term aesthetic complications; flapless surgery preserves peri-implant tissue circulation and accelerates recovery.

## Methodology

PRISMA systematic review, narrative synthesis (no meta-analysis). Databases: PubMed/Medline, Embase, Cochrane Library; English-language dental journals; search updated May 2016. Inclusion: prospective controlled studies comparing flap vs flapless in healed sites, >10 implants per group, ≥1 clinical parameter, survival rate specified, ≥1 week follow-up; excluded case reports, SRs/technical notes, immediate placement, and simultaneous bone regeneration. Flow: 889 records → 34 duplicates removed → 813 excluded on title/abstract → 42 full-text → 32 excluded (2 not dental implants, 14 not clinical parameters, 12 not flap-vs-flapless, 4 immediate implants) → **10 studies**. Pooled: 334 implants (range 22–60 per study), 3 failures, all single/partial rehabilitation, all mandibular except one study; follow-up 8 weeks–24 months (one study 1 week). Flap designs: 8 studies midcrestal flap with intrasulcular incision, 2 trapezoidal; flapless protocols: 8 tissue punch, 2 transmucosal without incision, 1 mini-incision (5 mm). Risk of bias assessed in duplicate with SYRCLE (animals) and Cochrane RoB (humans); results interpreted with caution since both well-described and poorly-reported trials tend to rate as "unclear".

## Results

| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| Studies included | 10 (6 animal experimental, 4 human clinical) |
| Implants / failures | 334 implants, 3 failures (22–60 implants per study) |
| Animal PD (flapless vs flap) | 1.0 vs 1.7 mm — significantly greater in flap group |
| Animal GI (flapless vs flap) | inflammation score 0 vs 0.9 |
| Animal inflammation (Kim et al.) | 0/12 flapless vs 5/12 flap implants with red, oedematous tissue |
| Human PD (2 studies) | significantly higher in flap group in both |
| Human GI (4 studies) | significantly higher in flap groups at 3 and 9 months (2 studies) |
| Human KG (2 studies) | significant between-group difference in one (mean 0.86 mm, flap group); both groups lose KG, flap group loses more |

### Animal studies (6) — flapless favored

- **Gingival index** (1 study): flapless involved less postoperative inflammation than flap (score 0 vs 0.9).
- **Probing depth** (1 study): significantly greater in the flap group (mean 1.7 vs 1.0 mm).
- **Inflammation** (Kim et al., the included canine study): soft tissue at all flapless sites free from signs of inflammation; red and oedematous in 5 of 12 implants at flap sites.
- **Recession** (1 study): no significant difference at 2 weeks, but less pronounced in flapless at 4 and 8 weeks — evidence that flapless causes minor epithelial contraction and better aesthetic outcomes.
- **Mucosal height** (2 studies): thicker at flap than flapless sites in one (3.5 vs 2.2 mm), but significantly higher at lingual flapless sites (no buccal difference) in the other.
- **Epithelial attachment** (3 studies, mixed): higher with flapless punch than flap in one (2.27 vs 1.38 mm, lowest 1.1 mm transmucosal); statistically significant higher with flap in another (2.2 vs 1.2 mm); no significant difference in the third.

### Human studies (4) — trend to flapless, no consensus

- **Probing depth** (2 studies): significantly higher in the flap group in both; in one (Wang et al.) PD increased in the flap group on the day of abutment connection vs 4 weeks post-surgery, then stabilized.
- **Gingival index** (4 studies): significantly higher in flap groups at 3 and 9 months in two; the other two showed a decrease at 1 and 4 weeks.
- **Plaque index** (3 studies): flapped implants exhibited higher PI, but the difference was no longer statistically significant at 15 months (two studies) and at 1–2 weeks post-surgery (one study).
- **Keratinized gingiva** (2 studies): one found statistically significant between-group differences (mean of 0.86 mm in the flap group), with both groups losing KG and the flap group losing more over time; the other measured flapless 4.2→3.7 mm and flapped 4.5→4.0 mm by abutment connection, stable at 24 months.
- **Papilla index & recession** (1 study, Bashutski et al.): no significant recession differences at any time point; PPI increased over time in both groups with a significantly larger flapless increase at 6 and 9 months; no PPI difference in thin biotypes, while thick-biotype flapless patients trended toward greater papilla fill at 9 months (not significant at 15 months).

The authors' practice implication: flapless implants in healed sites show better clinical parameters because the technique minimally disrupts peri-implant tissues and preserves their circulation, but evidence on complications/failure risk remains insufficient, so patient selection should weigh limited bone width, lack of keratinized tissue, and the difficulty of confirming the implant is fully surrounded by bone.

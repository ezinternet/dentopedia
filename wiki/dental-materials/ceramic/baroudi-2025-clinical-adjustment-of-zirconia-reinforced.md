## Three-line Summary

In-vitro biaxial flexural strength study (Materials 2025; 4 groups × n = 15 discs, 1.2 × 15 mm) of zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate (ZLS, Suprinity) and lithium disilicate (LDS, IPS e.max CAD) adjusted with an extra-fine cylindrical diamond bur (3099 FF) either before crystallization (AC) or after crystallization plus polishing (CA).

Abrasion after crystallization roughly halved strength in ZLS (232.1 → 115.0 MPa) and significantly lowered LDS (244.3 MPa when abraded before crystallization); two-way ANOVA showed the abrasion/crystallization sequence, not the material, was the only significant factor, and final surface roughness (표면거칠기, Ra) remained higher than baseline in every group despite polishing.

Clinical implication: adjust and try in glass-ceramic restorations in the pre-crystallized state whenever the workflow allows, because post-crystallization diamond adjustment leaves subsurface cracks that polishing cannot repair — and the paper does not simulate glazing, aging, or cyclic loading.

## 세줄요약

시험관 연구(Materials 2025, 4군 × n = 15, 직경 15 mm × 두께 1.2 mm 디스크): 지르코니아 강화 리튬실리케이트 (Zirconia-reinforced Lithium Silicate, ZLS — Suprinity)와 리튬디실리케이트 (Lithium Disilicate, LDS — IPS e.max CAD)를 초미세 원통형 다이아몬드 버(3099 FF)로 결정화 전(AC) 또는 결정화 후 연마까지 시행(CA)해 이축 굴곡강도 (Biaxial Flexural Strength, BFS)를 비교.

결정화 후 연삭 시 ZLS는 232.1 → 115.0 MPa로 약 절반, LDS도 결정화 전 연삭(244.3 MPa)보다 유의하게 낮았고, 이원배치 분산분석 (Two-way ANOVA)에서 유의 인자는 재료가 아니라 연삭·결정화 순서뿐이었으며, 연마 후에도 최종 표면거칠기 (Surface Roughness, Ra)는 모든 군에서 초기값보다 유의하게 높았다.

임상적 의미: 글라스세라믹은 가능하면 결정화 전(pre-crystallized) 상태에서 교합조정·시적을 끝내야 하며, 결정화 후 다이아몬드 조정은 연마로 회복되지 않는 표면하 균열을 남긴다 — 단 유약 재소성·에이징·반복하중은 이 연구에서 평가되지 않았다.

## Summary

This bench study asks a workflow question rather than an instrument question: does the *moment* of clinical adjustment — before or after crystallization — change the strength penalty paid by two milled glass-ceramics? Pre-crystallized ZLS (Suprinity) and LDS (IPS e.max CAD) blocks were sectioned into 1.2 × 15 mm discs, baseline Ra was recorded with a contact profilometer, and each disc was assigned to one of four groups (n = 15): ZLS-AC, LDS-AC (diamond abrasion → polish → crystallization) and ZLS-CA, LDS-CA (crystallization → diamond abrasion → polish). Adjustment was produced with an extra-fine cylindrical diamond bur (3099 FF) in a high-speed handpiece under manual pressure until a marker on the surface was removed — deliberately unstandardized to imitate chairside adjustment. Polishing used silicone diamond discs (AC) or rubber points (CA) for one minute. Strength was measured by piston-on-three-ball biaxial flexure with the abraded surface facing down, i.e. loaded in tension, which is the least favorable orientation. Weibull analysis (ENV 843-5) and SEM fracture-origin inspection completed the dataset.

Every adjusted group ended rougher than its own baseline, and polishing did not close that gap. Strength, however, separated cleanly by timing: both materials lost significant strength when adjusted after crystallization, and two-way ANOVA attributed significance to the sequence alone — not to material, and not to an interaction. In other words, the zirconia filler in ZLS provided no measurable protection on this axis. The authors attribute the surviving strength of the AC groups to glass-phase melting during the crystallization firing, which partially seals the abrasion defects (consistent with earlier reports that LDS cracks shrink or become undetectable after crystallization).

For clinical decision-making the trade-off is concrete: pre-crystallization adjustment buys strength but costs a laboratory round trip (or a chairside crystallization cycle), whereas post-crystallization adjustment is fast and allows immediate cementation at the price of roughly half the strength in ZLS. Because glaze re-application was not tested, the authors leave open whether glazing a post-crystallization adjustment would recover the loss — the repair route that the zirconia literature does support.

## Key Contributions

1. Makes **adjustment timing** an explicit independent variable — before vs after crystallization — rather than varying burs or polishing systems.
2. Shows the effect is **material-independent**: two-way ANOVA found significance only for timing (no material effect, no interaction), so ZLS's zirconia reinforcement did not offset abrasion damage.
3. Quantifies the post-crystallization penalty: ZLS 232.1 → 115.0 MPa (≈50% loss); LDS significantly lower after crystallization than its 244.3 MPa pre-crystallization value.
4. Demonstrates that **polishing after diamond abrasion does not restore Ra** in either glass-ceramic — a direct contrast with zirconia, where polishing is the accepted remediation step.
5. Provides a mechanism-level explanation (defect sealing by glass-phase flow during the crystallization firing) that reconciles LDS and ZLS behavior with the existing defect-healing literature.
6. Uses worst-case loading (abraded surface in tension) plus Weibull σ0, so the strength loss is not an artifact of favorable specimen orientation.

## Methodology

- **Specimens**: pre-crystallized ZLS (Suprinity, Vita Zahnfabrik) and LDS (IPS e.max CAD, Ivoclar Vivadent) blocks → 15 mm cylinders → diamond-blade sectioned to 1.2 ± 0.2 mm discs → 600-grit SiC finish on both faces.
- **Baseline Ra**: contact profilometer (SurfTest SJ-310, Mitutoyo), 0.25 mm cutoff, 0.5 mm/s, three parallel readings.
- **Groups (4 × n = 15; 60 discs)**: AC = abrasion **before** crystallization (ZLS-AC, LDS-AC); CA = crystallization **then** abrasion (ZLS-CA, LDS-CA).
- **Adjustment**: cylindrical extra-fine-grit diamond bur 3099 FF (KG Sorensen), high-speed handpiece, manual pressure, endpoint = complete removal of a surface marker (intentionally not standardized).
- **Polishing**: AC — silicone diamond discs DF→DFF (8090 D, Viking) 1 min; CA — rubber points (Viking) 1 min.
- **Crystallization**: Programat EP 5000 furnace (Ivoclar Vivadent), material-specific cycle.
- **Biaxial flexure**: three 1.6 mm spheres on a 10 mm diameter circle at 120°, 1.6 mm flat-ended piston, 0.5 mm/min; **abraded surface downward (tension side)**; S = −0.2387·P·(X−Y)/d².
- **Statistics**: Kolmogorov–Smirnov; paired t-test (Ra initial vs final); independent t-test (roughness increase between materials; BFS AC vs CA within material); two-way ANOVA (material × timing) + Tukey; Weibull m and σ0 per ENV 843-5; fragment count/origin; SEM.
- **Power**: ≈1.0 (α = 0.05, n = 15, SD 34.22, max intergroup difference 129 MPa).

## Results

- **Roughness**: final Ra significantly higher than initial Ra in **all four groups** (paired t-test); the magnitude of increase did not differ significantly between ZLS and LDS in either timing scenario.
- **Biaxial flexural strength** (values stated in text): LDS-AC 244.3 MPa; ZLS-AC 232.1 MPa; ZLS-CA 115.0 MPa. LDS-CA was significantly lower than LDS-AC (mean not given in the extracted text). Abrasion after crystallization produced the lowest values for both materials — the null hypothesis (timing has no effect) was rejected.
- **Two-way ANOVA**: significant effect only for the moment of abrasion/crystallization; material and interaction not significant.
- **Weibull**: m similar across groups (overlapping CIs — no reliability difference); characteristic strength σ0 significantly lower when materials were crystallized first and adjusted afterwards.
- **Fractography**: fragment count scaled with strength (minimum 2 fragments between 50–150 MPa; maximum 6 at 280 MPa); 100% of fractures initiated at the abraded surface, which sat in tension; SEM showed subsurface cracks and visibly sharper defects in post-crystallization groups.
- **Against unadjusted benchmarks**: literature values of 343.57–378.88 MPa (LDS) and ~289.1 MPa up to 507.53 MPa (ZLS) all exceed every adjusted group here — damage was irreversible even with pre-crystallization abrasion plus polishing.
- **Clinical arithmetic**: pre-crystallization adjustment costs an extra laboratory/chairside crystallization step; post-crystallization adjustment saves the session but costs roughly half the strength in ZLS.

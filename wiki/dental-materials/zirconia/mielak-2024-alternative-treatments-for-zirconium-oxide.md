## Three-line Summary

Six surface treatments on 3Y-TZP zirconia (polishing, grinding, sandblasting, 40% HF etching, laser structuring, dry plasma etching) were compared by XRD phase ratio, surface roughness, wettability, and surface free energy; sandblasting induced the highest monoclinic phase transformation (20.8%) while chemical etching induced the least (1.8%) but produced non-wettable surfaces. Laser structuring (7.9% monoclinic) and plasma etching (4.8%) achieved a clinically viable balance of low phase transformation, adequate roughness, and good surface free energy. Laser structuring is recommended as the superior alternative: controllable geometry, economical, and adaptable to load direction.

## Summary

This in vitro study compared six zirconia surface treatment methods for use in preparing prosthetic frameworks before porcelain layering. The central question: which treatment minimizes the tetragonal-to-monoclinic (t→m) phase transformation (Tetragonal-to-Monoclinic Phase Transformation, t→m PTR) while still providing a surface suitable for ceramic bonding?

Thirty 3Y-TZP cylindrical samples (Ceramill Zi, Amann Girrbach AG) were sintered at 1450°C, then divided into six groups of five for each surface treatment. Outcomes were measured by X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) using the Garvie-Nicholson method, SEM microscopy, contact angle measurement (water and diiodomethane, Owens-Wendt surface free energy), and profilometry (ISO 21920-3:2021).

The critical finding: mechanical energy methods (sandblasting, grinding, polishing) all drove >16% monoclinic phase conversion — the same mechanism that makes them effective at roughening surfaces also makes them damaging to zirconia's microstructure. Chemical, laser, and plasma energy methods stayed below 8%. However, chemical etching produced a non-wettable surface (water contact angle 101.5°) with near-zero roughness, making it unsuitable for clinical use despite its phase-preservation advantage.

Laser structuring and plasma etching emerged as the clinically optimal methods: low t→m transformation, adequate and tunable surface roughness (~5 µm laser grooves; ~1 µm plasma etch), and favorable surface free energy (50.3 and 49.3 mJ/m² respectively). Sandblasting retains practical value for immediate ceramic bond strength, but the 20.8% monoclinic phase burden may contribute to long-term chipping and framework microcracking.

## Key Contributions

- Head-to-head six-method XRD comparison of t→m phase transformation on the same material and sintering protocol
- Demonstrates that sandblasting (clinical standard) produces the most phase transformation (20.8%), not the least
- Establishes laser structuring and plasma etching as the best compromise treatments: low phase transformation + adequate surface for ceramic bonding
- Quantifies surface free energy and its polar/dispersive components — relevant to predicting ceramic behavior at firing temperatures, not just room-temperature wettability
- Practical conclusion that plasma etching is limited by masking requirement, making laser structuring the more flexible choice

## Methodology

**Material**: Ceramill Zi 3Y-TZP (Amann Girrbach AG), sintered at 1450°C, dimensions post-sintering: 25 mm diameter, 8 mm height, ~21% shrinkage.

**Treatment groups (n=5 each)**:

| Group | Treatment |
|---|---|
| 1 | Polishing (grits 120→2500 + 3 µm diamond) |
| 2 | Grinding (defined abrasive) |
| 3 | Sandblasting (110 µm Al2O3, 0.4 MPa) |
| 4 | 40% HF chemical etching, 30 min |
| 5 | CO2 Laser structuring |
| 6 | Dry plasma etching |

**XRD**: Empyrean diffractometer (Malvern Panalytical), CuKα 1.54056 Å, 40 kV / 45 mA; phase quantification by Garvie-Nicholson (G-N) formula.

**Wettability and surface free energy**: Krüss FM40 EasyDrop, 0.3 µL drops, two liquids (distilled water, diiodomethane), Owens-Wendt model for polar (γps) and dispersive (γds) components.

**Roughness**: Mitutoyo SJ-410, ISO 21920-3:2021.

## Results

### Phase Transformation (Monoclinic % by weight, XRD G-N method)

| Treatment | Monoclinic Phase (%) |
|---|---|
| Sandblasting | 20.8 |
| Grinding | 16.9 |
| Polishing | 16.8 |
| Laser structuring | 7.9 |
| Plasma etching | 4.8 |
| Chemical etching (40% HF) | 1.8 |

The gap between mechanical and non-mechanical treatments is large (~9–19 percentage points). The authors consider values below ~8% acceptable; values around 20% represent a long-term structural risk.

### Surface Roughness and Morphology

- Sandblasting and grinding: irregularity depths ~5 µm — optimal for ceramic anchoring
- Laser structuring: groove depth ~5 µm, width adjustable by laser parameters
- Plasma etching: depth ~1 µm (5× less than sandblasting), but adjustable with process parameters; masking required for defined pattern geometry
- Polishing and chemical etching: depths in tenths-of-micrometer range — insufficient for mechanical ceramic anchoring

### Wettability and Surface Free Energy

| Treatment | Water Contact Angle (°) | Surface Free Energy (mJ/m²) |
|---|---|---|
| Sandblasting | 30.3 ± 1.6 | 64.4 ± 3.0 |
| Laser structuring | 50.3 ± 1.5 | 50.3 ± 3.8 |
| Plasma etching | 52.2 ± 1.6 | 49.3 ± 0.7 |
| Grinding | — | ~30–38 |
| Polishing | 86.8 ± 2.3 | ~30–38 |
| Chemical etching | 101.5 ± 0.7 | ~38 |

Chemical etching: polar surface free energy component = 0.9 mJ/m² — essentially no polarity, so water-based ceramic slurry will not wet the surface. Sandblasting polar component: 52.6 mJ/m² (highest); laser structuring: 27.2 mJ/m²; plasma: 24.9 mJ/m².

### Overall Ranking

For clinical use in porcelain-fused-to-zirconia frameworks, the authors rank:

1. **Laser structuring**: best overall balance — low phase transformation, good roughness, good wettability, controllable, economical
2. **Plasma etching**: similar phase/wettability advantages, but masking constraint limits flexibility
3. **Sandblasting**: best surface free energy and roughness, but highest phase transformation risk long-term
4. **Grinding**: similar to sandblasting for phase transformation
5. **Polishing**: insufficient roughness, borderline wettability
6. **Chemical etching (40% HF)**: best phase preservation but non-wettable, insufficient roughness — not clinically viable as sole treatment

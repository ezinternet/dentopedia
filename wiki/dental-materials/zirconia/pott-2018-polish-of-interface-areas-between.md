## Three-line Summary

Fifty plates each of zirconia, silicate-ceramic, and nano-hybrid composite were ground to simulate intraoral adjustment surfaces, then polished with three diamond-containing intraoral systems (Dimanto/VOCO single-step, Komet two-step, Busch two-step) or lab controls; surface roughness Ra was measured by profilometry.

All three systems reduced zirconia Ra to values statistically equivalent to glaze firing (Ra < 0.20 µm, P > .899 vs Zlab); silicate-ceramic was polished adequately only by the single-step VOCO system; composite was rougher after all intraoral systems than after lab polish, though VOCO and Komet systems were equivalent on composite.

For mixed-material interface areas, the recommended polishing sequence is: zirconia-specific polisher first → veneering-ceramic polisher → composite polisher, to match abrasive progression to material hardness.

## Summary

Rough surfaces arise on cemented all-ceramic fixed partial dentures (FPDs) from chipping fractures, occlusal adjustments, and excess cement removal. Because rough surfaces promote plaque adhesion (threshold Ra ≤ 0.20 µm, Bollen 1997) and antagonist wear, intraoral polishing is clinically necessary. When a composite repair or cementation margin spans two ceramic materials, it is not possible to selectively polish one material — the polishing instrument crosses the interface.

This in-vitro study fabricated standardized flat-plate specimens of zirconia (CAD/CAM InCoris Maxi-S), silicate-ceramic (Ivoclar Vivadent), and nano-hybrid composite (GrandioSO/VOCO), ground uniformly to Ra ≈ 0.31 µm to simulate diamond-bur–treated clinical surfaces. Three diamond-containing rubber-point systems for intraoral use were tested: Dimanto (VOCO, single-step), Komet Zirkonpoliersystem (two-step), and Busch Zirkon-/Keramikpoliersystem (two-step). Polishing was performed at 6,000 rpm, water-cooled, with standardized contact pressure and time. Lab-side glazing (ceramics) or high-gloss machine polishing (composite) served as reference standards.

**Key findings**:
- Zirconia: all three systems achieved Ra < 0.20 µm, statistically equivalent to glaze firing (not significantly different, P > .899). Intraoral polishing is a viable alternative to glaze re-firing after chairside adjustment.
- Silicate-ceramic: only the single-step Dimanto (Sv) produced acceptable results comparable to lab polishing (Sv vs Slab: P = .576 on roughness, Sv vs Sgrit: P = .487). The two-step Komet system significantly increased roughness (Sk vs Slab: P < .001), suggesting that sharper, fresher diamond points of two-step systems scratch veneering ceramic more than a worn single-step point.
- Composite: all intraoral systems left Ra higher than lab-polished Clab (P < .014). The best-performing intraoral system for composite was Dimanto, whose result did not differ significantly from Komet (P = .991), but was significantly lower than Busch (Cv vs Cb: P = .015).
- Cross-material interface: after polishing with the VOCO system, zirconia and silicate-ceramic Ra did not differ significantly (Zv vs Sv: P > .999), suggesting matched surface quality at the zirconia–veneering-ceramic interface is achievable with this system.

**Clinical recommendation**: Polish mixed-material interfaces in sequence — zirconia-specific polisher first (all systems work on zirconia), then veneering-ceramic polisher, then composite polisher. The order matters because stiffer, diamond-rich points appropriate for zirconia would increase silicate-ceramic roughness.

## Key Contributions

1. Demonstrated that all three commercially available diamond polishing systems produce zirconia Ra equivalent to glaze firing — chairside polishing replaces re-glazing.
2. Identified single-step vs two-step polishing system behavior: single-step (worn diamonds) is gentler on silicate-ceramic; two-step systems risk over-abrasion of veneering material.
3. Established material-specific polishing sequence for interface areas of multi-material FPDs.
4. Confirmed plaque-adhesion-relevant Ra ≤ 0.20 µm is achievable on zirconia with all tested systems, not on composite.
5. Provided comparative Ra data across three common restorative materials under identical experimental conditions for benchmarking.

## Methodology

- **Specimens**: 50 plates each; zirconia 8.5×6.0×2.0 mm, silicate-ceramic 12.5×10.5×2.0 mm, composite cast in silicone moulds of zirconia plates
- **Baseline grinding**: 165 µm SiC grit, PowerPro 5000, 50 N force, water cooling → uniform Ra ≈ 0.31 µm
- **Polishing systems**: Dimanto (VOCO, 1-step), Komet Dental Zirkonpoliersystem (2-step), Busch Zirkon-/Keramikpoliersystem (2-step); all at 6,000 rpm, water cooling, 2 N × 30 s then 1 N × 30 s
- **Measurement**: Dektak 150 profilometer; 5 × 3,600 µm scans, 0.33 µm resolution; Ra computed
- **Statistics**: Kolmogorov-Smirnov + Levene + ANOVA + Scheffé; α = .05

## Results

|---|---|---|
| Zv, Zk, Zb vs Zgrit | P < .001 | All polishing systems significantly reduced zirconia Ra |
| Zv, Zk, Zb vs Zlab | P > .899 | Equivalent to glaze firing |

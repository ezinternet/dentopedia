## Three-line Summary

Systematic review integrating 7 studies (2009–2024; polysomnographic, cross-sectional, prospective cohort, laboratory-experimental designs) on the association between sleep disorders and temporomandibular joint pain dysfunction syndrome in adults.

TMD patients consistently show worse sleep quality and higher pain sensitivity than controls; sleep apnea symptoms predicted increased TMD risk in a large cohort (OPPERA); degenerative TMJ changes were linked to stress and poor sleep; up to ~90% of TMD patients report poor sleep vs ~15% of non-TMD population; but bruxism–TMD and OSA–TMD associations were inconsistent across studies.

Clinically supports routine sleep screening (e.g., PSQI, STOP-Bang) and multidisciplinary (dental + sleep medicine + psychological) management of TMD; key limitation is the review's reliance on cross-sectional, mostly self-reported/small-sample primary studies with no meta-analysis, limiting causal inference.

## 세줄요약

2009~2024년 발표된 7편(수면다원검사·단면연구·전향적 코호트·실험연구)을 통합한 체계적 문헌고찰로, 성인의 수면장애와 측두하악관절 통증기능장애증후군(TMD) 간 연관성을 다룸.

TMD 환자는 대조군보다 일관되게 수면의 질이 나쁘고 통증 민감도가 높았으며, 대규모 코호트(OPPERA)에서 수면무호흡 증상이 TMD 발생 위험 증가의 유의한 예측인자였고, 퇴행성 TMJ 변화는 스트레스·불량한 수면과 연관됨(TMD 환자의 최대 약 90%가 수면불량 보고, 비TMD군은 약 15%); 다만 이갈이(bruxism)-TMD 및 폐쇄성수면무호흡(OSA)-TMD 연관성은 연구 간 비일관적.

임상적으로 TMD 치료 시 PSQI·STOP-Bang 등을 이용한 정기적 수면 선별검사와 치과·수면의학·심리치료를 아우르는 다학제적 접근을 지지하나, 메타분석 없이 대부분 단면적·자가보고·소규모 원저 연구에 기반해 인과관계 추론에는 한계가 있음.

## Summary

This systematic review synthesizes 7 studies (2009–2024) examining the relationship between sleep disorders (poor sleep quality, insomnia, obstructive sleep apnea, and sleep bruxism) and temporomandibular joint pain dysfunction syndrome (TMD) in adults. Search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science; included studies were observational/comparative, assessing sleep either subjectively (questionnaires) or objectively (polysomnography). Findings converge on a reciprocal relationship: TMD patients show consistently worse sleep quality and higher pain sensitivity than controls, sleep apnea symptoms predict TMD risk in a large prospective cohort, and degenerative TMJ changes correlate with stress and poor sleep. However, the bruxism–TMD and OSA–TMD associations specifically remain inconsistent across the literature, a nuance the authors are careful to flag rather than overstate. The review proposes a plausible mechanistic pathway — sleep disturbance aggravating pain via descending pain-modulation dysregulation, central sensitization, and inflammation/cortisol elevation, with untreated OSA additionally contributing hypoxia and systemic inflammation — and recommends routine sleep screening (PSQI, STOP-Bang) plus multidisciplinary management (dental, sleep medicine, psychological/CBT-I) for TMD patients.

## Key Contributions

- Consolidates cross-design evidence (polysomnographic, cross-sectional, prospective cohort OPPERA, laboratory-experimental) into a single narrative synthesis of the sleep–TMD relationship.
- Quantifies the sleep-disturbance burden in TMD: up to ~90% of TMD patients report poor sleep vs. ~15% of the general population.
- Distinguishes the strength of evidence by sleep-disorder subtype: poor sleep quality–TMD association is described as relatively uniform, whereas bruxism–TMD and OSA–TMD associations are heterogeneous/inconsistent — an important qualifier for clinicians not to over-generalize the bruxism-driver narrative.
- Proposes a clinical mechanistic model (pain modulation, central sensitization, cortisol/inflammation, OSA-hypoxia) linking sleep disruption to TMD symptom severity.
- Recommends integrating sleep screening instruments (PSQI, STOP-Bang) into routine TMD workups and pursuing multidisciplinary management.

## Methodology

Systematic literature search (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science) for English-language, adult-population, observational/comparative studies assessing both a sleep disorder (subjective or objective/polysomnographic) and a TMD outcome. Case reports, reviews, animal studies, and studies lacking clear sleep or TMD outcome measures were excluded. 7 studies (2009–2024) met inclusion criteria; data were extracted on design, population, sleep-disorder type assessed, and key findings, and synthesized narratively with a single comparative summary table. No meta-analysis or pooled effect size was computed, and no formal risk-of-bias/PRISMA methodology is detailed in the retrieved full text — this is a narrative/qualitative systematic review rather than a quantitative SR+MA.

## Results

| Author (year) | Design | Population | Sleep measure | Key finding |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dubrovsky | Polysomnographic | Women with TMD | Sleep continuity, respiratory events | Poorer sleep quality, altered respiratory parameters |
| Lee | Cross-sectional | Chronic TMD vs. healthy controls | Sleep quality questionnaires | Significantly poorer sleep quality in TMD |
| Sanders | Prospective cohort (OPPERA) | Community cohort | Sleep apnea symptoms | Sleep apnea strongly associated with increased TMD risk |
| Smith | Laboratory experimental | TMD patients | Sleep disorders and pain sensitivity | Sleep disorders linked with higher laboratory pain sensitivity |
| Yap | Cross-sectional | Degenerative TMJ patients | Sleep quality and emotional disturbance | Degenerative TMJ disease associated with disturbed sleep and emotional distress |
| Dias | Cross-sectional | Patients with bruxism and TMJ changes | Sleep bruxism, sleep quality | Bruxism associated with low sleep quality and TMJ degeneration |
| Tran | Comparative study | TMJ degeneration group vs. controls | Sleep quality | Poor sleep quality significantly associated with degenerative TMJ changes |

A cited related SR (Dreweck et al.) confirmed a robust association between painful TMD and poor sleep quality across diagnostic methods/populations. A cited SR specific to TMJ osteoarthritis (Sánchez Romero et al.) found insufficient evidence linking that condition to increased sleep disorders — flagged by the authors as a condition-specific exception. Limitations: cross-sectional predominance, small samples, heterogeneous diagnostic criteria, mostly self-reported sleep measures (recall bias), scarce longitudinal/intervention studies limiting causal inference.

## Three-line Summary
Retrospective cohort study (n=260) from a tertiary oral and maxillofacial surgery emergency unit in Helsinki, Finland, over 14 years (Jan 2007–Oct 2020), selected from 800 ICD-coded records; the primary outcome was recurrent vs non-recurrent TMJ luxation, secondary outcomes were need for and type of surgical intervention.
61.9% of luxations were recurrent, 40.0% spontaneous and 36.2% caused by yawning/vomiting/eating; 57.3% were bilateral, 94.2% could not close the mouth, manual reduction was attempted in 91.9% and needed no medication in 69.2%, and only 1.9% (n=5) underwent eminectomy — any general condition carried a 1.57-fold (95% CI 1.27–1.94, P<0.001) and a neurological condition a 1.34-fold (1.12–1.60, P=0.003) risk of recurrence.
Acute reduction should be treated as a temporising measure with neurological/geriatric collaboration, since recurrence — not cure — is the expected course; the key limitation is single-centre retrospective capture that excludes luxations reduced in private dental offices, understating both recurrence and severity.

## Summary

This 2023 retrospective cohort study from the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Diseases, University of Helsinki and Helsinki University Hospital, examines 260 patients with confirmed TMJ luxation treated at a tertiary oral and maxillofacial surgery emergency unit between January 2007 and October 2020 — the largest single-centre cohort on the causes of luxation, and the quantitative answer to a literature the authors describe as "mainly based on case-reports". It was designed around a simple but clinically decisive question: is recurrent luxation the exception or the rule, and what predicts it? The answer is that recurrence is the rule — 61.9% of patients had at least two dislocations in their lifetime — and that it is predicted by comorbidity rather than trauma: any general condition carried a 1.57-fold risk of recurrence and a neurological condition a 1.34-fold risk, both significant, while geriatric age and sex were not. Luxation was most often spontaneous (40.0%) or triggered by yawning, vomiting or eating (36.2%), was bilateral in 57.3%, and traumatic causes accounted for only 6.2% — far below the 20–60% quoted from earlier series. Treatment was overwhelmingly conservative: manual reduction was attempted in 91.9% of patients and succeeded without any medication in 69.2%, i.v. sedation was the commonest adjunct, and eminectomy was performed in only 1.9%. For the acute-reduction topic this page anchors, the implication is that a successful reduction is not a resolution — the paper explicitly frames acute repositioning of a recurrent luxation as a temporary treatment requiring preventive measures, and links the high mortality (28.1% overall; 68.5% of the recurrent group) to the need for neurological and geriatric collaboration.

## Key Contributions

- **Recurrent luxation is the majority presentation (161/260, 61.9%)** — measured against a lifetime criterion of ≥2 dislocations, not a follow-up window, making the estimate immune to the short-follow-up problem that makes most recurrence figures incomparable.
- **Neurological comorbidity is a quantified recurrence risk factor, not an anecdotal one** — RR 1.34 (95% CI 1.12–1.60, P=0.003); any general condition RR 1.57 (1.27–1.94, P<0.001). Geriatric age (RR 1.15, P=.655) and female sex (RR 1.09, P=.365) were *not* significant despite the age and sex imbalance, which separates the true predictor from the demographic noise.
- **Recurrence and mechanism are separable** — mechanism differs sharply between groups (P=0.003): spontaneous in 71.2% of recurrent vs 28.8% of non-recurrent patients, while injury caused 81.2% of non-recurrent luxations. A traumatic first dislocation and a spontaneous recurrent one are different clinical entities.
- **Establishes the real-world treatment distribution for acute luxation** — 91.9% manual reduction attempt, 69.2% successful with no medication, aftercare dominated by jaw bandage (55.4%), mouth-opening restriction (55.0%) and soft diet (46.5%), nightguard in 7.3%, BoNT-A in 1.2%, surgery in 1.9%.
- **Mortality as a prognostic signal** — 28.1% of the cohort died within the study period; mean time to death from primary contact was 884 days (range 1–4664) in the recurrent group vs 489 days (6–3597) in the non-recurrent group, prompting the authors' conclusion that recurrent luxation should prompt careful evaluation of the *benefit* of major surgery in a population with limited life expectancy.

## Methodology

- **Design**: Retrospective cohort / chart review, single tertiary centre (Töölö Hospital Emergency Department, HUH; catchment >1.6 million)
- **Period**: 1 January 2007 – 20 October 2020 (14 years)
- **Case ascertainment**: electronic record search on 5 ICD codes (S03.4, S03.0, S03.5, K07.62, K07.69) → 800 records manually re-evaluated → 260 confirmed TMJ dislocation cases
- **Primary outcome**: recurrent vs non-recurrent luxation (recurrent = ≥2 dislocations in a lifetime)
- **Secondary outcomes**: need for, and type of, surgical intervention
- **Predictors**: age, sex, neurological condition, mechanism (spontaneous / yawning-vomiting-eating / dentist visit or gastroscopy / injury / medical seizure / habit or exercise)
- **Also recorded**: other general conditions, medication, living conditions, referral unit, clinical findings, radiographic interventions, manual reduction, aftercare, mortality during the study period
- **Analysis**: descriptive statistics; chi-square test; 2×2 tables for risk-ratio with 95% CI
- **Ethics**: HUH Head and Neck Center review board approval; consent waived (retrospective)

## Clinical Implications

- **Reduction ≠ cure.** With 61.9% lifetime recurrence, the emergency clinician's job is not only to relocate the condyle but to hand over a recurrence-prevention plan. This paper's own framing: acute repositioning of a recurrent luxation "can only be considered a temporary treatment".
- **Screen for neurological disease at the first luxation.** A neurological condition raised recurrence risk 1.34-fold and was present in 31.2% of the cohort. Given the mechanism proposed (altered muscular tonus, with epilepsy, connective-tissue disorders and oromandibular dystonia all described as risk factors), a neurological history is an actionable predictor, not background noise.
- **Trauma is not the expected mechanism — do not over-image on that assumption.** Injury accounted for 6.2% versus 20–60% in earlier series. Imaging was used in only ~32% and the diagnosis was clinical in the majority; the authors nonetheless flag that the adequacy of *differential* diagnosis (mandibular/condylar fracture exclusion) is unresolved and call for standardised diagnostic measures.
- **The reduction technique itself is low-barrier.** 69.2% succeeded with no medication at all, and over half of referrals came from general practitioners. The authors suggest the reason patients reach an emergency department is a lack of expertise at the referring centre — training rather than escalation is the lever.
- **Beware of the BoNT-A evidence gap in practice.** BoNT-A into the masticatory muscles was considered for 6 patients and given to 3 (1.2%), while the most-cited recurrence-prevention option in the wider literature is a BoNT-A injection targeting the lateral pterygoid. This cohort documents a large implementation gap, and cannot support any efficacy inference about the injection.
- **Eminectomy is rare and its benefit must be weighed against prognosis.** 15 patients had surgery planned; it was cancelled for anaesthetic contraindications in some, and five patients died before their planned surgery. The authors' explicit conclusion: major surgical intervention and its benefits "should carefully be evaluated, due to the potentially limited lifetime in geriatric patients with neurological conditions who get recurrent TMJ luxation".

## Limitations

- Retrospective design; no prospective prosthetic-treatment data (self-declared weakness)
- Single clinic and period — some patients likely received prior treatment elsewhere, so the recurrent count "might be even higher"
- Luxations reduced in other units (e.g. dentists' offices) were excluded, so the university-hospital sample skews toward more challenging cases
- Unstandardised diagnostic practice; imaging used in under a third of cases, raising an unresolved fracture-exclusion question
- No control group; RR estimates are for recurrence within a luxation cohort, not for the risk of luxation in the general population
- No efficacy inference possible for the preventive interventions, whose usage was too sparse (BoNT-A 1.2%, surgery 1.9%)

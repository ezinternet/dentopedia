## Three-line Summary

Uncontrolled retrospective case series of 42 patients (from 45 acute/chronic anterior TMJ dislocations treated 2011–2018 at the University of Buenos Aires dental emergency service; trauma cases excluded) describing a simplified non-traumatic manual reduction technique: seated patient, operator thumbs behind the last lower molars, gentle steady 45° downward-posterior pressure held 1–2 min, then patient-performed opening/closing and lateral jaw movements — no sedation or local/general anaesthesia, one operator only.

Reduction (full condylar repositioning into the glenoid fossa) succeeded in 100% of cases, taking 1.5–7 min in 40 of 42 patients; 12 cases needed up to 3 repetitions of the whole cycle (two 1-min rest intervals) and the 2 hardest cases — both referred after failed reduction elsewhere, dislocated for 2 h and 3 h — took up to 15 min and 6 repetitions; all 42 patients reported no pain during the manoeuvre.

A 23/42 (55%) subgroup arrived after ≥1 failed Hippocratic attempt elsewhere, so the series is enriched for exactly the patients in whom repeat conventional reduction usually fails — but with no control group, no force measurement and no numeric pain scoring, the 100% figure is a single-arm success rate that cannot establish superiority over the conventional method.

## Summary

Stolbizer, Saiegh and Andrada (University of Buenos Aires) report a retrospective, uncontrolled case series of **42 patients** with acute or chronic **anterior mandibular dislocation (AMD)** treated between 2011 and 2018 in a dental-school emergency service, describing a simplified non-traumatic manual reduction technique. The patient sits; the operator stands in front and places the thumbs behind the last lower molars — not on the occlusal surface, deliberately, so the patient cannot bite the operator — and applies a **gentle, steady 45° downward-posterior pressure for 1–2 min** while talking the patient through the fact that no brusque manoeuvre will occur. Crucially the operator never varies the intensity or direction of the pressure, because any such change is read by the patient as a warning and triggers muscle contraction; instead the **patient** then performs up to 5 opening/closing movements followed by up to 5 lateral jaw movements, and it is these active movements, performed with the elevator muscles relaxed, that reposition the condyles. All 42 reductions were successful; 40 of 42 took 1.5–7 min, 12 needed up to 3 repetitions of the cycle, and the 2 most difficult — both referred from other centres after failed reduction, dislocated for 2 h and 3 h — took up to 15 min and 6 repetitions. No sedation, local or general anaesthesia was used, and no patient reported pain. Because 23 of the 42 (55%) were referrals that had already failed Hippocratic reduction elsewhere, the series is enriched for the difficult cases, but the absence of a control group, of force quantification and of numeric pain scoring means the 100% success rate cannot be read as superiority over the conventional method.

## Key Contributions

- **Patient-driven reduction under non-varying passive pressure.** The single innovation is that the operator holds a constant, gentle 45° downward-posterior load and the *patient* moves the jaw. Because the elevator muscles stay relaxed, the opening/closing and lateral movements themselves induce natural condylar repositioning — the inverse of the conventional method, in which force stretches the muscle and the stretch reflex (myotatic reflex) snaps the mouth shut.
- **Fear and tension identified as the real obstacle to repeat reduction.** The authors argue that once a Hippocratic attempt has hurt, the patient's protective anticipatory tensing makes subsequent attempts likely to fail — a patient-comfort argument, not merely a force argument, for changing the technique.
- **Operator-safety modification.** Thumbs behind rather than on the last molars (no occlusal contact) to prevent being bitten; the authors also note the gentle approach reduces the risk of sudden masseter spasm.
- **Largest reported series for this indication, by the authors' account** — 42 patients versus the isolated cases or 7–20-patient series behind the other published variants (device-assisted, general-anaesthetic, traction, or surgical approaches), none of which are statistical comparisons either.
- **Unilateral variant specified**: thumbs are still placed bilaterally, but pressure is applied only on the dislocated side.
- **A simplification rather than an escalation.** The authors' stated rationale is that adding complexity to a method already proven effective is only worth it if it buys patient, operator, time or cost benefit — while the reverse (simplification) is always worth it if the simplified version is at least as effective.

## Methodology

- **Design**: uncontrolled retrospective case series / technique report; single centre (Emergency Service, School of Dentistry, University of Buenos Aires), single team, no comparator arm, no randomisation, no blinding.
- **Population**: 42 patients (male and female) with acute or chronic anterior TMJ dislocation, drawn from 45 TMJD cases treated 2011–2018 out of 394,000 patients who received oral care at the School of Dentistry in that period. **Trauma-related dislocations were excluded.** Ethics Committee Resolution 026/2011, Declaration of Helsinki, written informed consent.
- **Procedure conditions**: outpatient, **one emergency physician operator**, no procedural sedation and no local or general anaesthesia in any case.
- **Recorded outcomes**: time to achieve condylar repositioning; number of times the procedure had to be repeated; subjective pain perception (mild / moderate / severe).
- **Success definition**: full repositioning of the condyle in the glenoid cavity.
- **Comparator**: none — the authors name the missing Hippocratic-method control group as the report's principal limitation.

## Results

|---|---|

The technique, step by step: (1) patient seated, operator standing in front; (2) thumbs behind the last lower molars, not on the occlusal surface; (3) gentle, steady pressure directed downward and posteriorly at 45°, held 1–2 min while the operator speaks and reassures — **no change in intensity or direction at any point**; (4) without releasing the pressure, the patient performs up to 5 opening/closing movements, then up to 5 lateral jaw movements; (5) if not reduced, repeat step 4 once; (6) if still not reduced, rest ~1 min and repeat the manoeuvre.

Contextual figures the authors quote: TMJ dislocation accounts for **3% of all reported dislocated joints in the body**; it is more frequent in women in their second and third decades; reduction times reported across the competing techniques range from "few seconds" to **10 min**, and this series falls inside that band.

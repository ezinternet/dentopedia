## Three-line Summary

Single-centre, single-operator retrospective case series (83 consecutive acute TMJ dislocation patients, January 2017 – December 2022, Jiangxi Provincial People's Hospital emergency department) of a two-step patient-guided reduction (PGR) manoeuvre: the operator holds gentle sustained downward thumb traction on the external oblique ridges while the patient opens the mouth with head extension (Step 1) and then closes the mouth with head flexion (Step 2) — no sedation, no anaesthesia.

100% of 83 dislocations met the pre-defined primary outcome of complete condylar repositioning within the protocol's 5-minute window (simple dislocations n = 81 fully reduced; the 2 condylar-fracture cases achieved temporary palliative alignment only, as a bridge to surgery) at a mean reduction time of 150 ± 51.78 s (range 28–280 s, median 146 s, IQR 124–183 s), with no case reaching the 300-s ceiling, no iatrogenic injury, and success in all 12 patients (14.5%) who had already failed ≥2 Hippocratic attempts elsewhere.

Uncontrolled, single-operator and pain-unmeasured (chart descriptors only, no VAS/NRS), with 12 lateral cases (14.5%) bypassing the imaging-first protocol and a 30-day return window that cannot exclude recurrence treated elsewhere — a promising technique report, not a demonstrated advance on the 86.2–86.7% Hippocratic success rate it benchmarks against.

## Summary

Lin et al. (2026) report a retrospective case series of **83 consecutive patients** with acute temporomandibular joint dislocation treated over six years (Jan 2017 – Dec 2022) in a single Chinese emergency department by one senior oral and maxillofacial surgeon. The manoeuvre is a **patient-guided reduction (PGR)**: the operator maintains gentle, continuous downward traction with both thumbs placed intraorally as posteriorly as possible on the external oblique ridges, and the *patient* supplies the movement — mouth opening combined with head extension to unlock the joint (Step 1), then mouth closing with head flexion to guide the condyle back into the articular fossa (Step 2). The proposed mechanism is that synchronised low-force traction with voluntary movement relaxes the masseter and medial pterygoid (avoiding the myotonic reflex that sudden forceful pressure provokes) while the head movement dynamically alters the condylar-skull angle so the condyle clears the articular eminence. Against a pre-defined primary endpoint — complete repositioning within 5 minutes without sedation or anaesthesia — the series reports **100% success in all 83 cases** (81 simple dislocations fully reduced; 2 condylar-fracture cases only palliatively aligned pending surgery) at a mean of **150 ± 51.78 s** (range 28–280 s; median 146 s, IQR 124–183 s), with **no** case hitting the 300-s limit, **no** sedation required in any simple case, and no iatrogenic injury. Notably the technique also succeeded in 12 patients (14.5%) who had already failed at least two Hippocratic attempts elsewhere. The weight of this evidence is limited: no control arm, one operator, pain recorded only as chart narrative, a 14.5% deviation from the imaging-first protocol, and a 30-day return window that cannot capture recurrence treated elsewhere — so it is best read as a well-documented technique report with a plausible and easily taught biomechanical rationale, not as evidence of superiority over the conventional Hippocratic method.

## Key Contributions

- **Couples head movement to jaw movement in opposition.** Unlike patient-driven variants that only add jaw opening/closing and lateral movements, the distinctive element here is that mouth opening is paired with head extension and mouth closing with head flexion, actively modifying the condylar-skull angle so the condyle bypasses the articular eminence on re-entry.
- **A pre-specified success ceiling removes censoring ambiguity.** Success requires complete repositioning inside a 5-minute window without procedural sedation or anaesthesia, with a mandated fallback to conventional manual reduction under local anaesthesia or sedation. No case reached the 300-s limit, so the authors can state the 100% rate is not censoring-biased.
- **Quantified rescue of genuinely refractory cases.** All 12 patients (14.5%) referred after ≥2 failed Hippocratic attempts at other facilities were reduced, a mean of 4.2 h (range 2–12 h) after the last failed attempt — the only quasi-comparative signal against the conventional method in the paper.
- **Extraoral variant with a time signal.** In 3 anterior cases (3.6%) with extreme intraoral sensitivity, the same movement protocol with external pressure over the condylar area reportedly reduced in ≈30 s while removing the clinician's exposure to being bitten — flagged by the authors as preliminary.
- **Scope beyond the anterior type.** 16 lateral (19.3%) and 5 posterior (6.0%) dislocations were managed by the same protocol and illustrated with CT, extending claimed applicability to the atypical directions that are usually high-energy-trauma presentations.
- **Zero-anesthesia pathway.** No simple case required sedation or anaesthesia (pharmacological intervention was confined to the 2 fracture cases), removing the escalation ladder of auriculotemporal/intra-articular block, muscle relaxants, and general anesthesia with paralytics that the conventional approach may demand.

## Methodology

- **Design**: retrospective case series, single emergency department (Jiangxi Provincial People's Hospital), January 2017 – December 2022; consecutive inclusion of all eligible patients treated in the period. No sample-size pre-calculation — the authors state the cohort was assembled "aiming for sufficient clinical representation".
- **n**: 83 patients (59 F / 24 M); mean age 44.64 ± 18.87 y (range 19–85 y). Inclusion required age ≥16 y.
- **Inclusion / exclusion**: acute TMJ dislocation with written consent; excluded were TMJ tumour or systemic TMJ disease, chronic dislocation, previous TMJ surgery, and cognitive impairment preventing instruction-following.
- **Imaging protocol**: CT/X-ray standard for suspected traumatic dislocation, with a pragmatic exception for 12 lateral cases in severe distress with a clear history of failed prior reduction — post-reduction CT was obtained in all 12 to confirm repositioning and exclude occult fracture.
- **Primary outcome**: successful reduction = complete anatomical repositioning of the condyle into the articular fossa within a 5-minute observation window, without procedural sedation or anaesthesia.
- **Secondary outcome**: reduction time, timed by an **independent observer (Fei Lin)** from onset of sustained downward traction (Step 1) to audible/palpable condyle re-entry (end of Step 2).
- **Operator**: all 83 procedures performed by one senior clinician with >20 years of oral and maxillofacial surgery experience, deliberately to hold technical constant during initial validation; investigators completed calibration sessions for technique and data recording.
- **Post-reduction care**: soft diet 2 weeks, no wide yawning or hard foods, chin support while yawning, PRN analgesics, immediate discharge without mandatory scheduled follow-up; secondary EMR review up to 6 months.
- **Analysis**: descriptive only — no control group, no randomisation, no blinding, no statistical testing.

**Etiology** (sums to 83): excessive mouth opening 65 (yawning 39, laughing 23, biting hard objects 3) · iatrogenic 10 · blunt trauma 5 · drug-induced dystonia 3. Mean symptom duration on arrival 4 h (range 1–12 h).

**Benchmarks cited**: TMJ dislocation incidence ≈25 per 100,000/year; Hippocratic maneuver success **86.2–86.7%**, perceived as unpleasant, ergonomically costly, and carrying operator thumb-injury risk via the myotonic reflex. Note the 150-s mean here is faster than most published alternatives, but on a single-arm cohort with a lenient definition (no sedation requirement) and a selected operator.

**Proposed mechanism (authors label it theoretical — no cine-MRI, no EMG)**: (1) gentle sustained traction synchronised with voluntary mouth opening and head extension relaxes the mandibular elevators (masseter, medial pterygoid), removing protective guarding without provoking the stretch reflex; (2) coordinated head extension/flexion modifies the condylar-skull angle to a favourable re-entry orientation while the patient's own neck strength individualises the traction force; (3) minimising external force and using patient proprioception is argued to spare the TMJ capsule and temporomandibular ligament from over-extension, preserving post-reduction stability.

## Clinical Bottom Line

For an acute TMJ dislocation presenting to an emergency or dental service, this is a **cheap, teachable, two-step alternative to the Hippocratic maneuver** that in one 83-patient single-operator series achieved 100% reduction in a mean of 150 s, with no anaesthesia, no sedation, and no iatrogenic injury — including in patients who had already failed repeated Hippocratic attempts. The head-movement coupling is the teachable novel element and has an intelligible rationale (relax the elevators, clear the eminence, use the patient's own neck strength). It should nevertheless be adopted as an **additional option rather than a replacement**: there is no control arm, no force or pain quantification, the single-operator design inflates the success rate, and the 30-day return window cannot exclude recurrence handled elsewhere. Two hard limits apply — **pre-reduction CT/MRI is mandatory in any traumatic presentation**, and the technique is **not recommended** where fracture is suspected or confirmed (the 2 fracture cases here were palliative alignment only, pending surgery). And it does nothing about the underlying anatomy — ligamentous laxity or articular eminence morphology that drives recurrent dislocation is untouched, so a recurrent or chronic history still calls for the separate long-standing-dislocation pathway.

## Three-line Summary

Narrative review (keyword search across PubMed/Web of Science/Scopus through September 2023; no PRISMA flow, no risk-of-bias assessment, no meta-analysis) on psoriatic arthritis (PsA) involvement of the TMJ, covering pathogenesis, diagnostic signs, therapy, and a tentative treatment of sex/gender differences.

Pathogenesis is HLA-linked (HLA-B27, HLA-Cw6, IL23R) and driven by TNF-α/IL-17/IL-23-mediated synovial hypoxia-angiogenesis-erosion, with enthesitis and proliferative bone lesions serving as the key radiological differentiator from TMJ osteoarthritis (central-only erosion) and rheumatoid arthritis (faster degeneration course); the authors state directly that epidemiological data on TMJ involvement in PsA "are hard to find" and that therapeutic-management and quality-of-life studies are few.

Therapy runs pharmacology-first (NSAIDs/steroids → methotrexate → anti-TNF/IL-12-23/IL-17 biologics) with surgery reserved for refractory disease; the paper's actionable conclusion is that PsA-TMJ demands multidisciplinary coordination (rheumatology, dermatology, dentistry, plus gynecology for reproductive-age or pregnant patients) — a patient with known psoriasis and new TMJ symptoms is a referral conversation, not a splint-and-wait situation.

## Summary

According to PubMed, this narrative review ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/dj12020031); PMID 38392235; PMCID PMC10887631) examines psoriatic arthritis (PsA) involving the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), covering general PsA pathogenesis, PsA-specific TMJ pathogenesis, clinical presentation, diagnostic approach, therapeutic strategies, and a dedicated section on eventual sex/gender differences. PsA is a chronic autoimmune arthritis associated with psoriasis, affecting roughly 30% of psoriasis patients (general-population PsA prevalence 0.1–1%), typically presenting between ages 30–50. Joint involvement of any kind is reported in about 25% of combined psoriasis/PsA patients, and TMDs are described as more frequent in psoriatic patients than in the general population — but the authors are explicit that TMJ-specific epidemiological data "are hard to find" and that few studies address therapeutic management or the condition's quality-of-life impact. This is the wiki's first page on psoriatic arthritis and the third inflammatory-arthritis entity represented in the TMJ category, alongside rheumatoid arthritis and juvenile idiopathic arthritis.

**Tier and scope boundary.** This is a narrative review: search terms and inclusion/exclusion criteria are stated and dual-reviewer screening (with a third-author tiebreak) is described, but there is no PRISMA flow diagram, no risk-of-bias tool, and no meta-analysis. It can support claims about pathogenesis, diagnostic signs, and clinical awareness. It cannot support claims about TMJ-PsA prevalence, diagnostic accuracy, or treatment effect sizes — the paper itself says this evidence largely does not yet exist in the literature.

## Key Contributions

- Articulates PsA-TMJ pathogenesis as HLA- (HLA-B27, HLA-Cw6, IL23R) and cytokine- (TNF-α, IL-17, IL-23) driven, proceeding through synovial angiogenesis → tissue hypoxia despite increased vascularity → fibroblast-like synoviocyte proliferation (a "tumor-like" invasive pattern) → cartilage fibrillation/chondrocyte apoptosis + osteoclast-mediated bone erosion, with IL-22 additionally driving osteoblastic bone proliferation — so erosive and proliferative bone change coexist, a PsA hallmark.
- States a specific radiological differentiator: in osteoarthritis, erosions localize to the central portion of the condyle; in PsA, erosive lesions can occur centrally *or* at the articular-surface margins, with enthesitis/proliferative bone lesions being PsA-typical; rheumatoid arthritis is distinguished by a faster degeneration course (per Wang et al., as cited in the source).
- Lays out a PsA-TMJ therapy ladder distinct from the conservative-first ladder used for degenerative TMJ disease: NSAIDs/steroids first-line → methotrexate for moderate-severe disease → biologics (anti-TNF: infliximab, etanercept, adalimumab, golimumab; IL-12/23: ustekinumab; IL-17: brodalumab) → surgery (arthrocentesis, arthroplasty, condylotomy, custom total TMJ replacement) reserved for refractory cases.
- Names its own evidence gap directly rather than papering over it: TMJ-specific epidemiological data on PsA involvement "are hard to find," and few studies exist on therapeutic management or quality-of-life impact.
- Frames management as inherently multidisciplinary — rheumatology, dermatology, and dentistry, plus gynecology/obstetrics for reproductive-age or pregnant patients, given limited pregnancy/breastfeeding safety data for DMARDs and biologics and PsA's variable disease course around hormonal state.

## Methodology

Narrative/comprehensive review, not a systematic review or meta-analysis. Databases searched: PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, using terms "Psoriasis," "Psoriatic arthritis," "Temporomandibular joint," "Temporomandibular disorders," "Pathogenesis," and "Gender differences," limited to English-language items published through September 2023. Included study types: clinical studies, epidemiological studies, reviews/systematic reviews, case series, and case reports; excluded: conference proceedings, journal abstracts, and non-English articles. Two authors independently screened items, with a third author resolving disagreements; where information was unclear, corresponding authors of source studies were contacted and items excluded if clarification could not be obtained. No PRISMA flow diagram, formal risk-of-bias assessment, or meta-analysis is reported.

## Results

**Pathogenesis.** General PsA pathogenesis involves HLA-locus genetic susceptibility interacting with environmental triggers to activate inflammatory pathways; synovial-membrane inflammation with angiogenesis (tortuous, dilated, immature vessels) paradoxically produces tissue hypoxia, which activates cytokine release (IL-17, IL-22, IL-23) and drives fibroblast-like synoviocyte proliferation and invasion. The resulting cartilage fibrillation, chondrocyte apoptosis, and osteoclast-mediated bone erosion narrow the joint space (a pathognomonic radiographic sign); IL-22 simultaneously up-regulates osteogenic factors (Wnt-3a, BMP4), producing coexisting erosive and proliferative bone change. Mechanistically, this enthesitis/bone-marrow-edema-driven, cytokine-mediated process is distinct from the mechanical/degenerative-loading pathophysiology described for TMJ osteoarthritis — the paper's own diagnostic-differentiation section makes this contrast explicit via the erosion-location rule (central-only in OA vs. central-or-marginal in PsA) and the enthesitis/proliferative-bone-lesion sign as PsA-typical.

**Clinical presentation and diagnosis.** TMJ-PsA presents with pain (localized or radiating to face/temple/neck), joint swelling/synovitis, crepitus, limited jaw movement (including a "locked"/"stuck" sensation), and downstream effects on eating and speech. Diagnostic clinical signs include arthralgia, crepitus, and rarely bilateral ankylosis; nonspecific accompaniments include general orofacial pain, headache, masticatory myalgia, and sleep disturbance. Diagnosis rests on medical history (psoriasis history specifically), physical examination, and radiological evidence: cortical erosion, articular-space narrowing, flattening of articular surfaces, and osteophytic lesions, with CT for bone detail and MRI for soft-tissue/bone-marrow-edema assessment. Because clinical presentation overlaps with degenerative osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis, the radiological erosion-location rule and disease-course speed (RA progresses faster) are used for differentiation; genetic and rheumatoid-factor analysis supplements diagnosis when psoriasis history is negative.

**Therapy.** First-line treatment is pharmacological (NSAIDs and steroids), escalating to methotrexate for moderate-severe disease (with intraarticular administration an option), then to biologic therapy (anti-TNF agents, IL-12/23 inhibitor ustekinumab, IL-17 inhibitor brodalumab) for patients who cannot tolerate or fail NSAIDs/methotrexate. Anti-TNF therapy is noted to carry reported side effects including osteonecrosis of the jaw, a specific concern for TMD outcomes. Co-adjuvant gnathological and physical therapy (including occlusal therapy to equilibrate joint loading) may help, distinguishing PsA-TMD management from purely conservative-first TMD therapy by requiring systemic pharmacological control as the foundation. Surgery — arthrocentesis, arthroplasty, condylotomy, or custom total TMJ replacement — is reserved for severe, pharmacologically refractory cases and requires multidisciplinary team evaluation (rheumatology plus oral/maxillofacial surgery).

**Sex/gender differences — reported carefully, not extrapolated beyond what the paper states.** The paper itself frames this as tentative ("eventual gender differences" in the title). On general PsA sex distribution, the cited literature is contradictory: one study (Love et al.) found a higher female:male ratio in an Icelandic population, while another (Nossent et al.) found male predominance — the review does not resolve this. For clinical pattern, males are reported to show more peripheral-joint involvement while females show a higher prevalence of axial-pattern involvement — and the TMJ is grouped by the authors into this axial category alongside the spine and sacroiliac joints, without an independently cited TMJ-specific dataset supporting that grouping. Females are reported to experience higher pain, fatigue, and psychological distress; HLA-B27 (axial-associated) is reported more prevalent in males. Separately, the review draws on general-TMD (not PsA-specific) sex-hormone literature — Stinson et al. (estrogen affects condylar morphology/health), Hauru et al. (low estradiol linked to altered condylar cartilage proteoglycan levels via FTIR imaging), Christidis et al. (higher masseter serotonin-receptor expression in women, proposed as a pain-sensitivity mechanism), and Quinelato et al. (estrogen acting on bone, pain, and serotonin receptors) — to argue TMJ is "likely no exception" to general TMD sex-difference patterns. **No PsA-TMJ-specific sex-stratified dataset (prevalence, severity, or outcomes) appears anywhere in the retrieved full text.** The gender-difference content is either general-PsA data with TMJ appended by category, or general-TMD hormone biology applied by extrapolation — a distinction the review does not always flag explicitly, and one worth holding onto when applying this paper's gender claims clinically. Management considerations specific to female patients include monitoring TMJ symptoms through pregnancy-related hormonal flux and evaluating DMARD/biologic safety in pregnancy and breastfeeding in coordination with obstetrics/gynecology; the paper also discusses psychosocial and emotional-support needs (anxiety, depression, coping strategies) predominantly in the context of female patients with chronic PsA-TMJ pain.

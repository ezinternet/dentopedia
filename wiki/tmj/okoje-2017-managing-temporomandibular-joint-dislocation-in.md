## Three-line Summary

Uncontrolled retrospective case series (n=11) from the emergency department and dental centre of University College Hospital Ibadan, Nigeria, over 10 years (2004–2013), describing the presentation, aetiology, management and outcome of TMJ dislocation; diagnosis rested on history, clinical examination and radiographic findings.

Mean age 44.4 ± 15.9 years (range 25–65, modal age 25; 7 female / 4 male); aetiology wide mouth opening 6 (54.6%), trauma 4 (36.4%), unknown 1 (11.0%); 7 acute, 2 recurrent, 2 chronic; 10 bilateral anterior and 1 unilateral right anterior, with no posterior, medial, lateral or superior types; 6/11 presented within 24 hours. Hippocratic bimanual reduction alone was applied in 5/11 (reported as 45%), with IMF + sedation in 2, Hippocratic manoeuvre under GA in 1 and spontaneous reduction in 2; one patient failed closed reduction by every route (Hippocratic awake, Hippocratic under GA, class III maxillomandibular traction wires for 5 days) and required open reduction under GA, and 7/11 (63.6%) were lost to follow-up before 2 weeks (range 1 day to 9 months).

Clinical take: conservative manual reduction is genuinely first-line for anterior dislocation even where follow-up and staffing are thin, but n=11, a single 45% figure for the core manoeuvre, no comparator and 63.6% attrition make this context-setting service data, not effectiveness evidence — and the follow-up attrition is itself the finding with the widest reach.

임상적 시사: 전방성 탈구에서 보존적 수동복원 (manual reduction)이 추적 여건이 열악한 환경에서도 1차 선택지임을 뒷받침한다. 그러나 n=11, 핵심 기법의 단일 45% 수치, 대조군 부재, 63.6% 이탈률로 인해 효과 근거가 아니라 진료 환경 서술 데이터이며, 추적 이탈 자체가 가장 일반화 가능한 결론이다.

## Summary

Okoje, Aladelusi and Abimbola retrospectively reviewed 11 patients with temporomandibular joint dislocation seen at the emergency department and dental centre of University College Hospital Ibadan over 10 years (2004–2013). The cohort was 7 female and 4 male, mean age 44.4 ± 15.9 years (range 25–65), with wide mouth opening (6/11, 54.6%) the commonest aetiology ahead of trauma (4/11, 36.4%); 7 presentations were acute, 2 recurrent and 2 chronic, and 10 of 11 were bilateral anterior dislocations. Management was conservative-first: the Hippocratic bimanual manoeuvre alone was applied in 5/11 (45%), with IMF plus sedation in 2, the manoeuvre under general anaesthesia in 1 and spontaneous reduction in 2, and a single patient requiring open reduction after every closed route failed. The clinically transferable findings are the documented escalation pathway — including extra-oral reduction rescuing a failed Hippocratic attempt and bilateral eminectomy after recurrence despite IMF — and the follow-up attrition, with 7/11 (63.6%) lost before two weeks, which the authors attribute to the "sickness behaviour of the population" and which leaves recurrence and long-term joint function essentially unmeasured.

## Key Contributions

- **The whole escalation ladder in one series** — bimanual manual reduction → sedation + IMF → reduction under GA → extra-oral technique → open reduction, plus eminectomy for recurrent disease. No other page in this wiki documents the steps above a single manual technique for a low-resource service.
- **Extra-oral reduction as a rescue** (Case 7) — a 60-year-old housewife reduced successfully by steady external pressure over the palpable coronoid process and anterior ramus border after the Hippocratic manoeuvre failed, the only case in the series where a named alternative manual technique succeeded.
- **A complete closed-reduction failure** (Case 8) — a 48-year-old woman 5 days post-robbery-assault failed Hippocratic reduction awake, then under GA (2 attempts), then 5 days of class III maxillomandibular traction wires, and required open reduction under GA; post-operative mouth opening was limited and recovered with acrylic-screw jaw exercises over 6 weeks.
- **Non-structural and drug-mediated mechanisms made concrete** — the single idiopathic case was a patient on thioridazine + diazepam, and a 40-year-old epileptic on phenytoin + phenobarbitone presented with a seventh episode after a fall during a fit. This is direct clinical support for the neurogenic/drug axis of habitual dislocation rather than an anatomical one.
- **Follow-up attrition quantified** — 7/11 (63.6%) lost before 2 weeks, with only 4 followed beyond 14 days, and those four being precisely the recurrent and chronic cases.
- **Age and sex as risk signals** — a mean age roughly a decade above previous series (44.4 y), interpreted by the authors as wear-and-tear and joint laxity, and a female preponderance with recurrence commoner in women — both consistent with the 96-case Nigerian survey they cite but opposite to the male preponderance Agbara et al. reported from Zaria.

## Methodology

Retrospective case review from patient case files at the Accidents and Emergency unit and Dental clinic of University College Hospital Ibadan, 2004–2013. Data retrieved per patient: demographics, aetiology, duration of dislocation, number of episodes, underlying illness, drug history, radiographic findings, treatment of previous and/or current episodes, and outcome including complications. Diagnosis required history plus clinical examination substantiated by radiographic findings. Descriptive variables were analysed with the Chi-square test, although no test statistic, p-value or confidence interval is reported anywhere in the paper, leaving the analysis effectively descriptive. Outcomes were reduction success by method, need for escalation to a more invasive route, recurrence after reduction, follow-up duration and complications. Eight of the 11 patients (Cases 1–8) are narrated individually; three appear only as table cells.

## Results

**Demographics** — mean age 44.4 ± 15.9 years SD (range 25–65, modal age 25); 4 male, 7 female; 75% of males (3/4) presented below the mean age vs 28.6% of females (2/7).

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Acute | 4 (36.4) | 2 (18.2) | 1 (11.0) | 7 (63.6) |
| Recurrent | 0 (0.0) | 2 (18.2) | 0 (0.0) | 2 (18.2) |
| Chronic | 0 (0.0) | 2 (18.2) | 0 (0.0) | 2 (18.2) |
| **Total** | **4 (36.4)** | **6 (54.6)** | **1 (11.0)** | **11 (100.0)** |

**Systemic disease (Table 2)** — none 6 (54.6%), hypertension 2 (18.2%), psychiatric 1 (11.0%, thioridazine/Mellenil + diazepam), HIV 1 (11.0%, diagnosed, not yet started on ART), peptic ulcer disease 1 (11.0%).

**Anatomy** — 10 bilateral anterior, 1 unilateral right anterior; no superior, lateral, medial or posterior dislocation seen. Presentation timing: 6/11 within 24 hours; the rest were 2 recurrent and 2 chronic (>14 days).

**Management (Table 3)**

| Management | Trauma | Wide mouth opening | Unknown | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hippocrates's manoeuvre | 2 | 2 | 1 | 5 |
| Hippocrates + IMF + sedation | 0 | 2 | 0 | 2 |
| Hippocrates + GA | 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 |
| Spontaneous reduction | 1 | 1 | 0 | 2 |
| Unsuccessful | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| **Total** | **4** | **6** | **1** | **11** |

The Discussion reports the Hippocratic manoeuvre "successful in 45% of the cases" — 5/11, the manoeuvre-alone row. The Abstract decomposes the same patients differently: of the acute cases 4 were managed with the manoeuvre, 1 with the manoeuvre under GA and 2 by spontaneous reduction; both recurrent cases with manoeuvre plus IMF; and "fifty percent of the chronic cases" with the Hippocratic manoeuvre.

**Follow-up period (Table 4)**

| Follow-up group | Trauma | Wide mouth opening | Unknown | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| < 14 days | 2 | 5 | 0 | 7 |
| > 14 days | 2 | 1 | 1 | 4 |
| **Total** | **4** | **6** | **1** | **11** |

Range 1 day to 9 months; the authors state those followed beyond 2 weeks "were those with recurrences or chronic dislocations".

**Escalation outcomes from the case narratives**

| Case | Presentation | Route | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — 45 M | 1 wk after fall from moving bus, ~10 min LOC | Hippocratic after 4 attempts | Recurrence at 2 days after yawning → Hippocratic + diazepam + barrel bandage; clear at 2 days |
| 2 — 65 F | 1 yr of right TMJ clicking; hypertensive | Intra-articular triamcinolone 40 mg weekly × 6 wk + physiotherapy | Symptoms improved, then did not return |
| 3 — 25 F | 1 day after yawning | Hippocratic + barrel bandage | Full TMJ movement restored at 2 days |
| 4 — 28 F | 1 wk after tooth brushing; RVD awaiting HAART | Hippocratic under diazepam | Successful at 2nd attempt |
| 5 — 40 F | 1 day; fall during epileptic fit; 7th episode | Hippocratic | No complication recorded |
| 6 — 26 M | ~1 day after yawning; ~10 prior episodes; short condyles on radiograph | Hippocratic + MMF 2 wk | Immediate recurrence → definitive bilateral eminectomy; complete resolution incl. facial nerve weakness at 5 months |
| 7 — 60 F | 3 hours after yawning; similar episode 4 months earlier | Extra-oral technique after failed Hippocratic | Successful + barrel bandage |
| 8 — 48 F | 5 days post-robbery-assault; failed elsewhere | Hippocratic awake → GA → class III wires 5 days → **open reduction under GA** | Successful; limited mouth opening managed with acrylic-screw jaw exercises, gradual restoration to 6 weeks |

**Complications** — immediate recurrence after IMF (Case 6); reduced mouth opening after open recovery (Case 8); recurrence within 2 days (Case 1).

**Comparison with the quantitative cohorts cited** — Lowery et al. 37 dislocations over 7 years in a US ED with ~100,000 annual visits; Sang et al. 29 patients over 10 years in Nairobi; Agbara et al. 26 patients over 7 years in Zaria, Nigeria. Mean age here is "about a decade higher than previous studies"; trauma comprised 36.4% (road traffic crashes 18.2%), "much smaller than expected" given that road traffic crashes dominate Nigerian maxillofacial injury.

## Related Papers

- [[tmj/stolbizer-2020-anterior-dislocation-of-the-temporomandibular]] — the closest comparator and the other end of the technique question. Stolbizer's 42-patient single-centre series reports a simplified anaesthesia-free manual reduction at 100% (42/42) in 1.5–7 min; Okoje's conventional Hippocratic pathway shows 45% (5/11) for the manoeuvre alone, one total closed-reduction failure, and an open-reduction endpoint. Both are uncontrolled single-arm series from one dental/emergency service, so neither establishes superiority — but together they show that the achievable success rate for manual reduction in this setting is technique-sensitive rather than fixed.
- [[tmj/tarhio-2023-causes-and-treatment-of-temporomandibular]] — the direct scale contrast: n=260 over 14 years in Helsinki vs n=11 over 10 years in Ibadan. Tarhio's cohort is 61.9% recurrent, 40.0% spontaneous, manual reduction attempted in 91.9%, and eminectomy needed in only 1.9% (n=5), with neurological disease independently raising recurrence risk (1.34-fold, 95% CI 1.12–1.60). Okoje's is 18.2% recurrent, 54.6% wide-mouth-opening, and 1 open reduction in 11. The divergence in surgical end-point care is most plausibly a difference in surgical capacity, not disease severity — the clearest way to read these two cohorts together.
- [[tmj/prechel-2018-the-treatment-of-temporomandibular-joint]] — a clinical practice guideline on TMJ luxation. It grades the conservative-first strategy that this series supplies real-world outcome data for, and is the appropriate frame for weighing a single-centre n=11 against a guideline's recommendation.
- [[tmj/abrahamsson-2020-treatment-of-temporomandibular-joint-luxation]] — the systematic review that pools the treatment evidence this series is one small, low- and middle-income-setting sample of; the synthesis-level counterweight to the 45% single-arm figure.
- [[tmj/lin-2026-rapid-reduction-of-temporomandibular-joint]] — a contemporary acceleration-based technique whose refractory-after-multiple-Hippocratic-attempts subgroup is exactly the population Okoje's Case 7 (extra-oral rescue) and Case 8 (open reduction) illustrate in an African setting.
- [[tmj/falletti-2026-update-trismus-etiology-diagnosis-treatment]] — the mirror image of this page: the trismus (restricted opening) literature against this series' locked-open jaw, with the same first fork in the diagnostic pathway separating them.

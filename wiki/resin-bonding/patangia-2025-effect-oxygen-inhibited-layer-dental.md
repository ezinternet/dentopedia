## Three-line Summary

This systematic review synthesized 8 in vitro studies on the oxygen-inhibited layer (OIL) of dental adhesives and its effect on bond strength. OIL increased bond strength with light-cured composites but decreased it with chemically-cured composites. Current evidence is insufficient for definitive clinical recommendations, but the findings have direct implications for layered restorations and dual-cure cementation.

## 세줄요약

산소억제층(Oxygen-Inhibited Layer, OIL)이 치과 접착제의 결합 강도(Bond Strength, BS)에 미치는 영향을 체계적으로 종합한 SR(n=8 연구, 730개 치아). 광중합 레진에서는 OIL 보존이 BS를 높였고, 자가중합 레진에서는 OIL이 BS를 낮추고 접착 실패율을 높였다. 임상 권고를 내리기엔 근거가 부족하지만, 광중합 수복 시 OIL 제거를 피해야 함을 시사한다.

## Background

### What is the Oxygen-Inhibited Layer?

The oxygen-inhibited layer (OIL) is a thin (4–40 µm), sticky, incompletely polymerized surface film that forms on light-cured dental adhesives when atmospheric oxygen quenches free radicals during photopolymerization. Oxygen competes with methacrylate monomers for reactive radicals, leaving residual unsaturated double bonds at the adhesive surface.

**Factors influencing OIL thickness:**
- Monomer composition: higher HEMA concentration → thicker OIL (lower viscosity facilitates O₂ diffusion)
- Filler morphology: particles may act as barriers or conduits for O₂
- Initiator–activator system type and concentration
- Curing light parameters (intensity, duration)
- Environmental O₂ concentration

### Mechanistic Rationale for OIL's Effect on Bonding

The residual reactive double bonds in OIL can undergo copolymerization with the overlying composite resin, creating a chemical interlocking zone. This copolymerization is the proposed mechanism for increased bond strength with light-cured composites. With chemically-cured composites, however, residual acidic monomers from self-etch adhesives may interact with peroxide–amine initiator systems, inhibiting the composite's polymerization at the interface and reducing bond strength.

## Study Details

**Design:** Systematic review, PRISMA 2020 compliant, PROSPERO registered (CRD42024574190)  
**Search:** PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar; January 2000 – September 2024; English, open-access  
**Included studies:** 8 in vitro studies  
**Total sample:** 730 teeth (450 bovine incisors + 280 human molars)  
**Quality assessment:** QUIN tool; risk of bias 40–70% (mostly medium risk; 1 high risk)  
**Meta-analysis:** Not feasible due to heterogeneity  

## Key Findings

### Bond Strength by Curing Mechanism

| Composite Type | OIL Present | Bond Strength Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Light-cured composite | Yes (preserved) | Comparable or **higher** BS |
| Light-cured composite | No (removed) | Comparable or lower BS |
| Chemically-cured composite | Yes (preserved) | **Lower** BS, higher failure rate |
| Chemically-cured composite | No (removed) | Higher BS |

This pattern was **independent of adhesive brand** and was consistent across both shear bond strength (SBS) and microtensile bond strength (µTBS) testing methods.

### Etching Mode

Etching strategy (self-etch vs. total-etch) did not override the curing-mechanism-dependent direction of effect. The composite curing mechanism was the primary determinant of OIL's impact on bond strength.

### OIL Modification Strategies

| Strategy | Effect |
|---|---|
| Ethanol application | Effectively removes OIL; use with caution (may alter resin/interface) |
| Extended curing time | May reduce OIL thickness but associated with decreased BS in one study |
| Physical barriers (Mylar strips) | Limits O₂ exposure; variable effect on bond strength |
| Thermal activation (110–120 °C) | Reduces OIL thickness without altering resin composition |
| Phosphine additives (DPPS) | Enhances polymerization under aerobic conditions; suitable for dental use |

### Surface Free Energy

Removal of OIL frequently increased surface free energy, but this did not consistently translate into higher bond strength. Surface free energy alone is not a reliable predictor of bond strength in OIL research.

### Failure Mode

Adhesive failure predominated regardless of OIL condition. No consistent failure mode pattern attributable solely to OIL presence or absence was identified.

## Clinical Implications

1. **Do not remove OIL before placing light-cured composite.** Preserving the OIL facilitates copolymerization at the adhesive–composite interface and may improve bond strength.

2. **Consider OIL removal for self-cure and dual-cure scenarios.** When a chemically-cured or dual-cure composite/cement is placed over a self-etch adhesive, the OIL may interfere with the amine–peroxide initiator system and reduce bond strength. Physical removal (air-thinning, Mylar strip removal, light re-cure) before placing the material should be considered.

3. **Extended curing beyond manufacturer instructions may be counterproductive.** One study associated increased curing time with reduced bond strength, suggesting overcuring the adhesive before composite placement may degrade interfacial quality.

4. **Ethanol removal of OIL requires caution.** While effective at removing OIL, ethanol may alter resin structure and should not be used routinely.

## Limitations

- Only 8 studies included; heterogeneous experimental protocols
- Mostly medium-to-high risk of bias (QUIN: 40–70%)
- No meta-analysis possible
- No direct enamel-vs-dentin comparison for OIL effect
- All in vitro evidence — clinical extrapolation is not supported by current data
- Publication bias cannot be excluded (open-access only studies included)

## Evidence Assessment

**Strength:** Low-to-moderate (n=8 in vitro studies, heterogeneous, QUIN medium risk)  
**Consistency:** Moderate — the direction of effect by curing mechanism is consistent, but magnitude and confounders vary  
**Clinical relevance:** High — OIL is present in every adhesive application; findings directly impact layered restorations and indirect cementation protocols

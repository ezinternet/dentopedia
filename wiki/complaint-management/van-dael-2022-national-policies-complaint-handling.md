## Three-line Summary

Qualitative case study at a large acute NHS Trust (20 semi-structured staff interviews + documentary analysis) examining whether national English complaint-handling policies support local quality improvement.

Four adverse practice areas were identified: muddled complaint routes, investigations structured to scrutinise complaint 'validity' rather than learn, futile data-collection systems, and adverse incentives driving defensive workarounds.

National complaint policy as practised diverges markedly from its stated learning intent, meaning complaint systems are structurally oriented toward institutional defence rather than service improvement.

## Summary
Van Dael and colleagues used a case study of a large English NHS Trust -- 20 staff interviews plus documentary analysis -- to ask whether national complaint-handling policies actually support quality improvement. They found the opposite tendency: muddled complaint routes, investigations geared to test a complaint's 'validity', data systems that collect without learning, and incentives that drive defensive workarounds. The study shows that organisational and policy architecture, not just individual responders, structures complaint handling away from learning.

## Key Contributions
- Examines how national policies shape *local* complaint practice -- an under-studied link.
- Identifies four adverse areas: muddled routes for formal complaints, investigations structured to scrutinise complaint 'validity', futile data-collection systems, and adverse incentives/workarounds.
- Shows policy-as-intended diverges from policy-as-practised.
- Argues complaint systems are structured to defend rather than to learn.

## Methodology
Qualitative case study at a large acute multi-site NHS Trust: semi-structured interviews with 20 clinical, managerial, complaints, and patient-advocacy staff plus documentary analysis of complaint policies and infrastructure.

## Results
Four practice areas where national policy had adverse local consequences: (1) muddled routes for raising formal complaints; (2) investigative procedures structured to scrutinise the 'validity' of complaints rather than learn; (3) futile data-collection systems; (4) adverse incentives and workarounds. Together these undermine an improvement-focused approach.

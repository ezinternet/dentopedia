## Three-line Summary

Systematic review of 46 prospective studies (mean follow-up 2.08 years) on Type I immediate implant placement into fresh extraction sockets, searched via MEDLINE/Cochrane Library (1991–July 2010).

Annual failure rate 0.82% (95% CI 0.48–1.39%), translating to a 2-year survival rate of 98.4% (97.3–99%); among 5 factors analyzed (extraction reason, antibiotic use, implant position, loading type), only postoperative antibiotic use significantly reduced failure; marginal bone loss was mostly <1 mm, occurring predominantly in year 1.

Clinical implication: immediate implant survival is high, but true "success" (biological/technical/aesthetic complications) could not be reliably quantified due to sparse/heterogeneous reporting — more long-term, standardized-outcome studies are needed, with particular attention to aesthetic results.

## Summary

This landmark systematic review by Lang et al. (2012, Clinical Oral Implants Research, Suppl. 5) pooled 46 prospective studies (mean follow-up 2.08 years) identified via MEDLINE and the Cochrane Library (1991–July 2010) to quantify survival and success of Type I (immediate) implants placed into fresh extraction sockets. Using STATA and an inverse-variance weighting method, the review derived an annual failure rate of 0.82% (95% CI 0.48–1.39%), corresponding to a 2-year survival rate of 98.4% (97.3–99%). Among five candidate risk factors (reason for extraction, antibiotic regimen, implant position [anterior vs. posterior, maxilla vs. mandible], and loading protocol), only postoperative antibiotic use significantly reduced failure risk. In contrast to the strong survival data, "success" — defined by absence of biological, technical, and aesthetic complications — could not be robustly assessed because complication reporting was scarce and inconsistent across the included studies. Soft-tissue changes clustered in the first 3 months after restoration and stabilized by the end of year 1; marginal bone loss was similarly concentrated in year 1 and generally stayed below 1 mm. The benefit of platform-switching for hard-tissue preservation remained an open controversy at the time of this review. The authors concluded that despite excellent short/medium-term survival, more long-term studies with standardized outcome reporting are needed, with particular attention to esthetic results.

## Key Contributions

- Large-scale (46-study) quantitative pooled survival benchmark specific to Type I immediate implants: annual failure rate 0.82%, 2-year survival 98.4%
- First systematic identification of postoperative antibiotic use — among 5 candidate factors — as the only significant modifier of immediate-implant survival
- Documented time-course of soft- and hard-tissue change: soft tissue stabilizes by year 1 after early (3-month) remodeling; marginal bone loss predominantly occurs in year 1 and is generally <1 mm
- Explicitly identified a review-level evidence gap: implant "success" (vs. mere survival) was not assessable due to inconsistent complication reporting — a methodological critique that shaped later immediate-implant reporting standards

## Methodology

Systematic review with quantitative pooling of prospective (largely non-comparative) cohort studies — not an RCT-restricted meta-analysis. MEDLINE/Cochrane Library searched 1991–July 2010; 46 prospective studies meeting ≥1 year mean follow-up were included, with a large parallel excluded-study list documented by reason (e.g., not implant-immediate-specific, follow-up <1 year, un-isolable immediate-implant subgroup). Survival rates computed in STATA; weighted means of soft/hard tissue change obtained via the inverse-variance method.

## Three-line Summary

Narrative critical-assessment commentary (J Periodontol, 2021) that revisits whether "microbial dysbiosis" correctly describes the pathogenesis of gingivitis and periodontitis, contrasting the periodontal literature against classical dysbiosis criteria from GI/skin disease.

Most common forms of periodontitis in systemically healthy adults show *increased* microbial diversity relative to periodontal health — the opposite of classical dysbiotic diseases (IBD, dermatitis) which show *decreased* diversity from pathobiont overgrowth — arguing pathogenesis is driven by increased total biomass/metabolic activity of a diverse, evolutionarily-stable commensal community ("personalized pathology") rather than overgrowth of a few keystone pathobionts; true reduced-diversity dysbiosis is reserved for genetically susceptible (e.g., leukocyte adhesion deficiency) or systemically compromised hosts.

Clinical implication: monitoring/treating periodontitis based on one or a few "biomarker" species (red complex, keystone pathogens) — including systemic-antibiotic prescribing on that basis — lacks strong evidentiary support in systemically healthy patients; most cross-sectional microbiome studies also cannot establish causality due to lack of an accepted "active disease" definition, pooled sampling, and unvalidated relative-abundance data.

치은염·치주염 병인론에서 "미생물 dysbiosis(불균형)" 개념이 실제로 타당한지 재검토한 비평 논평(J Periodontol, 2021) — 장·피부 질환의 고전적 dysbiosis 기준과 치주 문헌을 대조.

전신적으로 건강한 성인의 흔한 치주염은 건강 상태보다 오히려 미생물 다양성이 증가하는 양상을 보여(IBD·피부염 등 고전적 dysbiosis는 다양성 감소가 특징), 소수 병원균(keystone pathobiont) 과증식이 아니라 진화적으로 안정된 다양한 공생균총의 총 생물량·대사활성 증가에 의한 "개인맞춤형 병리(personalized pathology)"로 설명하는 것이 더 타당하다고 주장; 진짜 다양성 감소형 dysbiosis는 백혈구 부착 결핍증 등 유전적 감수성·전신질환 동반 환자에 국한.

임상적 의미: red complex·keystone pathogen 등 소수 지표균만을 근거로 한 모니터링·치료(항생제 처방 포함)는 전신 건강 환자에서 근거가 약하며, 대부분의 횡단면(cross-sectional) 마이크로바이옴 연구는 "활동성" 치주염의 표준 정의 부재·pooled sampling·비검증된 상대적 존재비 데이터로 인해 인과관계를 입증하지 못한다는 한계가 있음.

## Summary

Scannapieco and Dongari-Bagtzoglou provide a historical and critical re-examination of the "dysbiosis" concept as applied to periodontitis pathogenesis. Tracing the arc from Koch's single-pathogen postulates through the non-specific plaque hypothesis (Socransky), the specific plaque hypothesis (Loesche), the "red complex" (Socransky 1998), to the keystone-pathogen/polymicrobial synergy and dysbiosis (PSD) model (Hajishengallis & Lamont, 2012), the authors argue that the PSD framework — borrowed from GI-tract disease models where dysbiosis manifests as *decreased* microbial diversity from pathobiont overgrowth — is a poor conceptual fit for most common (chronic/adult) periodontitis. Instead, periodontitis-associated subgingival microbiomes almost always show *increased* diversity relative to periodontal health, with health-associated species retained but diluted within a larger total biomass. The authors propose that in systemically healthy hosts, periodontal pathogenesis is better explained by increased biomass and cooperative metabolic/virulence activity of an already-diverse, evolutionarily co-adapted commensal community ("personalized pathology") than by outgrowth of a few "pathobionts." True reduced-diversity dysbiosis, by contrast, is documented in genetically susceptible hosts (e.g., leukocyte adhesion deficiency) and some cases of aggressive periodontitis with tissue-invasive species overwhelming the community — situations that do map onto the classical dysbiosis definition.

## Key Contributions

- Distinguishes "true" (reduced-diversity) dysbiosis from the diversity-*increasing* microbial shifts typical of common periodontitis, arguing the two should not be conflated under one label.
- Frames periodontal pathogenesis in systemically healthy hosts as driven by increased biomass/metabolic activity of a diverse commensal community rather than overgrowth of specific keystone pathobionts — directly challenging the keystone-pathogen/PSD model's generalizability.
- Introduces "personalized pathology" as the preferred conceptual frame: variable, patient-specific microbe-host risk-factor constellations driving site- and time-limited ("burst") tissue destruction.
- Surveys comparative dysbiosis across body sites (gut, skin, vagina, respiratory tract) to show that the direction of diversity change (increase vs. decrease) that defines "dysbiosis" is context-dependent on the site's baseline diversity, undermining a one-size-fits-all periodontal dysbiosis definition.
- Catalogs methodological limitations of the periodontal microbiome literature (cross-sectional design, no accepted "active disease" definition, pooled sampling, unvalidated relative-abundance data) that limit causal inference for any dysbiosis model.
- Identifies genetically susceptible (leukocyte adhesion deficiency) and aggressive-periodontitis hosts as the subset where classical, diversity-reducing dysbiosis genuinely applies.

## Methodology

Narrative/critical-assessment commentary (not a systematic review) synthesizing: historical dental-plaque ecology literature (Socransky, Loesche, Löe experimental-gingivitis model, Gibbons attachment studies); paleomicrobiology of ancient dental calculus; cross-sectional and longitudinal human oral 16S/metagenomic/metatranscriptomic studies; and comparative dysbiosis literature from other body sites (gut/IBD, dermatitis, bacterial vaginosis, asthma-associated airway dysbiosis, pneumonia). No new primary data; the contribution is conceptual re-analysis against explicit dysbiosis criteria borrowed from non-oral disease models.

## Results

- Classical dysbiotic diseases (IBD, ulcerative colitis, Crohn's, dermatitis) show *decreased* microbial diversity from overgrowth of a limited taxon set; periodontitis-associated microbiomes show the *opposite* — increased diversity relative to health.
- Even the most abundant disease-associated species (e.g., red-complex members) still represent <4% of total microbial species implicated in periodontitis, and identities vary widely between individuals/studies.
- Neither increased probing depth (reduced local O2 tension) nor bleeding on probing (inflammation marker) reliably predicts shifts in microbial community structure — undermining the hypothesis that local inflammatory/hypoxic microenvironment drives dysbiotic remodeling.
- Systemic host background (rheumatoid arthritis, diabetes) can alter subgingival microbiome composition independent of periodontal disease severity, producing a "healthy periodontium with altered plaque" pattern that does not meet a dysbiosis definition.
- Smoking-associated reduced subgingival diversity is a candidate case of true periodontal dysbiosis, but the same investigative team found Gram-negative anaerobe enrichment from smoking did not consistently translate into periodontal disease in a separate cohort — weakening simple diversity-loss-causes-disease claims.
- Genuine reduced-diversity dysbiosis with tissue-invasive species is documented in leukocyte adhesion deficiency and some aggressive periodontitis cases — the exception that fits the classical dysbiosis model.

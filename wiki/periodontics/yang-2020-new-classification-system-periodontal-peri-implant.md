## Three-line Summary

Korean-language clinical review (J Korean Acad Adv Gen Dent 2020;9:83–96; Yonsei University, Department of Advanced General Dentistry) of the 2017 AAP/EFP World Workshop classification of periodontal and peri-implant diseases and conditions — written to overcome the diagnostic imprecision and clinical applicability problems of the 1999 classification used for two decades.

Periodontitis: staged I–IV (initial to severe; stage III/IV reflect tooth-loss probability, complexity factors such as grade 2–3 furcation involvement can raise a case regardless of attachment loss) and graded A/B/C by progression rate (assume B, seek direct evidence for A or C, then modify by systemic risk factors — smoking, HbA1c, HIV); each condition now carries an explicit case definition.

Key boundary detail: gingivitis is defined by probing depth ≤3 mm with <10% sites bleeding on probing, while "clinical health" in successfully treated periodontitis patients is defined as ≤4 mm without bleeding — a deliberate paradox so stable post-treatment sites with isolated bleeding are not over-treated; peri-implant diseases are separated into mucositis vs peri-implantitis, and gingival phenotype/recession concepts are mapped.

## Overview

A Korean review article (Yonsei University, Dept. of Advanced General Dentistry) explaining the 2017 World Workshop classification (AAP + EFP co-sponsored) to practitioners, including clinical case definitions intended to standardize diagnosis and treatment. The review is structured around the four classification axes: periodontal health/gingival diseases, periodontitis (staging + grading), other conditions (gingival recession, trauma, etc.), and peri-implant diseases and conditions.

## Key Findings

- **Motivation**: the 1999 classification had been criticized for diagnostic imprecision and application difficulties in both clinical and scientific practice; the 2017 workshop appended case definitions to every category to establish clear diagnostic and treatment standards.
- **Periodontal health and gingivitis boundary**: gingivitis = probing depth ≤3 mm with <10% BoP; clinical health in successfully treated periodontitis patients is deliberately defined as ≤4 mm without bleeding — preventing stable post-treatment sites (isolated bleeding) from being re-treated invasively; a success criterion the review flags explicitly as a designed paradox.
- **Periodontitis staging (I–IV)**: stage I initial, II moderate, III severe (tooth-loss possible), IV severe (tooth-arch loss possible); stages III/IV account for teeth already lost to periodontitis; complexity factors (e.g., grade 2–3 furcation involvement) can raise a case to stage III/IV independent of attachment loss — a single complexity factor suffices.
- **Periodontitis grading (A/B/C)**: grade reflects the rate of progression; always prefer direct evidence; in clinical practice assume grade B and look for evidence to shift to A or C; after grading by progression evidence, modify by risk factors (smoking, diabetes/HbA1c, HIV+/AIDS with CD4 count <200, etc.).
- **Peri-implant diseases**: peri-implant mucositis vs peri-implantitis separated; case definitions applied (bleeding on probing, radiographic bone loss thresholds).
- **Gingival recession & phenotype**: recession classification incorporates interdental CAL, gingival phenotype (gingival phenotype vs biological phenotype distinction discussed), and root condition.

## Clinical Implications

1. When staging a periodontitis case, check furcation involvement and tooth-loss history — these alone can move a case to stage III/IV.
2. Grade the disease by progression evidence first; start from B and only move to A/C with explicit justification; then apply risk-factor modification.
3. Do not treat isolated bleeding in stable treated-periodontitis patients as active disease — the ≤4 mm no-bleeding "clinical health" definition is intentional.
4. For case presentation/documentation, use the category-specific case definitions rather than generic stage/grade labels.

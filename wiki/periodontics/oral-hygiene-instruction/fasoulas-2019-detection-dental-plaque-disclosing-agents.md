## Three-line Summary

Cross-sectional study of 588 public-school children (295 boys / 293 girls, aged 4–18 y) in a rural area of northern Greece (school year 2017–2018) that mapped dental biofilm detection sites using chewable two-tone disclosing tablets.

Age/education level, socioeconomic status (nationality proxy), BMI, and caries (DMFT) were each significantly associated with where plaque was detected (e.g., preschool vs other grades: upper posterior palatal p < 0.0001; overweight/obese: upper posterior buccal p = 0.0014; severe caries DMFT 6–10: upper posterior palatal p < 0.0001, lower posterior buccal p < 0.0001), whereas sex was the only variable with no effect on any detection surface (all p > 0.05).

Disclosing agents localize plaque to specific oral surfaces and can therefore target oral-hygiene instruction and program evaluation in schoolchildren, but the convenience-sampled cross-sectional design cannot establish causality.

## Summary

Fasoulas et al. (Heliyon 2019) conducted a cross-sectional study in 588 public-school children aged 4–18 in a rural area of northern Greece, using chewable two-tone disclosing tablets (Mira-2-Ton) to record where dental biofilm was detected across 11 tooth surfaces plus the tongue. They tabulated detection as multiple responses against sex, nationality (a socioeconomic proxy), education level (age proxy), BMI, and oral-health variables (DMFT, orthodontic disorder, periodontal disease), tested with likelihood-ratio chi-square. Age/education level, socioeconomic status, BMI, and caries status were each significantly associated with biofilm detection points, while sex was the only variable with no impact. Notably, tongue biofilm was high even in caries-free children (91.5%) and did not vary across DMFT categories (p = 0.6018), and overweight/obese children accumulated more plaque on posterior occlusal and buccal surfaces. The authors conclude that disclosing agents can usefully guide and evaluate preventive oral-hygiene programs in schools, but the convenience sample and association design preclude causal claims.

## Key Contributions

- First evaluation (by the authors' account) of the association between biofilm *detection location* and sex, age, socioeconomic status, BMI, and oral-health status in schoolchildren aged 4–18.
- Site-level map of biofilm detection (upper/lower anterior labial and lingual; upper/lower posterior buccal, lingual, occlusal; tongue).
- Confirms the hypothesis for age, socioeconomic status, BMI, and oral health; rejects it for sex — the only variable with no significant effect on any surface.
- Demonstrates that dorsal tongue biofilm is independent of caries status (91.5% even at DMFT = 0; p = 0.6018 across DMFT).
- Positions disclosing agents as an auxiliary tool for guiding and evaluating school oral-hygiene programs.

## Methodology

Cross-sectional observational study; no intervention or randomization. Setting: 14 public schools in rural northern Greece, school year 2017–2018, 58 sessions across preschool, primary, middle, and high school. Participants: n = 588 (295 boys, 293 girls), aged 4–18; children with learning disabilities/special healthcare needs excluded; written parental consent; approved by the Ministry of Education. A dentist (A.F.) examined oral health: DMFT categorised as 0 / 1–5 / 6–10; orthodontic disorder absence/presence; periodontal absence/presence (presence = bleeding on probing with/without attachment loss). A dietitian (E.P.) recorded height and weight; BMI classified via WHO child/adolescent growth curves. Biofilm was disclosed with two-tone chewable tablets (Mira-2-Ton, Hager & Werken), chewed 30–60 s; presence/absence recorded without scoring quantity. Statistics: biofilm detection tabulated as multiple responses; likelihood-ratio chi-square for differences across grouping levels; individual observed-vs-expected tests; α = 0.05; Minitab® 18.1 and JMP 13.2.

## Results

| Variable | Association with biofilm detection sites |
|---|---|
| Sex | **None** — only factor with no effect; largest χ² = 1.1230 (upper posterior buccal), p = 0.2893; all p > 0.05 |
| Nationality (SES proxy) | Non-Greek higher on upper anterior labial (p = 0.0136), upper anterior lingual (12.1%, p = 0.0172), upper posterior palatal (29.3%, p = 0.0001), lower posterior buccal (29.3%, p = 0.0001); Greek higher on lower anterior labial (47.4% vs 25.9%, p = 0.0126) |
| Education level (age proxy) | Preschool highest on upper posterior palatal (p < 0.0001) and lower posterior buccal (p < 0.0001); upper anterior labial (χ² = 109.203, p < 0.0001), upper anterior lingual (χ² = 101.847, p < 0.0001), lower posterior lingual (p = 0.0043). Upper anterior lingual > in preschool than primary (p < 0.001); lower anterior labial > in preschool/middle than high school (p < 0.001) |
| BMI | Overweight/obese higher on upper posterior occlusal (p = 0.0238), upper posterior buccal (p = 0.0014), lower posterior occlusal (p = 0.0184) |
| DMFT (caries) | Moderate (1–5): almost all surfaces, predominantly lower anterior labial (χ² = 212.998, p < 0.0001). Severe (6–10): upper posterior palatal (p < 0.0001), lower posterior buccal (p < 0.0001), lower posterior lingual (p = 0.0015), tongue. Other significant: upper anterior labial p = 0.0007, upper anterior lingual p = 0.0008, upper posterior occlusal p = 0.0002, upper posterior buccal p < 0.0001, lower anterior lingual p < 0.0001, lower posterior occlusal p = 0.0001 |
| Tongue across DMFT | No significant difference (p = 0.6018); high even when caries-free (DMFT = 0: tongue 91.5%, lower anterior labial 88.4%, p < 0.001) |
| Orthodontic disorder | Upper anterior labial (p = 0.0122) and lingual (p = 0.0072) staining rare with OD (2.6% each); no significant overall OD vs non-OD difference |
| Periodontal disease | Upper anterior labial/lingual 0% with periodontal disease; upper posterior palatal (p = 0.001) and lower posterior buccal (p = 0.001) more frequent *without* periodontal disease |

Baseline: 44.0% caries-free, 44.8% without periodontitis, 69.4% without orthodontic disorder. Limitation: convenience sample; orthodontic diagnosis without radiographs; binary periodontal and plaque (presence/absence) recording; cross-sectional design.

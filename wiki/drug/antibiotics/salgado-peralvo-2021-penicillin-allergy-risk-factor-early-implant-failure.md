## Three-line Summary

Systematic review without meta-analysis (Medline + Web of Science, PRISMA, JBI appraisal) of 5 studies (4 observational cohorts, 1 retrospective case-control) comparing early dental implant failure in penicillin-allergic patients given clindamycin vs non-allergic patients given amoxicillin.

Allergic patients on clindamycin failed substantially more often (24.68% vs 8.03%, RR 3.84; 2.10% vs 0.80%, OR 3.10; immediate-implant failure 5.7–10× higher) with up to six-fold higher infection risk, but 4 of 5 studies used self-reported, unverified penicillin allergy (SRPA).

Because allergy and drug are confounded and the allergy was mostly unconfirmed, penicillin allergy per se cannot be declared a risk factor; the authors recommend allergy testing and, in confirmed cases, replacing clindamycin with azithromycin 500 mg 1 h preoperatively.

## Summary

This systematic review (no meta-analysis) asks whether penicillin-allergic dental implant patients — who cannot receive amoxicillin, the best-studied prophylactic agent — have a higher risk of early implant failure than non-allergic patients. Five studies met the criteria (four observational cohorts, one retrospective case-control; JBI appraisal: four high-quality, one low). All five compared implants placed under clindamycin cover in penicillin-allergic patients against amoxicillin cover in non-allergic patients. Allergic patients had consistently and often significantly higher failure (relative risks ~3.1–3.8; e.g. implant-level failure 24.68% vs 8.03% in Salomó-Coll 2018; 2.10% vs 0.80% in French 2016) and up to six-fold higher postoperative infection; immediate implants in allergic patients failed 5.7–10 times more often. However, four of five studies used self-reported penicillin allergy without confirmatory testing, so allergy status and antibiotic choice are confounded. The authors conclude penicillin allergy cannot be called an independent risk factor, that the signal most likely reflects clindamycin being a suboptimal agent (bacterial resistance, MRSA selection, dose-dependent osteoblast toxicity), and that clinicians should confirm allergy by testing and, when confirmed, prefer azithromycin 500 mg one hour preoperatively over clindamycin until better evidence exists.

## Key Contributions
- Reframes the implant-antibiotic question around the penicillin-allergic subgroup and the *alternative* drug, rather than re-analysing amoxicillin-vs-placebo data.
- Documents a consistent 3–4× failure and up-to-6× infection signal for clindamycin-covered allergic patients across 5 independent datasets, with immediate implants worst affected (5.7–10×).
- Argues the culprit is clindamycin, not allergy, since 4/5 studies never verified the allergy and 80–99% of self-reported penicillin allergy is disproved on testing.
- Recommends a specific replacement protocol — azithromycin 500 mg 1 h preoperatively — with pharmacokinetic and anti-inflammatory justification.

## Methodology
Systematic review following PRISMA; no meta-analysis. Medline/PubMed and Web of Science searched (not time-restricted, updated March 2021) with MeSH terms (penicillin allergy OR clindamycin OR erythromycin OR azithromycin OR metronidazole) AND (dental implant OR dental implant failure OR dental implant complications); Google Scholar and reference lists hand-searched. Two independent reviewers with third-reviewer consensus; corresponding authors contacted about allergy verification. Risk of bias by the Joanna Briggs Institute 10-domain Critical Appraisal Tool. From 111 Medline + 202 WoS records, 5 studies were included: 4 observational cohort studies and 1 retrospective case-control; allergy confirmed by testing in only 1.

## Results

| Study | Comparison | Failure / infection finding | Effect size |
|---|---|---|---|
| Salomó-Coll 2018 | SRPA + clindamycin (100%) vs non-allergic + amoxicillin | Implant-level failure 24.68% vs 8.03% (p=0.032); patient-level 18.86% vs 5.17% (p=0.046); in SRPA 78.95% of failures early | RR 3.84 (implant), RR 3.64 (patient) |
| French 2015 | Confirmed allergy + clindamycin vs amoxicillin | "Twice the risk"; NS (p=0.11), overall failure 0.70% | HR 2.16 |
| French 2016 | SRPA vs non-allergic | Failure 2.10% vs 0.80% (p=0.002); post-op infection 3.40% vs 0.60% ("six times higher", p<0.05); survival 1/5/10y 98.1/97.3/97.3% vs 99.5/98.9/98.4% | OR 3.10 |
| French 2016 (immediate implants, n=687) | SRPA vs non-allergic | Immediate-implant failure 10.50% vs 1% ("10 times higher", p<0.001) | — |
| Wagenberg & Froum 2006 | SRPA + clindamycin vs non-allergic + amoxicillin | Immediate-implant failure secondary to infection 8.52% vs 2.95% ("5.70 times higher", p<0.001) | RR 3.34 |
| Block 2021 | Retrospective case-control, 224 patients with ≥1 failure | SRPA associated with failure within 1 y of placement; not years 1–4 or >4 y; antibiotics unspecified | OR 2.98 |

Number of studies included: **5** (4 observational cohort studies + 1 retrospective case-control). JBI quality: 4 high, 1 low.

Authors' recommendation (verbatim sense): confirm self-reported penicillin allergy with diagnostic testing; in test-positive patients "it seems prudent to avoid the use of clindamycin in favor of other drugs"; "the authors recommend the use of azithromycin 500 mg 1 h before surgery as an alternative until further studies are conducted." Amoxicillin 2–3 g 1 h preoperatively remains the proven protocol for non-allergic patients.

Key caveat: "it is not possible to state that penicillin allergy per se constitutes a risk factor for early dental implant failure" because most included patients had SRPA without diagnostic testing; heterogeneity and absence of RCTs limit certainty.

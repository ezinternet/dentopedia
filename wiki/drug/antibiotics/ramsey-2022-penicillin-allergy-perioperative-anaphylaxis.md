## Three-line Summary

Single-author narrative review (Frontiers in Allergy 2022) on the epidemiology of penicillin allergy and perioperative anaphylaxis, their overlap, the pregnancy/Group B Streptococcus case, penicillin–cephalosporin cross-reactivity, pre-operative penicillin allergy evaluation, and post-event allergy referral.

Penicillin allergy is reported by ~10% of the population but most labelled patients are not truly allergic; perioperative anaphylaxis is rare (1 in 6,531–10,000 procedures, ~2% fatal); penicillin/cephalosporin dual allergy is 0.7% of meta-analysis patients (3% with a confirmed penicillin allergy) — far below the historical 8–10% figure — and before/after studies show cefazolin can replace vancomycin/clindamycin in penicillin-allergy-labelled surgical patients with no rise in hypersensitivity.

The vast majority of penicillin-allergy-labelled patients can safely receive perioperative cephalosporins; pre-operative direct challenge delabels most low-risk patients; every patient with prior perioperative anaphylaxis should be referred to allergy/immunology, since history alone identifies the agent in only ~18% yet evaluation lets ~45/47 tolerate future anaesthesia.

## 세줄요약

단독 저자 내러티브 리뷰(Frontiers in Allergy 2022): 페니실린 알레르기 (Penicillin Allergy)와 수술기 아나필락시스 (Perioperative Anaphylaxis)의 역학, 그 교차점, 임신/B군 연쇄상구균 (Group B Streptococcus, GBS) 상황, 페니실린-세팔로스포린 (Cephalosporin) 교차반응, 수술 전 페니실린 알레르기 평가, 사건 후 알레르기 의뢰.

페니실린 알레르기는 인구의 약 10%가 보고하나 라벨 보유자 대다수는 진짜 알레르기가 아님; 수술기 아나필락시스는 드묾(시술 6,531~10,000건당 1건, 약 2% 치명적); 페니실린/세팔로스포린 이중 알레르기는 메타분석 환자의 0.7%(확인된 페니실린 알레르기에서는 3%)로 과거 8~10% 통설보다 훨씬 낮으며, 전후 비교 연구에서 세파졸린 (Cefazolin)이 반코마이신 (Vancomycin)/클린다마이신 (Clindamycin)을 대체해도 과민반응 증가 없음.

페니실린 알레르기 라벨 환자 대다수는 수술기 세팔로스포린을 안전하게 받을 수 있음; 수술 전 직접 유발검사 (Direct Challenge)로 저위험 환자 대부분을 라벨 해제 (Delabeling); 수술기 아나필락시스 병력자는 모두 알레르기/면역내과로 의뢰 — 병력만으로는 원인이 약 18%에서만 밝혀지나 평가 후 47명 중 약 45명이 이후 마취를 견딤.

## Why Ingested

The wiki's antibiotic-prophylaxis pages ([[drug/antibiotics/wilson-2021-ie-prophylaxis-aha-scientific-statement]], [[drug/antibiotics/thornhill-2019-adverse-reactions-oral-antibiotics-dentists]], [[overviews/sinus-augmentation-perioperative-pharmacology-overview]]) all route the ~10% of patients with a penicillin-allergy label to clindamycin or a cephalosporin but held nothing quantifying how weak penicillin–cephalosporin cross-reactivity actually is, or how readily such labels can be removed. This medical (allergy/immunology) narrative review supplies the numbers: dual-allergy frequency 0.7% overall, 3% with a confirmed penicillin allergy — versus the old 8–10% dogma — plus institutional data (Sexton, Grant, Macy) showing perioperative cefazolin can safely replace vancomycin/clindamycin in unverified low-risk labels. **Dental relevance:** for a penicillin-allergy-labelled patient needing surgical or infective-endocarditis prophylaxis before an extraction, implant, or sinus lift, this is the evidence that a cephalosporin (cefazolin/cephalexin) is almost always safe for an unverified low-risk history, that clindamycin's higher fatal-ADR profile ([[drug/antibiotics/thornhill-2019-adverse-reactions-oral-antibiotics-dentists]]) makes it a poor default, and that a pre-operative penicillin allergy evaluation can delabel most of these patients. It is not dental-specific — the surgical/anaesthesia/obstetric evidence is applied to the dental prophylaxis decision by inference.

## Summary

Narrative review by a single allergist placing penicillin allergy (reported by ~10% of the population, mostly not true allergy) alongside perioperative anaphylaxis (rare: 1 in 6,531 procedures in the US National Inpatient Sample, 2% fatal; possibly as frequent as 1:353 in a UK/Ireland audit). Antibiotics — chiefly beta-lactams, with cefazolin the single leading agent — are the most common perioperative anaphylaxis trigger in the US and UK, while neuromuscular blocking agents dominate in mainland Europe. The review's central practical message is that penicillin–cephalosporin cross-reactivity is much lower than historically taught: a meta-analysis of 6,147 patients (77 studies) found dual allergy in 0.7% overall, rising to 3% among patients with a confirmed penicillin allergy, and multiple before/after institutional interventions increased perioperative cefazolin use in penicillin-allergy-labelled patients (e.g. 34% → >80% in 756 patients) with no observed increase in hypersensitivity reactions. Removing the EHR penicillin/cephalosporin cross-reactivity alert (Macy et al.) raised cephalosporin prescribing without more reactions. Pregnancy is a special case: penicillin G is the drug of choice for Group B Streptococcus prophylaxis, beta-lactams cause 58% of pregnancy anaphylaxis and dominate the 2nd/3rd trimesters, but the benefit still outweighs the low anaphylaxis risk. After any perioperative anaphylaxis, allergy/immunology referral is strongly advised: history alone identifies the culprit in only ~18%, yet formal evaluation lets ~45/47 patients tolerate future anaesthesia. Pre-operative penicillin allergy evaluation / direct challenge delabels most low-risk patients (Savic: 55/56).

## Key Contributions

- Quantifies penicillin allergy and perioperative anaphylaxis side by side and at their intersection.
- Consolidates the modern low-cross-reactivity evidence (0.7% overall / 3% confirmed) against the historical 8–10% teaching.
- Summarises institutional QI interventions (Sexton, Grant, Macy) that safely shifted penicillin-allergy-labelled patients to perioperative cefazolin.
- Reviews the pregnancy / Group B Streptococcus intersection where penicillin G remains first choice.
- Argues for routine allergy/immunology referral after perioperative anaphylaxis and for pre-operative delabeling.

## Methodology

Narrative review, single author (sole contributor), no systematic search protocol, no pooled analysis, no risk-of-bias appraisal. Draws on US National Inpatient Sample data (Gonzalez-Estrada), the UK/Ireland NAP6 audit, Mayo/MGH/Spanish case series, the Sousa-Pinto cross-reactivity meta-analysis (6,147 patients, 77 studies), and single-institution before/after antibiotic-stewardship interventions. Full text retrieved (PMC open access).

## Bottom line for the penicillin-allergy-labelled dental patient

- **Unverified, low-risk history (remote, cutaneous-only, no SCAR):** a cephalosporin (cefazolin IM/IV, cephalexin PO) is appropriate first-line prophylaxis; the cross-reactivity risk is well under 1%. Avoid defaulting to clindamycin.
- **Verified penicillin allergy or high-risk unverified history:** ~3% cross-reactivity — consider allergist consultation before giving a cephalosporin.
- **Any SCAR history (SJS/TEN, DRESS):** do not re-expose to any beta-lactam; this is the exclusion in every cefazolin-substitution policy.
- **Pregnant patient needing prophylaxis:** penicillin/beta-lactam benefit still outweighs anaphylaxis risk; beta-lactams are the leading cause of peripartum anaphylaxis, so keep it on the differential if a reaction occurs.
- **After any perioperative/peripartum anaphylaxis:** refer to allergy/immunology — history alone is unreliable (~18%).

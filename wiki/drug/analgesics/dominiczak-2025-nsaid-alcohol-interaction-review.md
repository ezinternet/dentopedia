## Three-line Summary

Narrative review (Quality in Sport 2025) surveying PubMed/Google Scholar literature on NSAIDs-alcohol interactions, covering pharmacokinetic (CYP2E1 induction, ADH competition) and pharmacodynamic mechanisms across GI, hepatic, and renal systems.

Alcohol induces CYP2E1 and competes for ADH, altering NSAID metabolism; combined use exacerbates gastrointestinal mucosal damage, increases ulcer/GI bleeding risk, enhances nephrotoxicity, and may contribute to hepatotoxicity and inflammation-mediated carcinogenesis.

Clinical practice implications: healthcare providers should assess patient risk factors (GI history, renal impairment, cardiovascular disease) and counsel patients on avoiding alcohol while taking NSAIDs.

## Summary

Dominiczak et al. (2025) present a comprehensive narrative review of the literature on interactions between nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and alcohol, published in Quality in Sport. The review synthesizes evidence from PubMed and Google Scholar using keywords including "non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs," "alcohol," "COX-1," and "COX-2."

NSAIDs are among the most widely used pharmacological agents globally, accounting for approximately 8% of all prescriptions with 30–50 million daily users. Alcohol is similarly ubiquitous, and the two substances frequently co-occur in clinical and OTC use. The review organizes the interaction into two mechanistic categories: **pharmacokinetic** (alcohol inducing CYP2E1 and competing for alcohol dehydrogenase, thereby altering NSAID metabolism and clearance) and **pharmacodynamic** (additive local toxicity on the gastric mucosa, increased prostaglandin suppression effects, and enhanced oxidative stress).

The review systematically addresses three organ systems. **Gastrointestinal**: both alcohol and NSAIDs independently damage the gastric mucosa — alcohol disrupts the mucosal barrier while NSAIDs suppress protective prostaglandin synthesis via COX-1 inhibition. The combined effect is additive or synergistic, significantly increasing the risk of ulcers and upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB). The landmark Kaufman et al. (1999) case-control study (N=4,169) found a 2.7-fold higher risk of UGIB among regular ibuprofen users who drank any amount of alcohol. **Hepatic**: alcohol-induced oxidative stress and CYP2E1 metabolism generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), which when combined with NSAID exposure may contribute to hepatotoxicity. **Renal**: alcohol enhances NSAID-induced nephrotoxicity, increasing the likelihood of acute kidney injury (AKI) and chronic kidney disease (CKD), particularly in dehydrated individuals or those with pre-existing renal impairment.

Emerging evidence discussed in the review suggests that chronic NSAID-alcohol use may promote inflammation-mediated carcinogenesis, potentially increasing colorectal cancer risk, with individual susceptibility modulated by genetic polymorphisms in inflammatory pathways.

The authors conclude that given the prevalence of both NSAID use and alcohol consumption, awareness of these interactions is crucial in clinical practice. Healthcare providers should carefully assess patient risk factors, particularly in individuals with a history of gastrointestinal disorders, renal impairment, or cardiovascular disease. Patient education on the risks associated with NSAID and alcohol co-administration remains essential in minimizing adverse health outcomes.

## Key Contributions

- First comprehensive review specifically focused on the NSAIDs-alcohol interaction topic (distinct from NSAID-aspirin antiplatelet interaction literature)
- Mechanistically organized framework distinguishing pharmacokinetic vs. pharmacodynamic interaction pathways
- Addresses multi-organ impact: gastrointestinal, hepatic, renal, and cardiovascular systems
- Discusses emerging evidence on inflammation-mediated carcinogenesis linking chronic NSAID-alcohol use to colorectal cancer
- Highlights genetic polymorphism contributions to individual susceptibility variations
- Provides clinical guidance for risk assessment and patient counseling

## Methodology

- **Design**: Narrative review (not systematic review with meta-analysis)
- **Databases**: PubMed, Google Scholar
- **Search keywords**: "non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs," "alcohol," "COX-1," "COX-2"
- **Scope**: Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic interactions between NSAIDs and ethanol; effects on GI, hepatic, renal, and cardiovascular systems
- **Included literature**: Review articles, case-control studies, and clinical evidence on NSAID-alcohol interactions

## Key Results

| System | Mechanism | Clinical Consequence |
|--------|-----------|---------------------|
| **Gastrointestinal** | Alcohol disrupts mucosal barrier + NSAIDs suppress prostaglandin synthesis via COX-1 → additive damage | Increased risk of ulcers and UGIB; regular ibuprofen use + any alcohol: RR 2.7 (Kaufman 1999) |
| **Hepatic** | Alcohol induces CYP2E1 → increased ROS + NSAID oxidative stress | Elevated hepatotoxicity risk; CYP2E1 induction may alter NSAID metabolism |
| **Renal** | Alcohol enhances NSAID-induced nephrotoxicity | Increased AKI and CKD risk; especially in dehydrated or pre-existing renal impairment |
| **Carcinogenesis (emerging)** | Chronic NSAID + alcohol → sustained inflammatory signaling | Potential colorectal cancer risk amplification; individual susceptibility via genetic polymorphisms |

Key pharmacokinetic findings:
- **CYP2E1 induction**: Chronic alcohol use induces CYP2E1 → accelerated NSAID metabolism → potential reduced efficacy or increased toxic metabolite formation
- **ADH competition**: Alcohol competes for alcohol dehydrogenase, potentially altering NSAID clearance
- **Gastric emptying**: Alcohol alters gastric emptying rates, affecting NSAID absorption
- **Dose-dependent**: GI bleeding risk increases with increasing alcohol consumption and NSAID dose/duration

## Three-line Summary

Randomized, placebo-controlled pilot trial (n=12; maxillary implant patients) comparing naproxen 220 mg q8h for 7 days (n=7) against placebo (n=5), both with adjunctive acetaminophen, tracking implant stability (ISQ) and marginal bone loss over 16 weeks.

At 4 weeks ISQ rose +1% (naproxen) vs +41% (placebo) and at 16 weeks +34% vs +67%; the naproxen group also showed ~55% more marginal bone loss at 4 weeks and ~52% more at 16 weeks — none statistically significant given the small sample, though point estimates consistently favored impairment.

As an underpowered pilot it cannot confirm a clinical effect, but it supplies effect sizes showing an ISQ-based definitive trial needs >=64/group while a marginal-bone-loss endpoint needs only 7-10/group, recommending MBL as the efficient endpoint for future trials.

## Summary

Naproxen is a widely used NSAID for post-operative dental pain, but NSAIDs inhibit cyclooxygenase (COX) and reduce prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), a pathway critical for craniofacial bone repair (COX-2 knockout mice exhibit calvarial non-union). This raises a mechanistic concern that NSAIDs could impair early implant osseointegration. Prior to this study, that concern rested on general bone-healing evidence rather than direct human osseointegration data. This pilot randomized controlled trial is the first to directly measure implant stability and marginal bone loss in humans taking an NSAID versus placebo, and its explicit purpose was to generate effect sizes and sample-size estimates for a future definitive trial.

Across all outcomes the between-group differences were not statistically significant — expected for an underpowered n=12 pilot — yet the point estimates were directionally consistent, with the naproxen group showing slower implant-stability gain and greater marginal bone loss. Naproxen did effectively control pain: only 14.3% of the naproxen group needed rescue medication versus 80% of placebo.

## Key Contributions

- First human RCT to directly measure osseointegration outcomes (ISQ and marginal bone loss) under NSAID exposure.
- Provides quantitative effect sizes and a power analysis for both endpoints, identifying marginal bone loss as the more efficient primary endpoint (7-10/group vs >=64/group for ISQ).
- Confirms naproxen's analgesic efficacy in the implant setting while flagging a directional signal of possible osseointegration impairment.

## Methodology

Prospective, randomized, placebo-controlled pilot trial in patients receiving maxillary dental implants. Patients were randomized to naproxen 220 mg every 8 hours for 7 days (n=7) or matching placebo (n=5); both groups also received acetaminophen 500 mg every 6 hours, with acetaminophen-codeine as rescue analgesia. Twelve patients were analyzed after one naproxen subject was removed following implant failure at 1 week. Smokers, patients with uncontrolled diabetes, and chronic NSAID users were excluded. Implant stability was measured by the Implant Stability Quotient (ISQ) using resonance frequency analysis (Osstell) at baseline and 1, 4, and 16 weeks; marginal bone loss (MBL) was measured on periapical radiographs.

## Results

| Outcome | Naproxen (n=7) | Placebo (n=5) | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| ISQ increase at 4 weeks | +1% | +41% | Not significant |
| ISQ increase at 16 weeks | +34% | +67% | Not significant |
| Marginal bone loss at 4 weeks | ~55% more vs placebo | reference | Not significant (p >= 0.05) |
| Marginal bone loss at 16 weeks | ~52% more vs placebo | reference | Not significant (p >= 0.05) |
| Rescue medication required | 14.3% | 80% | (naproxen relieved pain) |
| Implant failure | 1 (at 1 week) | 0 | — |
| Power / sample size to detect effect | ISQ endpoint: >=64/group (up to hundreds) | MBL endpoint: ~7-10/group | MBL recommended |

All implants had ISQ > 40 by study end. A separate questionnaire of 18 early-implant-failure patients found 67.7% reported post-operative NSAID use versus ~26% in the general population (NHANES); 32.3% did not use NSAIDs. Overall, no between-group difference reached statistical significance, but the consistent point-estimate direction suggests NSAIDs may impair early osseointegration — a hypothesis this underpowered pilot was designed to size, not confirm.

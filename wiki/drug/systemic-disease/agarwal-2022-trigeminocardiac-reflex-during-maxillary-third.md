## Three-line Summary

Case report / small case series (n=2: 44-year-old and 29-year-old systemically healthy women) of sudden loss of consciousness with bradycardia during non-surgical extraction of a maxillary right third molar under local anesthesia, attributed by the authors to trigeminocardiac reflex (TCR) rather than vasovagal syncope.

Case 1 had pulse 33–42 bpm, respiratory rate 8–9/min and BP 90/70 mmHg after extraction, recovering to HR 60–65 bpm and BP 100/80 mmHg over 30–40 min; Case 2 had an unrecordable pulse with no respiration while rising from the chair, recovering after one deep gasp and normalising (BP 110/70 mmHg) within 15–20 min; neither received atropine and both went home the same day.

The clinical value is the differential, not the incidence: TCR (afferent CN V2/V3 → brainstem → vagal efferent) is treated first by withdrawing the surgical stimulus, whereas vasovagal syncope is managed with positioning, oxygen and reassurance — but with n=2, no ECG and retrospective labelling, the distinction here is a hypothesis-level teaching frame.

## Summary

This is a two-case report from an oral and maxillofacial surgery practice describing sudden unresponsiveness during routine, non-surgical extraction of a maxillary right third molar under local anesthesia in two systemically healthy women (44 and 29 years). In Case 1 the collapse followed an uncomplicated extraction in a patient who had been comfortable; the recorded pulse was 33–42 bpm, respiratory rate 8–9 cycles/min and BP 90/70 mmHg, and she recovered to HR 60–65 bpm and BP 100/80 mmHg over 30–40 minutes with oxygen 5 L/min, an IV line and ICU observation. In Case 2 the collapse occurred while the patient tried to rise from the chair; the pulse was not recordable and no respiration was observed, and she responded after a single deep gasp, with vitals normal and BP 110/70 mmHg within 15–20 minutes. Neither patient received atropine or glycopyrrolate, and both were discharged the same day.

The authors' argument is mechanistic. They apply the classic TCR criterion — a fall of more than 20% in mean arterial pressure and heart rate coinciding with trigeminal-nerve stimulation — and conclude that these were TCR episodes rather than simple syncope, because the negative vital-sign change exceeded 20% and coincided with a procedure known to stimulate the trigeminal nerve. They lay out the arc as afferent short and long ciliary nerves → ciliary ganglion → gasserian ganglion → sensory nucleus of the trigeminal nerve in the floor of the fourth ventricle → internuncial fibers of the reticular formation → vagal motor nucleus → depressor fibers to the myocardium, and locate the clinical difference from vasovagal syncope in the treatment step: identify the bradycardia, **stop the procedure to withdraw the stimulus**, and reserve atropine or glycopyrrolate for bradycardia that is severe or persists after the stimulus is gone. They also cite Arakeri and Arali's 400 local-anesthesia extractions and the term "dentocardiac reflex" as precedent, and argue for intraoperative vital-sign monitoring because neither patient reported any warning discomfort.

The paper's evidential weight is low and should be read accordingly. There is no denominator, no ECG or continuous trace, and the "TCR" label was applied retrospectively — expressly so in Case 2, which was first managed as a presumed vasovagal syncope. Case 2's trigger (rising from the chair) is equally compatible with orthostatic/vasovagal collapse, prodromal symptoms were not systematically elicited, and the paper contains an internal inconsistency about whether VVS pathophysiology is "fully elucidated" (abstract) or not (discussion). What it does establish is the chairside differential and the reason it matters: a reflex driven by surgical afferents has a different first move than a psychogenic/orthostatic faint.

## Key Contributions

- Documents TCR during **routine non-surgical** maxillary third molar extraction under local anesthesia — not under general anesthesia and not during major maxillofacial surgery — with objective vital-sign values (HR 33–42 bpm; unrecordable pulse with apnea; BP 90/70 mmHg).
- Separates TCR from vasovagal syncope on mechanism (CN V2/V3 afferent → vagal efferent vs psychogenic/baroreceptor-mediated) and on treatment (withdraw the stimulus first vs positional/psychologic management).
- Reproduces the full reflex arc and the >20% HR/MAP definition, giving a checkable criterion rather than a vague "faint".
- States the escalation ladder: stimulus cessation reverses most cases; atropine or glycopyrrolate only for severe or persistent bradycardia; Arasho et al. algorithm for systematic management.
- Argues for vital-sign monitoring during minor oral procedures because TCR can occur without prodrome in a calm patient.

## Methodology

- **Design**: Case report / small case series (n=2). No protocol, no consecutive enrolment, no control group, no statistics.
- **Setting**: Authors' oral and maxillofacial surgery practice (India); both patients transferred to an intensive care unit for observation.
- **Cases**: maxillary right third molar in both — Case 1 grossly decayed, extracted with luxators and forceps after 2 days of premedication; Case 2 deeply carious, extracted with forceps after 2% lignocaine with adrenaline.
- **Data**: opportunistic chairside pulse, respiratory rate and blood pressure during the collapse and recovery; no ECG, no continuous monitoring.
- **Diagnostic rule applied**: >20% negative change in vitals coinciding with trigeminal-nerve stimulation (classic TCR definition).

## Results

| Outcome | Case 1 (44 y F) | Case 2 (29 y F) |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Immediately after non-surgical extraction | While rising from the chair after extraction |
| Heart rate | 33–42 bpm (fluctuating) | Not recordable |
| Respiratory rate | 8–9 cycles/min | None noticed |
| Blood pressure | 90/70 mmHg | Not recorded during event |
| Support given | O₂ 5 L/min, IV line, ICU | ICU observation |
| Recovery | HR 60–65 bpm, RR 12–14/min, BP 100/80 mmHg at ~30–40 min | Vitals normal, BP 110/70 mmHg at 15–20 min |
| Anticholinergic | None | None |
| Disposition | Same-day discharge, fair condition | Same-day discharge, fair condition |

- Both episodes met the authors' applied >20% criterion and reversed once the surgical stimulus was removed.
- Cited context: Schaller et al. (1999) extended TCR to intracranial trigeminal stimulation; the oculocardiac reflex (CN V1) is the historical prototype; Arakeri & Arali (2010) reported TCR after maxillary first molar extraction and studied 400 extractions under local anesthesia, coining "dentocardiac reflex".
- The paper notes psychological variation is **not** an established risk factor for TCR (unlike its role in VVS), while still recommending psychological control for safety.

### TCR vs vasovagal syncope — what changes chairside

| Axis | Trigeminocardiac reflex (TCR) | Vasovagal syncope (VVS) |
|---|---|---|
| Afferent | Trigeminal sensory branches (CN V1/V2/V3) — here V2, maxillary molar region | Psychogenic/baroreceptor (Bezold–Jarisch), not a surgical afferent |
| Trigger | Mechanical/nociceptive manipulation of trigeminal-innervated tissue | Anxiety, pain expectation, sight of instruments, dehydration, standing up |
| Prodrome | Often absent — both patients here reported no discomfort | Usually present (warmth, nausea, sweating, tunnel vision) |
| Vital pattern | Bradycardia ± hypotension ± apnea, temporally locked to the stimulus | Bradycardia + hypotension, often after the stimulus/procedure |
| First-line act | **Stop the procedure / withdraw the stimulus** | Supine or Trendelenburg, legs elevated, oxygen, reassurance |
| Drugs | Atropine or glycopyrrolate if bradycardia is severe or persists after stimulus removal | Antimuscarinic/fluids if persistent; ephedrine for hypotension with adequate HR |
| Implication | A completed extraction can still be the trigger — "the procedure is over" is not reassurance | Removing the patient from the operatory situation is often enough |

## Three-line Summary

Single case report (n=1, 29-year-old man, ASA II) of severe cardioinhibitory vasovagal syncope during 22-gauge IV cannulation before general anesthesia for third molar extraction, in a patient on duloxetine 20 mg, quetiapine 12.5 mg, etizolam 1 mg, eszopiclone 1 mg, and trazodone 25 mg.

Heart rate fell from 60 bpm to sinus arrest about 45 seconds after bradycardia onset, with two 3–5-second generalized tonic seizures and systolic BP transiently 76 mmHg; chest compressions plus atropine 0.5 mg restored spontaneous circulation and consciousness in about 45 seconds, and persistent 30–40 bpm bradycardia required ephedrine 5 mg.

The authors attribute the low syncope threshold to additive autonomic effects of the psychotropic regimen (α1 blockade, SNRI-driven pressure fluctuation, GABA-A-mediated sympatholysis), while BIS fell from 90 to the 70s before bradycardia and to the low 50s at arrest — a single case cannot separate drug-induced modulation from a pure reflex.

## Summary

A case report from an oral and maxillofacial surgery department describes 환자 A (29-year-old man, 178 cm, 58 kg, ASA II) referred for third molar extraction under general anesthesia because severe dental phobia made local-anesthesia extraction unfeasible. His regular regimen was duloxetine 20 mg each morning, quetiapine 12.5 mg at bedtime, and the hypnotics etizolam 1 mg, eszopiclone 1 mg, and trazodone 25 mg at bedtime, all for anxiety, depression and insomnia; preoperative blood tests, ECG and chest radiography three weeks earlier were normal. He had recurrent vasovagal reactions during blood sampling since his mid-teens (never with loss of consciousness), and at the preoperative orientation visit he became presyncopal with a blood pressure of 74/32 mmHg and a heart rate of 52 bpm. In the operating room his vitals were stable (111/63 mmHg, 79 bpm, SpO₂ 100%), and a 22-gauge IV catheter was placed in the left forearm without premedication or topical anesthetic. Vitals were still stable immediately afterwards (112/60 mmHg, 60 bpm), but during fixation of the line the heart rate declined progressively and reached sinus arrest approximately 45 seconds after bradycardia onset, followed immediately by two generalized tonic seizures lasting 3–5 seconds with systolic pressure transiently 76 mmHg while SpO₂ stayed at 100%. Chest compressions were started and atropine 0.5 mg given; spontaneous cardiac activity and consciousness returned about 45 seconds after the onset of arrest, and persistent bradycardia of 30–40 bpm was corrected with ephedrine 5 mg. BIS had fallen from 90 to the 70s before bradycardia and to the low 50s at arrest, returning promptly to the 90s once circulation was restored. Emergency evaluation showed only a mildly elevated creatine kinase (303 U/L vs 172 U/L preoperatively; normal 50–240) and, incidentally, marked enlargement of the posterior bodies and trigones of the lateral ventricles suggestive of periventricular leukomalacia; cardiology classified the event as cardioinhibitory VVS and advised preoperative IV access and fluids in the ward plus preparation for temporary pacemaker insertion should general anesthesia be needed again. The teaching point is that a reflex habitually labelled benign can terminate in sinus arrest after a stimulus as small as venipuncture, and that polypharmacy with psychotropics — α1-blocking quetiapine and trazodone, SNRI duloxetine, and GABA-A-acting hypnotics — is a plausible threshold-lowering background that a single case can suggest but not prove.

## Key Contributions

- **The counterexample to "benign vasovagal"**: a *single* 22-gauge venipuncture in an ASA II young adult produced sinus arrest, two generalized tonic seizures, and a resuscitation — the wiki's other procedural-VVS pages describe faints, this one documents the ceiling of the spectrum.
- **A fully printed psychotropic regimen** (agent + dose + timing) rather than the usual "on psychiatric medication" — turning a vague risk factor into a checkable one: quetiapine 12.5 mg, duloxetine 20 mg, etizolam 1 mg, eszopiclone 1 mg, trazodone 25 mg.
- **Mechanistic decomposition by drug class**: α1-adrenergic blockade → peripheral vasodilation and orthostatic hypotension (quetiapine, trazodone); SNRI-augmented noradrenergic transmission → blood-pressure fluctuation; GABA-A-mediated central inhibition → reduced sympathetic outflow and, via muscle relaxation, reduced venous return. The authors explicitly frame the combination as potentially *synergistic* in lowering the VVS threshold.
- **BIS as a pre-bradycardic signal**: BIS fell from 90 to the 70s *before* the heart rate and pressure fell, and to the low 50s at arrest. This is a hypothesis-generating observation (one prior case, Win NN et al.: 98 → 35), offered with an explicit call for prospective study — not a validated monitoring recommendation.
- **A documented prior-visit prodrome** (74/32 mmHg, 52 bpm at the preoperative orientation) — evidence that the susceptibility was visible before the event, which is what makes the "prior vasovagal history is a perioperative red flag" recommendation concrete.
- **A concrete future-anesthesia plan**: preoperative IV access and fluid in the ward, and temporary pacemaker standby — a transferable template for patients with a cardioinhibitory history facing general anesthesia.

## Methodology

- **Design**: single case report (n=1), narrative chronology with continuous intraoperative monitoring; no comparator, no protocolized provocation test, no drug-level or autonomic-function testing.
- **Setting**: oral and maxillofacial surgery, general anesthesia for bilateral third molar extraction.
- **Patient**: 환자 A, 29-year-old man, 178 cm / 58 kg, ASA II; bilateral horizontally impacted mandibular third molars and pericoronitis of the maxillary third molars; anxiety, depression, insomnia under psychiatric care.
- **Medications**: duloxetine 20 mg (morning); quetiapine 12.5 mg, etizolam 1 mg, eszopiclone 1 mg, trazodone 25 mg (all bedtime); plus a probiotic and a gastrointestinal prokinetic.
- **Fasting/hydration**: admitted the day before; nil by mouth from midnight, no fluids from 07:00; 200 mL water up to two hours before entering the operating room; no dehydration on admission; **no preoperative intravenous fluid**.
- **Monitoring**: non-invasive blood pressure, ECG, pulse oximetry, BIS; oxygen 6 L/min by face mask.
- **Index stimulus**: 22-gauge IV catheter, left forearm, **without premedication or topical anesthesia**.
- **Outcomes**: vital-sign and BIS time course, duration of arrest and of unconsciousness, interventions and response, emergency-department workup, follow-up at one and two weeks.

**Event sequence (as printed).** Progressive heart-rate decline during fixation of the IV line → sinus arrest approximately 45 seconds after the onset of bradycardia → two generalized tonic seizures of 3–5 seconds with systolic BP transiently 76 mmHg, SpO₂ 100% throughout → chest compressions begun immediately and atropine 0.5 mg given → spontaneous cardiac activity and consciousness restored approximately 45 seconds after the onset of arrest → bradycardia of 30–40 bpm persisting until ephedrine 5 mg → hemodynamic stabilization.

**Workup.** Creatine kinase 303 U/L (preoperative 172 U/L; normal range 50–240 U/L); ECG normal sinus rhythm without ST-T changes; head-to-pelvis CT showed marked enlargement of the posterior bodies and trigones of the bilateral lateral ventricles, suggestive of periventricular leukomalacia, which neurology judged of limited clinical significance and advised to follow up. Returned to the ward about 2.5 hours after onset, fully alert; discharged the following day; asymptomatic at the one-week outpatient visit and at neurology/cardiology review two weeks later. No EEG was performed; the seizures were attributed clinically to cerebral hypoperfusion from sinus arrest and epilepsy was considered unlikely. Cardiology concluded cardioinhibitory VVS.

**Authors' causal reading.** Three strands are offered and none is tested: (1) the reflex itself — Bezold-Jarisch physiology with individual variation in the central autonomic network; (2) the drug regimen — quetiapine's α1 blockade and high orthostatic-hypotension risk among antipsychotics, duloxetine's SNRI-driven pressure fluctuation, GABA-A hypnotics reducing sympathetic outflow and venous return, and trazodone's potent α1 blockade plus concentration-dependent inhibition of hERG (IKr), IKs, INa and ICa; (3) brain structure — ventricular enlargement here, set against reports of right insular atrophy, central-autonomic-network reorganization, blood–brain-barrier permeability after a single vasovagal episode, and periventricular white-matter lesions in recurrent syncope, with the authors stating the link in this case remains unclear. The paper's own hedge is "may have lowered the threshold"; there is no drug level, no tilt-table test, no withdrawal/rechallenge — **a single case cannot separate drug-induced autonomic modulation from a pure reflex**, nor from the structural finding.

**Prevention recommendations drawn by the authors** (not all applied to this case): treat a long-standing history of procedural vasovagal reactions as a perioperative red flag and build it into anesthetic planning; systematically review psychotropics that impair autonomic regulation, citing the 2017 ACC/AHA/HRS recommendation to consider drug-induced orthostatic hypotension during medication review (Class IIa); avoid perioperative dehydration; establish IV access in a calm, monitored area outside the operating room with emergency treatment immediately available; keep atropine or glycopyrrolate immediately available; and regard a falling BIS in psychotropic-treated patients as a possible early marker of a severe vasovagal episode.

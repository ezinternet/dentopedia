## Three-line Summary

A nationally distributed online cross-sectional survey of 442 Lebanese dentists (both dental associations, August–October 2024) measured emergency experience, clinic equipment and drugs, dentist/staff training, and self-rated capability, using an instrument adapted from the Jordanian survey lineage.

Vasovagal reactions ranked **first** among emergencies in the preceding 12 months — 91 of the 161 dentists (56.5%) who had encountered any emergency — ahead of hypoglycemia (55.9%) and orthostatic hypotension (54.0%), while preparedness stayed thin (emergency kit 38.2%, defibrillator 5.9%, staff trained 18.8%) and no **VVS-specific** confidence figure was reported because capability was measured for the bundled "minor conditions" category at 69.0%.

The paper documents a training cliff rather than a knowledge vacuum: 71.3% received university-curriculum emergency training but only 39.6% postgraduate, with no Lebanese regulation mandating BLS for dentists or staff.

## Summary

This is the first national survey of medical-emergency preparedness among Lebanese dentists, a cross-sectional online study (n = 442, August–October 2024) whose questionnaire was adapted from the instrument used in Jordan, translated into French and back-translated, and distributed by the two Lebanese Dental Associations via WhatsApp with four reminders. Experience was dominated by minor events: 74.2% had ever faced a minor problem, 36.4% had faced at least one emergency in the past 12 months, and among those 161 dentists vasovagal reaction was the most frequent emergency at 56.5%, ahead of hypoglycemia (55.9%) and orthostatic hypotension (54.0%); major emergencies (22.4% ever) and cardiac arrest (1.8% ever) were rare. Preparedness was low on every axis — emergency kit 38.2%, any emergency equipment 88.0% but defibrillator only 5.9%, at least one emergency drug 42.8%, staff trained 18.8% of those with staff, an emergency response plan in 16.1% — and 87.8% wanted more training. Confidence fell steeply with acuity: 69.0% considered themselves capable for minor conditions, but only 18.8% for cardiac arrest management and 8.2% for pharyngeal-tube insertion. Postgraduate training, prior defibrillator use, and having an emergency kit were independently associated with higher capability. The central clinical reading is that Lebanon does not lack undergraduate exposure (71.3%) but lacks any mechanism compelling its maintenance (7.9% trained within the past year), which is precisely what the authors ask the Ministry of Public Health and the dental schools to mandate.

## Key Contributions

- **Provides a national-sample frequency hierarchy with an explicit denominator**: VVS 56.5% among dentists who had any emergency in the past 12 months (n = 91/161), versus 20.6% of the whole sample (91/442, derived).
- **Separates dentist preparedness from clinic preparedness**: the staff axis (18.8% trained, 16.1% with an EMRP, and of those only half simulate annually) is far weaker than the dentist training axis.
- **Identifies the "training cliff"**: 71.3% undergraduate versus 39.6% postgraduate training, with only 7.9% trained within the past year — a policy failure rather than an educational one, since no Lebanese regulation mandates first aid for dentists or staff.
- **Links modifiable preparedness factors to capability**: emergency kit (OR 2.16 for minor, 1.61 for major), emergency drugs (OR 2.09 for major), prior defibrillator use (OR 5.05 / 2.77 / 5.07 across the three outcomes), and EMRP presence (OR 3.63 for cardiac arrest).
- **Establishes the Eastern-Mediterranean comparison set** against which the Polish (smereka-2019) data can be read.

## Methodology

- **Design**: cross-sectional national online survey (self-administered, English + French), data collection August–October 2024.
- **Sampling frame**: dentists registered with the Lebanese Dental Associations of Beirut and Tripoli; link broadcast via WhatsApp from LDA phone databases, four two-week reminders.
- **Sample size**: Epi Info StatCalc, population 7,000, expected frequency 61.1%, 95% CL, α 5% → required 347; achieved **n = 442**. **Response rate not reported.**
- **Instrument**: adapted from the Jordanian study tool (Abu Al Ghanam et al.); sections on participant/professional characteristics, emergency experience (minor = VVS/hypoglycemia/orthostatic hypotension; major = asthma/seizures/allergy/foreign-body inhalation/ACS; cardiac arrest counted separately), equipment and drugs, training, plus added staff-training and EMRP items. Pilot-tested with 5 dentists.
- **Analysis**: SPSS v25; chi-square/Fisher screens then dimension-wise logistic regressions; capability dichotomized (fully capable + capable enough = capable); α = .05.

**Reading the VVS number correctly.** 56.5% is the share of **dentists who had at least one emergency in the past 12 months**, not the share of all dentists (20.6%) and not the share of emergency episodes. The Polish comparator in [[drug/systemic-disease/smereka-2019-preparedness-and-attitudes-towards-medical]] reports 46.30% as the share of **all surveyed dentists** with ≥1 case in 12 months — the rank ordering agrees, the denominators do not, so the two percentages cannot share one column without a label.

**What this paper cannot tell you about VVS.** It contains no VVS-only confidence item, no VVS-only training outcome, and no VVS case definition beyond the label — and Figure 3, where the itemized capability data live, is not recoverable from the retrieved full text. Any VVS-specific competence claim attributed to this paper would be fabricated; use [[drug/systemic-disease/li-2026-syncope-in-dentistry-risk-factors]] or [[drug/systemic-disease/smereka-2019-preparedness-and-attitudes-towards-medical]] for VVS-specific management confidence instead.

> Med Hypotheses 2010. This page records the **origin** of the claim "extraction-induced syncope = trigeminocardiac reflex", not a test of it. No incidence, heart-rate or outcome data are reported in the retrieved abstract, and full text was not retrieved. Do not cite this page for the proposition that TCR causes syncope.

## Three-line Summary

Hypothesis paper (Med Hypotheses 2010; abstract-only, full text not retrieved) reporting one case of trigeminocardiac reflex after maxillary first-molar extraction, after which the authors state they observed 400 extractions under local anesthesia.
The proposal: the trigeminocardiac reflex — usually described under general anesthesia when sympathetic reflexes are blunted — also occurs under local anesthesia during maxillary molar extraction (the authors' "dento-cardiac reflex") and mediates syncope; no incidence or hemodynamic figures appear in the abstract.
This is the historical origin of the dental TCR-syncope hypothesis, not a demonstration of it — the mechanism stays a hypothesis until monitored observational studies measure it.

## Summary

Arakeri and Arali (Med Hypotheses 2010) published the hypothesis that the trigeminocardiac reflex (TCR), rather than an unexplained vasovagal reaction, is what produces transient loss of consciousness during tooth extraction. The argument starts from one clinical observation — a TCR episode after extraction of a maxillary first molar — and the authors state that they then studied 400 extractions under local anesthesia to examine the relation between TCR and syncope. Their proposition is that the reflex, conventionally described under general anesthesia where sympathetic counter-regulation is blunted, can also be elicited under local anesthesia during maxillary molar extraction, and that this "dento-cardiac reflex" mediates syncope. The retrieved record is the PubMed abstract only; it contains no measured outcome, no incidence figure, and no description of monitoring or analysis, so this page cannot report what the 400 extractions showed. Its value to the wiki is entirely historical and mechanistic: it is the origin of a claim that later dental reviews now carry as background, such as [[drug/systemic-disease/li-2026-syncope-in-dentistry-risk-factors]], which lists TCR (CN V2/V3 afferents → vagal outflow → bradycardia/hypotension) as one of two syncope pathways. Whether that pathway actually accounts for extraction syncope is an empirical question this paper raised but did not answer — that requires continuous heart-rate/blood-pressure monitoring during extraction and a comparison against competing explanations (psychogenic vasovagal, orthostatic, local-anesthetic effects).

## Key Contributions

- Names TCR as the candidate mechanism of extraction-induced syncope, shifting the question from "syncope happens" to "which reflex arc produces it".
- Asserts that TCR is not confined to general anesthesia — it can be triggered under local anesthesia by maxillary molar extraction.
- Coins/uses the term "dento-cardiac reflex" for the dental-specific trigger.
- Locates the trigger at maxillary molars (index case: maxillary first molar), a testable anatomical claim.
- Provides the mechanistic vocabulary that later dental reviews reuse when they describe syncope pathophysiology.

## Methodology

- **Design**: hypothesis paper (Med Hypotheses article type), not a controlled study.
- **Basis**: one index case of post-extraction TCR plus a stated observation series of 400 extractions under local anesthesia.
- **Measurement**: not stated in the abstract — no monitoring protocol, no defined TCR or syncope criteria, no comparator, no statistics.
- **Exposure**: extraction of maxillary molars under local anesthesia.
- **Outcome of interest**: occurrence of TCR and its relation to syncope.
- **Retrieval status**: abstract-only; the full methods and any results are unavailable to this page.

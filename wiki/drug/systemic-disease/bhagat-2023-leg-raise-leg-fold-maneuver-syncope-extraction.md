## Three-line Summary

Single-centre pilot study (n=30 adults aged 18–75 y with a prior syncope history and dental anxiety, 15 per group) teaching leg raise and leg fold maneuvers before and during extraction, versus conventional extraction.

Post-treatment syncope occurred in 5/15 (33.3%) controls versus 0/15 (0%) in the maneuver group (chi-square 6.000, p=0.014), with giddiness 9/15 (60.0%) vs 3/15 (20.0%) (p=0.025); post-op systolic BP was 136 ± 8.281 vs 109.33 ± 8.837 mmHg and pulse 80.07 ± 4.728 vs 68.93 ± 3.515 bpm (both p<0.001).

The largest dental-extraction signal for a physical counterpressure prophylaxis to date, but from 30 unblinded patients with an unadjusted baseline age imbalance (p=0.026) — hypothesis-generating, not established prophylaxis.

## Summary

Bhagat et al. ran a single-centre pilot study at a government dental college in Chennai, India, in 30 adults aged 18–75 years who all had a previous history of syncope and dental anxiety. Fifteen patients were taught two physical counterpressure maneuvers — five leg raises to at least 15 cm per leg, then five knee-extension leg folds per leg, performed while seated in the dental chair — and were instructed to perform them before local anesthesia, at intervals during the extraction, and after the procedure if needed; the other 15 received conventional extraction. Blood pressure, SpO2 and pulse rate were recorded pre-, intra- and post-operatively, and clinical signs/symptoms (syncope, giddiness, sweating, cold clammy skin) were charted. No patient in the maneuver group fainted after treatment, versus 5 of 15 (33.3%) controls (chi-square 6.000, p=0.014), and giddiness was also less frequent (3/15, 20.0% vs 9/15, 60.0%; p=0.025), while sweating and cold clammy skin did not differ. In the maneuver group, post-operative systolic BP rose from 116 ± 9.856 to 136 ± 8.281 mmHg and pulse from 72.20 ± 3.167 to 80.07 ± 4.728 bpm (both p<0.001); between groups, post-op systolic BP, diastolic BP and pulse were all higher in the maneuver arm (p<0.001). The authors attribute this to augmented venous return and cardiac output from large-muscle isometric contraction, and recommend the maneuvers as a free, low-risk adjunct for anxious patients with prior syncope. The claim should be read cautiously: this is a 30-patient pilot with no described sequence generation, allocation concealment or blinding, a significant baseline age imbalance (p=0.026) that was not adjusted for, syncope ascertained from unblinded clinical observation, and extra clinician attention/education given only to the maneuver group.

## Key Contributions

- Provides the only dental-extraction trial in the wiki that tests a physical counterpressure maneuver as **prophylaxis** (taught and rehearsed before treatment), rather than as abortive management of an established faint.
- Quantifies the signal in a deliberately enriched high-risk sample (100% prior syncope, dental anxiety), with an absolute risk reduction of 33.3 percentage points for post-treatment syncope (5/15 → 0/15) and 40 points for giddiness (9/15 → 3/15).
- Operationalizes the maneuver into a doable chairside protocol (5 raises ≥15 cm + 5 knee folds per leg, avoid jerky movements) that does not obstruct the operator.
- Links the mechanism to muscle-mass-dependent sympathetic activation, citing non-dental counterpressure literature (hand grip, squatting, isometric arm contraction) rather than dental data.
- Documents feasibility and acceptance: no patient failed to perform the maneuver, but repeated verbal reminders were required and a few patients had jerky movements attributed to anxiety.

## Methodology

- **Design**: pilot study, two parallel groups of 15, single centre (Tamil Nadu Government Dental College and Hospital, Chennai, India). The abstract says patients were "randomly divided"; the Methods say only that patients "were divided into two groups consisting of 15 patients each", with no sequence generation, no allocation concealment, and no blinding described.
- **Population**: 18–75 y with previous syncope history and dental anxiety. Excluded: musculoskeletal disease, suspected/overt heart disease with likely cardiac syncope, orthostatic hypotension, non-syncopal loss of consciousness, cognitive/physical incapacity, doubtful compliance, inaccessible follow-up, pregnancy, unwillingness to consent.
- **Intervention**: pre-operative education and rehearsal of leg raise (5× to ≥15 cm, each leg separately or combined) then leg fold (5× knee extension per leg, no jerky movements), performed before local anesthesia, at regular intraoperative intervals, and postoperatively if required.
- **Control**: conventional extraction.
- **Rescue protocol (both arms)**: stop the procedure, place chair in reverse Trendelenburg, follow standard syncope management.
- **Outcomes**: BP (manual sphygmomanometer), SpO2 and pulse rate (pulse oximeter) pre-/intra-/post-op; clinical syncope and features (giddiness, sweating, cold clammy skin). VVS diagnosed per ESC guideline definition.
- **Statistics**: chi-square for categorical outcomes; paired t-test within the maneuver group; independent-samples t-test between groups. n=30.

## Appraisal

- **Evidence level**: the paper calls itself a pilot RCT in the abstract, but randomization is asserted only in that one sentence — the Methods do not describe sequence generation or allocation concealment, no blinding is mentioned for patients, operator or outcome assessor, and the significant baseline age imbalance is what one expects when allocation is loosely handled. Ingested as `prospective` rather than `rct` (logged as a confidence-judgment deviation).
- **Precision**: 5/15 vs 0/15 with no reported confidence interval for the risk difference; the p=0.014 chi-square is fragile to a single event (one syncope in the maneuver arm would erase significance).
- **Ascertainment**: syncope and giddiness were clinical judgements by an unblinded clinician; "giddiness" has no operational definition, opening the door to expectancy effects in a group that received extra education.
- **Co-intervention**: the maneuver arm got teaching, rehearsal, and repeated reminders — attention and rapport alone can lower anxiety-driven vasovagal events, so the effect cannot be attributed cleanly to muscle contraction.
- **Hemodynamic inference**: the between-group post-op BP/HR comparison has no between-group baseline comparison, and the groups differed in age; a rise in SBP to 136 mmHg and pulse to 80 bpm is equally consistent with exercise-induced sympathetic activation as with "improved hemodynamics". The diastolic within-group change was not significant (p=0.054).
- **Rescue position error**: the paper describes reverse Trendelenburg (head up) as the rescue position for developing syncope; conventional management — and the rest of this wiki's pages — uses supine with legs elevated / Trendelenburg. Do not copy this detail.
- **Bottom line**: consistent in direction with non-dental counterpressure evidence, cheap and low-risk to try, but not sufficient to state that leg raise/leg fold "prevents" syncope during extraction. A blinded, adequately powered trial with concealed allocation is still missing.

## Three-line Summary

Case series (n = 2) from a Japanese dental anesthesiology department, reporting two patients with dental phobia and prior vasovagal syncope (VVS) during vaccination, venipuncture, and dental treatment with local anesthetics, both scheduled for dental treatment under intravenous sedation.

Both again developed VVS during venipuncture with a 24-gauge indwelling needle; treating pain as the main trigger, 60% lidocaine tape applied 3 hours before venipuncture at their next visits permitted comfortable IV catheter placement without any VVS.

This is hypothesis-generating, abstract-level evidence with no control and only two patients, and the setting is venipuncture for sedation — it does not support transferring the 3-hour lidocaine tape protocol to routine dental local anesthetic injection.

## Summary

This two-patient case series from Asahi University's dental anesthesiology department describes patients in whom venous access itself, not the dental procedure, was the failing step. Both patients had dental phobia and histories of vasovagal syncope during vaccination, venipuncture, and dental treatment with local anesthetics, and both were scheduled for dental treatment under intravenous sedation — a setting that requires an IV line before sedation can begin. At their scheduled visits both experienced VVS during venipuncture with a 24-gauge indwelling needle. The authors judged pain to be the main trigger and, at the subsequent visits, applied 60% lidocaine tape 3 hours before venipuncture; in both cases the tape allowed comfortable placement of the IV catheter without any onset of VVS. The report is a proof-of-concept for pre-procedural topical anaesthesia in needle-triggered reflex syncope, but it carries no control condition, no haemodynamic data, and no follow-up beyond the described visits, and its subject is venipuncture for sedation rather than intraoral local anaesthesia.

## Key Contributions

- Supplies a concrete, reproducible pre-procedural protocol for needle-triggered VVS in dentistry: 60% lidocaine tape, 3 hours before venipuncture.
- Argues from the case sequence that pain was the operative trigger in these two patients — they fainted at the needle despite being booked for sedation, and blocking the needle pain changed the outcome.
- Places topical anaesthesia upstream of sedation: it is usable in the patient in whom sedation cannot start *because* IV access keeps failing.

## Methodology

Retrospective, descriptive case series of two patients in a single dental anesthesiology department. Both had dental phobia and prior VVS during vaccination, venipuncture, and dental treatment with local anesthetics, and both were scheduled for dental treatment under IV sedation. Index event: VVS during venipuncture with a 24-gauge indwelling needle. Intervention at the next visit: 60% lidocaine tape applied 3 hours before venipuncture. Outcome: success of IV catheter placement and absence of VVS. No control group, no randomisation, no blinding, no reported monitoring or haemodynamic values. ## Clinical Reading (Scope Guard)

- **Venipuncture ≠ dental injection.** The intervention is a *dental anaesthesiology* pre-procedural measure for establishing IV access before sedation. Nothing here transfers to intraoral infiltration, inferior alveolar nerve block, or routine local anaesthesia.
- **Not a general LA premedication protocol.** The 3-hour lead time suits a planned sedation visit; it has no role in unscheduled care and the abstract gives no safety data for prolonged 60% lidocaine occlusion.
- **Evidence weight.** Two uncontrolled cases; the observed success is compatible with expectation/habituation effects at the next visit. Use it to justify a trial of topical anaesthesia in a patient with documented needle-triggered VVS, not as an established standard.

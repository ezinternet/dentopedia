## Three-line Summary

Clinical vignette (Penn Medicine 2023; Dent Clin North Am 67(3):499-501) describing a patient with severe dental anxiety who experienced vasovagal syncope (VVS) during a restorative appointment, with guidance on prodromal recognition and emergency response protocol.

Key teaching: dental anxiety and phobia are themselves vasovagal triggers; providers must recognize the prodrome (facial pallor, diaphoresis, dizziness, nausea/vomiting) and immediately assess airway-breathing-circulation before escalating to BLS and EMS.

No original data — a 2-page management-focused case summary targeting general dental practitioners who may encounter syncopal episodes at the chair.

## Key Clinical Points

**VVS trigger in this context**: extreme dental anxiety / dental phobia → sympathetic activation → rebound vagal surge → vasovagal syncope.

**Prodromal signs and symptoms** (act before loss of consciousness):
- Facial pallor
- Diaphoresis (cold sweat)
- Fainting sensation / dizziness
- Nausea or vomiting

**Management sequence**:
1. Recognize prodrome early
2. Assess ABCs (airway, breathing, cardiovascular status)
3. If intact: recline to Trendelenburg position, remove stimulus, monitor
4. If any ABC element compromised: commence BLS immediately
5. Notify EMS

## Clinical Insights

This paper adds the **dental-anxiety pathway** to the VVS causal map that the wiki's existing papers mostly cover from the procedural side (injection reflex, pain response). The key insight is that psychological fear alone — even before any noxious stimulus — can trigger the vagal cascade, making pre-appointment anxiety screening and anxiolytic consideration part of VVS prevention.

The emphasis on "early recognition" is clinically important: the prodrome window (before loss of consciousness) is the intervention opportunity. Once the patient is unconscious, the response escalates to BLS/EMS.

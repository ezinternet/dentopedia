## 한국어 핵심요약

> [!summary] 한국어 핵심요약
> - 미주신경성 실신 (Vasovagal Syncope, VVS)은 시술 중 가장 흔한 즉각 합병증으로 발생률 **0–4%** (Kennedy 2013: 전체 2.6%, 경막외 스테로이드 주사 3.5%)
> - 핵심 기전: 벨졸트-야리쉬 반사 (Bezold-Jarisch Reflex) — 정맥 울혈 → 압수용체 → 미주신경 긴장 항진 → 서맥 + 저혈압
> - 3가지 유형: 심억제형(심박수 <40 bpm), 혈관억제형(SBP <80 mmHg 또는 30% 이상 감소), 혼합형
> - 가장 강력한 위험인자: **이전 VVS 병력** (재발률 23.3% vs. 예방 시 0%)
> - 기타 위험인자: 남성(2×), 65세 미만(2.4×), 시술 전 통증점수 <5, 미숙련 시술자, 탈수·불안
> - 예방: 의식하 진정 효과적이나 척수 손상 위험 있어 일상 사용 제한; 항무스카린제(glycopyrrolate — 심박수 항진 부작용↓, 아트로핀보다 선호)와 IV fluid는 유망하나 시술 특이적 근거 부재
> - 처치: 즉시 중단 → 제렌델렌부르크 체위 (Trendelenburg Position, 15–30°) → IV fluid → Ephedrine/Glycopyrrolate/Atropine → SBP<90·HR<50 시 응급실 이송
> - 임상 takeaway: 치과 포함 외래 시술에서 이전 VVS 병력 환자 = 고위험군; 항무스카린제 예방 투여(0.2 mg glycopyrrolate IV)는 근거 수준은 낮지만 부작용 프로파일이 양호해 고려 가능

## Three-line Summary

Vasovagal reactions are the most common acute complication of interventional pain procedures (0–4% incidence), mediated by the Bezold–Jarisch reflex causing parasympathetic overdrive with bradycardia and hypotension. Prior VVS history is the strongest risk factor (2× for males, 2.4× for age <65), and moderately sedated procedures show near-zero recurrence, but sedation carries iatrogenic spinal risks. Antimuscarinics (glycopyrrolate preferred) and IV fluids are promising prophylactic options but lack procedure-specific RCT data.

## 세줄요약

시술 중 미주신경성 실신 (Vasovagal Syncope, VVS)은 가장 흔한 즉각 합병증(0–4%)으로, 벨졸트-야리쉬 반사 (Bezold-Jarisch Reflex)를 통한 부교감 신경 항진으로 서맥과 저혈압이 발생한다. 이전 VVS 병력이 가장 강력한 위험인자이며, 의식하 진정으로 재발을 거의 완전히 예방할 수 있으나 척수 손상 위험이 제한 요인이다. 항무스카린제(glycopyrrolate 선호)와 정맥 수액은 예방 효과가 기대되나 시술 특이적 무작위 대조 시험(RCT) 근거가 아직 부재하다.

## Pathophysiology

**벨졸트-야리쉬 반사 (Bezold-Jarisch Reflex)** is the primary mechanism:

1. Excessive venous pooling (orthostasis, epidural sympathetic blockade)
2. Decreased blood pressure sensed by baroreceptors (aortic arch, carotid sinus, heart walls, intrathoracic vessels)
3. Relay to nucleus tractus solitarius
4. Sympathetic inhibition + vagal tone increase
5. → Hypotension + bradycardia → syncope if severe

External stimuli (fear, anxiety, pain, blood exposure, heat) activate an additional pathway: increased cardiac contractility despite underfilled left ventricle → ventricular mechanoreceptors → vagal afferents → parasympathetic overdrive.

VVS is the most common syncope type — up to **40%** of all outpatient syncopal events.

### Classification

| Type | HR | SBP |
|---|---|---|
| Cardioinhibitory | < 40 bpm | Normal |
| Vasodepressor | Normal | < 80 mmHg or >30% drop |
| Mixed | < 40 bpm | < 80 mmHg or >30% drop |

## Incidence

| Study | Population | VVS Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Kennedy 2013 | >8,000 fluoroscopic procedures (5 years) | **2.6% overall; 3.5% ESI** |
| Trentman 2009 | Cervical vs. lumbar ESI | Cervical 8% vs. Lumbar 1% |
| Kennedy 2013 (cervical vs. lumbar) | CESI vs. LESI | Cervical 0.47% vs. Lumbar 3.67% |
| Literature range | Various interventional pain procedures | **0–4%** |

Note: cervical vs. lumbar ESI data are conflicting across studies; training level confounds both Kennedy datasets.

## Prevention

### Moderate Sedation (midazolam + fentanyl)
- Kennedy 2015 (n=6,364): **0% VVS with sedation** vs. 3.3% without
- High-risk patients (prior VVS, n=134): **0% recurrence with sedation** vs. 23.3% without sedation
- **Risks**: inadvertent spinal cord stimulation masked (67% of ASA cervical closed claims involved sedation); airway compromise, arrhythmia, hypotension
- Conclusion: effective for high-risk patients; not recommended for routine prophylaxis

### Anxiolytics (benzodiazepines)
- Gebhardt 2018 (n=2,747): midazolam 1–2 mg IV → VVS rate halved (15% → 7.5%, p<0.001)
- James 2005: lorazepam 2–4 mg SL → prevented VVS recurrence in 95% of biopsy patients
- No interventional pain–specific studies; extrapolated from other settings
- Limitations: sedation, fall risk, confusion, anterograde amnesia (especially elderly)

### Antimuscarinics
| Agent | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Glycopyrrolate** | Preferred: greater potency, longer duration, selective salivary/sweat gland effect, minimal cardiac/CNS effects, less tachycardia than atropine |
| Atropine | Effective for cardioinhibitory form (Santini 1999: 69.7% vs. 21.9% placebo); no effect on vasodepressor form alone |

- Prophylactic efficacy demonstrated across: femoral sheath removal, cryoablation (all 3 VVS forms prevented), C-sections (bradycardia), ophthalmological surgery
- **Gap**: no RCT for interventional pain procedures; Mahajan 2008 recommends glycopyrrolate 0.2 mg IV increments for high-risk patients (expert opinion only)

### IV Fluids
- Corrects pre-procedure dehydration (fasting patients: clear liquids restricted 2 h, solids 6 h per ASA guidelines)
- Provides line for immediate pharmacological intervention
- **Gap**: no studies on standalone IV fluid prophylaxis for interventional pain VVS

## Management Protocol

```
1. Stop procedure immediately
2. Cold compress on neck
3. Position: supine → Trendelenburg 15–30° (head below feet)
4. Counterpressure: squatting, leg crossing
5. If above fails:
   - Start IV fluids (if not running)
   - Ephedrine 5–10 mg IV increments
   - Glycopyrrolate 0.2 mg IV increments
   - Atropine 0.4–1.0 mg IV increments
6. Transfer to ED if: SBP < 90 mmHg | MAP < 65 mmHg | HR < 50 bpm
```

Pre-procedure monitoring for all patients: pulse oximetry + ECG + BP monitoring.

## Clinical Relevance for Dentistry

Although this review focuses on interventional pain management procedures, the mechanisms, risk factors, and management apply directly to outpatient dental procedures:

- **Same VVS triggers**: anxiety, pain, sight of blood, local anesthetic injection, prolonged supine positioning
- **Risk stratification**: prior VVS history = highest risk; young males; pre-procedure anxiety
- **Applicable prevention**: consider glycopyrrolate 0.2 mg IV for high-risk patients; oral anxiolytics (benzodiazepines) for anxious patients
- **Management**: Trendelenburg position + IV atropine/glycopyrrolate aligns with BLS/ACLS dental office protocols
- See also: [[jevon-2020-management-anaphylaxis-dental-practice-update]] for anaphylaxis protocols

## Evidence Assessment

| Domain | Rating | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Pathophysiology | Well-established | Bezold-Jarisch mechanism; tilt-table validated |
| Incidence data | Good | Kennedy 2013 n>8,000; multi-study range 0–4% |
| Risk factor data | Moderate | Kennedy 2013 primary source; some extrapolation |
| Prevention evidence | Weak–moderate | Sedation: good evidence; anxiolytics/antimuscarinics: indirect evidence from other settings |
| Management | Expert consensus | No RCTs; standard emergency medicine approach |

**Evidence level**: Narrative review — integrates studies of varying quality; no meta-analysis. Sedation data strongest (Kennedy 2015); antimuscarinic prophylaxis for this specific context remains the key research gap.

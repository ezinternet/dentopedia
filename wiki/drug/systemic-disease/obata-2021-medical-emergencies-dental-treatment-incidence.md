## Three-line Summary

Single-center retrospective observational study of medical emergencies in the dental outpatient department of Okayama University Hospital (Japan, April 2012–March 2019), with a denominator of 1,146,929 dental outpatient visits and 42 emergencies (0.0037%; derived 3.66 per 100,000 visits, ~1 per 27,300).
Vasovagal syncope dominated (26/42, 61.9%), followed by dehydration (4, 9.5%), hypoglycemia (4, 9.5%), seizure (3, 7.1%) and unexpected bleeding (3, 7.1%); 19 (45.2%) occurred during extraction and events were split nearly evenly before (17), during (12) and after (13) treatment; 90.5% recovered the same day and 9.5% were hospitalised.
Because a case required both an emergency call and an i.v. line, this is a floor estimate of severe emergencies — not the incidence of vasovagal syncope or of all adverse events — and its single-university-hospital setting limits generalisation.

## Summary

This is the denominator paper the dental-emergency literature usually lacks. Over eight years the dental outpatient department of Okayama University Hospital recorded 1,146,929 visits and 42 medical emergencies — an incidence of 0.0037%, or a derived 3.66 events per 100,000 visits (~1 per 27,300 visits). The numerator is dominated by vasovagal syncope (26/42, 61.9%), with dehydration and hypoglycemia at 9.5% each, then seizure and unexpected bleeding at 7.1% each. Extraction accounted for 45.2% of events, general dental treatment 28.6%, and other surgery (implant placement, maxillofacial trauma) 14.3%. Events were distributed almost evenly before (17), during (12) and after (13) treatment — the pre-treatment group was older (median 59 vs 26 years, P = 0.030) and far more comorbid (88.2% vs 15.4%, P < 0.001). Rescue was medical: 41/42 (97.6%) received i.v. fluids, 14 (33.3%) needed emergency-department support, 38 (90.5%) recovered and went home the same day, 4 (9.5%) were hospitalised, and there were no cardiac arrests.

The critical caveat is built into the case definition: an event only entered the numerator if it triggered an emergency call **and** an i.v. line. Ordinary chairside syncope managed by positioning and observation never appears. So 0.0037% is a **floor estimate of severe emergencies as defined**, not the incidence of vasovagal syncope, and it cannot be read as "syncope is vanishingly rare in dentistry" — it means *escalation* to a medical rescue is vanishingly rare per visit. For a denominator-loving reader this is still the best available per-visit anchor: it converts the survey literature's "46% of dentists saw a syncope last year" into "one rescue-level event per ~27,000 visits".

## Key Contributions

- **The denominator itself.** 1,146,929 visits over 8 years is the number that lets every other dental-emergency paper's frequency claim be expressed per visit instead of per dentist-year.
- **A typed numerator with counts, not just percentages** — VVS 26, dehydration 4, hypoglycemia 4, seizure 3, bleeding 3, other 2 — so the tail is visible rather than compressed into "others".
- **Timing as an independent axis.** A third of events occur *after* treatment ends (13/42) and 40.5% *before* it starts, which is decisive for where monitoring and recovery space should sit in a clinic workflow — not just "during the procedure".
- **Pre-treatment risk signature.** Older age and comorbidity concentrate in the "before" group (88.2% comorbid, median 59 years) — i.e. the waiting room, not the chair, is where the fragile patient decompensates.
- **Disposition data, which surveys cannot give**: 90.5% same-day recovery / 9.5% hospitalisation and 0 cardiac arrests — a concrete severity anchor for consent conversations and for staffing decisions.
- **Bleeding cohort detail**: 2 of 3 unexpected-bleeding cases were on antithrombotic therapy.

## Methodology

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| Design | Single-center retrospective observational study (electronic medical records) |
| Setting | Dental outpatient department, Okayama University Hospital, Japan ("Advanced Treatment Hospital": 50 departments, 853 beds, 12 dental departments / 20 dental beds; ~165,000 dental patients/year, 150 dental units in 2019) |
| Period | 2012-04-01 to 2019-03-31 (8 years) |
| Denominator | 1,146,929 dental outpatient visits |
| Case definition | Required medical intervention with **emergency medical call + establishment of an i.v. line**; cases extracted by i.v.-line use, then reviewed by the authors |
| Timing groups | before = scheduled time → start of treatment; during = start → end; after = end → home/discharge |
| Variables | age, sex, underlying disease, emergency type, onset timing, treatment type, responders, medications, disposition |
| Statistics | median (IQR); Kruskal–Wallis for continuous, Fisher's exact for categorical; P < 0.05; SPSS 14.0 |
| Ethics | IRB 2011-028, consent waived |

## Clinical Relevance

1. **Put the number in the consent conversation, with the caveat attached.** "About 1 in 27,000 visits needs a medical rescue" is honest only when paired with "mild syncope managed by positioning is far more common and was not counted here". Using 0.0037% as reassurance without the floor-estimate caveat misrepresents the paper.
2. **Monitoring does not start when the bur touches the tooth.** 40.5% of events were *before* treatment and 31.0% *after*. Pre-operative intake (comorbidity, fasting status, glucose) and a supervised recovery/seating period matter as much as intra-operative vigilance.
3. **Pre-treatment events belong to the old and comorbid** (88.2% comorbid, median 59 y, P < 0.001 vs after group). The waiting room is a clinical area: a hypertensive/diabetic patient who has been fasting since the night before and is anxious is the highest-yield pre-brief target.
4. **Fasting diabetics are a distinct failure mode.** Hypoglycemia (4/42, 9.5%) was almost entirely pre-treatment (3 of 4), whereas dehydration clustered post-treatment (3 of 4) — two opposite fluid/glucose problems at opposite ends of the visit.
5. **Extraction remains the index procedure** (45.2%), which is where VVS prophylaxis evidence (positioning, counterpressure, anxiety management — see [[drug/systemic-disease/bhagat-2023-leg-raise-leg-fold-maneuver-syncope-extraction]]) should be deployed.
6. **Training is justified by severity, not frequency.** 9.5% hospitalised and 33.3% requiring emergency-department support out of only 42 events — rare does not mean benign, which is exactly the argument of the preparedness surveys ([[drug/systemic-disease/smereka-2019-preparedness-and-attitudes-towards-medical]], [[drug/systemic-disease/choufani-2025-lebanese-dentists-preparedness-to-deal]]).
7. **Antithrombotic patients bleed.** 2 of 3 unexpected-bleeding cases were on antithrombotic therapy — airway-protection planning, not just haemostasis, is the issue.

## Limitations

- **Case definition = floor estimate (the headline limitation).** Requiring both an emergency call and an i.v. line excludes every syncope managed with supine positioning and observation. The 0.0037% must never be cited as the incidence of vasovagal syncope or of adverse events generally. The authors themselves state that less-intervention cases "were missing from our analysis".
- **Single center, and an atypical one.** A large university "Advanced Treatment Hospital" with 12 dental departments and 20 dental beds plausibly sees more medically complex patients than a community clinic; the authors flag this as non-generalisable to smaller outpatient clinics.
- **Even this size does not capture the worst events.** The authors note severe emergencies were so rare that the analysis does not reveal the full picture of the most severe presentations; zero cardiac arrests means no inference about cardiac arrest incidence is possible.
- **n = 42 numerator.** Percentages of 7.1% are 3 cases; the timing-group comparisons rest on cells of 0–10 and several Fisher's exact tests have expected counts below 5. The "roughly equal thirds" timing pattern is descriptive, not a tested hypothesis (emergency-type differences across groups were non-significant).
- **No treatment-specific denominators.** 19 extraction events cannot become a per-extraction rate because the visit denominator is not broken down by procedure — the single most useful number this design could have produced is missing.
- **Denominator wording.** The paper reports "1,146,929 patients visited"; this is a visit count, not unique patients, so "per patient" language would be wrong.
- **Retrospective attribution of emergency type.** Dehydration and "other" are clinical judgements by the responding team without measured fluid deficit or a diagnostic protocol; misclassification among VVS/dehydration/other is plausible.
- **Table 1 lists each case with age, sex, comorbidity and date-independent detail** — a re-identification hazard in a single-center series. This page reports aggregates only (age/sex-level detail, no identifiers).
- **Japan-specific system**: in-hospital emergency call + immediate emergency-physician availability is a university-hospital capability; a community clinic's denominator and numerator would both differ, and the paper's own conclusion (education + first-aid drills) is a preparedness argument rather than an incidence generalisation.

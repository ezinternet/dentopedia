## Three-line Summary

Narrative commentary on perioperative alcohol withdrawal syndrome in head and neck cancer surgery, synthesising addiction-medicine principles for surgeons.

Postoperative withdrawal is associated with increased medical and surgical complications, longer hospital stay, and higher costs; harm-reduction and addiction-medicine approaches can reduce morbidity and mortality but are not fully validated in this surgical population.

For dentistry the value is as a prompt, not a protocol: the heavy-drinking history that predicts implant failure also predicts withdrawal, and it should be screened for before surgery rather than discovered afterwards.

## Summary

This commentary addresses a failure mode the rest of this wiki's alcohol literature does not: the patient who is physically dependent, where the perioperative danger is withdrawal rather than poor healing. Head and neck cancer surgical patients are described as at elevated risk, and postoperative withdrawal is associated with more medical and surgical complications, longer hospitalisation, and higher hospital-related costs. The authors argue that harm-reduction and addiction-medicine approaches can reduce morbidity and mortality, while being explicit that these approaches have not been fully validated in perioperative head and neck cancer surgery. It is a framing document, not a protocol: it tells the clinician to screen and to involve addiction medicine, and it supplies no dosing, no severity thresholds, and no effect sizes.

## Key Contributions

- **Names withdrawal as the perioperative outcome of heavy drinking**, distinct from infection, bleeding, and non-integration.
- **Attaches cost and length-of-stay consequences** to the problem, which is what makes it a departmental rather than individual concern.
- **Introduces harm reduction as a legitimate perioperative philosophy**, i.e. an alternative to the abstinence-or-nothing framing patients often hear.
- **Is honest about its evidence status**, stating that validation in this specific surgical population is incomplete.

## Methodology

- **Design**: commentary synthesising addiction-medicine principles and current perioperative strategies.
- **Target readership**: otolaryngology–head and neck surgery.
- **Scope**: perioperative assessment and management of alcohol withdrawal syndrome.
- **Evidence base**: narrative — no systematic search, no primary data, no pooled estimates.

## Results

- Head and neck cancer surgical patients: **increased risk** of alcohol withdrawal syndrome.
- Postoperative withdrawal associated with: increased medical complications, increased surgical complications, longer length of stay, higher hospital-related costs.
- Harm-reduction/addiction-medicine strategies: presented as morbidity- and mortality-reducing, **not fully validated** perioperatively in head and neck cancer.
- No incidence, effect size, or protocol dose is reported in the available text.

## Clinical Reading

- **Screen, do not assume.** A patient who volunteers "a few drinks" and a patient in physiological dependence can present identically in a dental chair; the operative question is the pattern and the previous withdrawal history.
- **Treat the dental procedure as the trigger.** Even a short inpatient stay or an sedation/general anaesthetic episode can precipitate withdrawal in a dependent patient — plan for it rather than react.
- **Dentistry is not head and neck oncology.** Absolute withdrawal risk in a routine extraction or implant patient is far lower; this page justifies *asking and referring*, not instituting a benzodiazepine protocol in a dental practice.
- **The referral is the intervention.** The actionable step is a handover to addiction medicine or the patient's physician when heavy intake is disclosed — the commentary's own prescription.

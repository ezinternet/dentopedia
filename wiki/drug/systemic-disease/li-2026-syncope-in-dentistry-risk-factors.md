## Three-line Summary

Narrative review of vasovagal syncope (VVS) in dental practice, synthesizing 5 databases (Google Scholar, EMBASE, PubMed, ScienceDirect, Cochrane; July 1997–October 2025) on etiology, trigeminocardiac/psychogenic pathophysiology, risk factors, prevention, P–CAB–D management, and recovery criteria.
VVS accounts for more than 60% of reported dental-office emergency incidents and over 50% of all syncope cases; local anesthetic administration triggers approximately 75% of episodes, 39% occur before the procedure starts, and male adolescents are 2.69-fold more likely to faint after dental injection.
Management is positioning-first (supine with legs level or slightly higher; Trendelenburg 15–30° controversial) with atropine 0.5 mg IV up to 3 mg for symptomatic bradycardia and ephedrine 5 mg IV (max 50 mg) for hypotension with heart rate >60/min; the evidence base is narrative and Tables 1–7 were missing from the extracted text.

## Summary

This narrative review (J Dent Anesth Pain Med 2026;26(1):29-40) consolidates the dental-specific evidence on vasovagal syncope, the single most common medical emergency in dental practice. The authors searched five databases from July 1997 to October 2025 and organized the material into etiology, pathophysiology, clinical phases, predisposing risk factors, prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and recovery. Vasovagal syncope is reported to account for more than 60% of dental-office emergency incidents and over 50% of all syncope cases, with local anesthetic administration the dominant procedural trigger (approximately 75%), 39% of episodes occurring before treatment begins, and male adolescents carrying 2.69-fold the risk of female adolescents after dental injection. Two mechanisms are described: a trigeminocardiac reflex driven by maxillary (CN V2) and mandibular (CN V3) afferents, and a psychogenic neurocardiogenic reflex routed through the central nucleus of the amygdala. Management follows the P–CAB–D algorithm, with supine positioning (legs level or slightly elevated) preferred over Trendelenburg, oxygen 6–10 L/min for conscious and 10–15 L/min by bag-valve mask for unconscious patients, atropine 0.5 mg IV every 3–5 min (max 3 mg) for symptomatic bradycardia, and ephedrine 5 mg IV (max 50 mg) for hypotension with heart rate >60 beats/min. Clinically, the review's value is operational — triage thresholds (ASA III screening, age <10 or >40 with unexplained syncope needing urgent referral) and recovery criteria (>2 min to consciousness or >15–20 min for full recovery suggest an alternative diagnosis) — but it is not systematic and does not grade its evidence.

## Key Contributions

- Quantifies the dental burden of syncope in one place: 3 of 4 dentists face a medical emergency in their careers, >60% of incidents are VVS, 45% of dentists manage ≥1 case per year (mean 1.2), and ~36% feel inadequately prepared.
- Maps the trigeminocardiac reflex anatomy for dentistry — CN V2/V3 → Gasserian ganglion → trigeminal sensory nucleus → reticular formation → nucleus ambiguus and dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus → AV-node parasympathetic drive → bradycardia, hypotension, occasional transient asystole.
- Separates a second, centrally mediated pathway (central nucleus of the amygdala inhibiting the nucleus tractus solitarius and rostral ventrolateral medulla) to explain fear/anxiety-triggered syncope.
- Structures prevention in three tiers: pre-appointment screening (ASA III or higher, poorly controlled hypertension, type 2 diabetes, asthma, COPD), psychological/behavioral management (Corah Dental Anxiety Scale, CBT, guided breathing, audiovisual distraction, counterpressure maneuvers), and pharmacology (nitrous oxide or oral diazepam conscious sedation, IV sedation/GA for severe phobia).
- Standardizes chairside management as P–CAB–D and anchors the resuscitation numbers to AHA 2020 and ERC 2021 (100–120 compressions/min, 30:2; 15:2 for two-rescuer pediatric) plus ERC atropine and FDA ephedrine dosing.
- Adds a distinct protocol for orthostatic hypotension (dental chair inclined ~10° on subsequent visits; midodrine or fludrocortisone if supportive measures fail) rather than folding it into VVS care.

## Methodology

Narrative (non-systematic) review. Databases: Google Scholar, EMBASE, PubMed, ScienceDirect, Cochrane Database; search window July 1997–October 2025. Search terms: "vasovagal syncope", "vasovagal prevention", "anxiolytic vasovagal", "syncope dental management", "dental emergencies", combined with "anxiolytic behavior management", "sedation management", "anticholinergic pharmacology". Inclusion: guidelines, systematic reviews, RCTs, cohort/cross-sectional/case-control/interventional studies and relevant textbooks, in adults and children receiving dental treatment under local anesthesia or conscious sedation. Exclusion: non-English publications, editorials, animal studies. No PRISMA flow, no risk-of-bias tool, no pooled estimate; n is not applicable. Outcomes are descriptive: epidemiology, mechanism, risk factors, preventive strategies, diagnostic workup, treatment steps, recovery/referral criteria.

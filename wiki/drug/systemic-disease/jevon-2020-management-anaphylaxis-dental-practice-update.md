## Three-line Summary

Narrative clinical update (British Dental Journal 2020), aligned to Resuscitation Council UK guidance, on recognising and treating anaphylaxis chairside — written around the UK adrenaline auto-injector (AAI) supply crisis that now requires general dental practitioners to draw up and give intramuscular (IM) adrenaline from a glass ampoule.

UK anaphylaxis hospital admissions rose ~700% (1→7 per 100,000/yr, 1992–2012, ~20 deaths/yr); dental triggers are antibiotics (mainly amoxicillin; ~50% of UK perioperative anaphylaxis, 4.0 per 100,000 administrations), chlorhexidine (two UK deaths after socket irrigation), latex, local anaesthetic (true allergy ~1% of the 0.1–1% LA adverse events), Recaldent toothpaste and iodoform/Alvogyl; IM adrenaline 500 µg (>12 y) into the anterolateral middle-third of the thigh, repeated every 5 min, is the one life-saving act and ~40% of UK anaphylaxis deaths got no adrenaline.

Stock adrenaline 1:1,000 (1 mg/mL) ampoules with 23G 25 mm (blue) needles; current IM thigh technique drops routine gloves, alcohol swab, skin–hub gap, aspiration and post-injection massage; an AAI is now a fallback only when it is the sole adrenaline preparation available.

## Summary

This 2020 British Dental Journal update sets out how the dental team should recognise and manage anaphylaxis following Resuscitation Council UK guidance, with the practical emphasis shifted by the national AAI shortage: general dental practitioners are now expected to draw up adrenaline 1:1,000 from an ampoule and give it intramuscularly with a syringe and needle. It compiles the dental-specific triggers (antibiotics — chiefly amoxicillin; chlorhexidine in mouthwash and as socket irrigant; latex; local anaesthetic and its excipients; Recaldent toothpaste; iodoform/Alvogyl) with UK epidemiology showing a ~700% rise in admissions over two decades. The core message is unchanged and blunt: prompt IM adrenaline is the only intervention that changes outcome, delay kills, and ~40% of UK anaphylaxis deaths received no adrenaline at all. The article also documents the de-escalated IM thigh-injection technique (no routine gloves, alcohol swab, skin–hub gap, aspiration or post-injection massage) and the recommended kit (adrenaline 1:1,000 ampoules; 23G 25 mm blue needle; AAI only as last-resort preparation).

## Key Contributions

- Establishes ampoule-drawn IM adrenaline as a required GDP competency (not an AAI substitute), driven by AAI supply failure and product recalls.
- Dental trigger list with UK numbers: antibiotics (amoxicillin) ~50% of perioperative anaphylaxis; chlorhexidine socket irrigation implicated in two UK deaths (MHRA alert 2014; SDCEP advises saline for dry socket); latex-induced anaphylaxis in dentistry vanishingly rare (one rubber-dam case in 20 years; zero in a 266-case perioperative series).
- Local-anaesthetic reality check: 0.1–1% adverse events, ~1% of those truly allergic; least allergenic amides = mepivacaine and plain prilocaine; many reactions are to excipients (benzoates, metabisulphite) or are vasovagal/psychogenic.
- Current IM thigh technique: no routine gloves, no alcohol swab, no skin–hub gap, no aspiration, no post-injection massage.
- Restates Resuscitation Council UK dosing (500 µg IM for >12 y; repeat at 5 min) and the AAI-as-last-resort rule for healthcare settings.

## Methodology

Narrative clinical update; no systematic search, no pooled analysis. Draws on Resuscitation Council UK, MHRA, SDCEP, Public Health England, Chief Dental Officer for England, Department of Health and GDC guidance, plus case reports and national audit data (e.g. a UK review of 266 perioperative anaphylaxis cases; a series of 593 anaphylaxis cases; a UK anaphylaxis death registry). Structured as: definition → incidence → dental causes → clinical features and differential diagnosis → adrenaline (dose, route, through-clothing, allergy to adrenaline) → emergency drug kit → suggested IM injection procedure → management algorithm → debrief, documentation, reporting.

### Dental triggers

- **Antibiotics** — amoxicillin (most common), phenoxymethylpenicillin, metronidazole. Deaths from amoxicillin anaphylaxis reported.
- **Chlorhexidine** — mouthwashes, toothpastes, implants, and socket irrigation. Two UK deaths after chlorhexidine mouthwash used to irrigate extraction sockets; MHRA alert 2014. SDCEP: use saline (not chlorhexidine) to irrigate a dry socket; COVID-era advice — warm saltwater rinse.
- **Local anaesthetic** — type I (immediate, systemic) or type IV (delayed, local contact dermatitis). Amide allergy very rare; least allergenic = mepivacaine, plain prilocaine. Esters more allergenic (→ PABA); benzocaine is the only ester used in dentistry (topical). Many reactions are to excipients — benzoate preservatives (multi-dose vials), metabisulphite antioxidants (adrenaline-containing solutions) — or are psychogenic/vasovagal. Latex risk from LA cartridges is minimal.
- **Latex (NRL)** — bungs, gloves, dams; cross-reactive foods banana, kiwi, avocado; worse reactions if uncontrolled asthma. Anaphylaxis extremely rare; fatal latex anaphylaxis — isolated case reports, none in dentistry.
- **Toothpaste / Recaldent (CPP-ACP, milk-derived)** — fatal anaphylaxis in a dairy-allergic US teenager; non-fatal Australian paediatric "tooth mousse" case.
- **Iodoform / Alvogyl** — avoid with allergy to procaine-type anaesthetic, iodine or iodine-related compounds; two anecdotal anaphylaxis reports.

### Recognition and differential

- Rapid onset: skin (redness, itching), mucosal/angioedema swelling, airway swelling (mouth/throat/tongue), wheeze, tachypnoea, tachycardia, hypotension.
- Assess with **ABCDE**. Anaphylaxis is likely if all three criteria are met: sudden onset and rapid progression; life-threatening airway and/or breathing and/or circulation problem; usually with skin/mucosal changes. *(The exact 3-criteria box did not render in the retrieved text.)*
- Differential: panic attack, vasovagal syncope — separate using ABCDE. *(Box did not render.)*

### Adrenaline — chairside essentials

| Parameter | Detail (as stated in the paper) |
|---|---|
| **Drug** | Adrenaline (epinephrine) 1:1,000 = 1 mg/mL |
| **Dose (>12 years)** | **500 µg IM** (Resuscitation Council UK) |
| **Paediatric doses by age** | *Boxed dose list did not render in retrieved text — cross-check the current Resuscitation Council UK anaphylaxis algorithm before use* |
| **Repeat interval** | Every **5 minutes** until adequate response |
| **Route** | **Intramuscular** |
| **Site** | **Anterolateral aspect of the middle third of the thigh** (vastus lateralis) — high plasma level within minutes |
| **Deltoid (arm)** | Slower absorption; not routinely recommended; only "in a desperate situation" |
| **Through clothing** | Bare leg ideal; AAIs may be given through light clothing; ampoule-drawn adrenaline through light clothing is "reasonable" when time-critical — avoid trouser seam and pocket contents |
| **Allergy to adrenaline** | Real but very rare — usually sodium metabisulphite hypersensitivity (preservative in adrenaline 1:1,000 and in all 3 UK AAIs). **Still give adrenaline** — life-saving need outweighs theoretical sulphite risk |
| **Why it matters** | Failure to give adrenaline is the commonest cause of anaphylaxis death; ~40% of UK anaphylaxis deaths received none; delay increases death risk |

### Emergency drug kit and needle

- Stock **adrenaline 1:1,000 (1 mg/mL) ampoules** (prefilled syringes also acceptable; both have had UK supply issues). Chief Dental Officer for England asked practices to replace AAIs with ampoules to preserve national AAI stock.
- **AAIs (UK)**: Emerade, EpiPen, Jext — self-use devices; accidental self-injection reported. Use an AAI **only if it is the only available adrenaline preparation** in a healthcare setting.
- **Needle**: 25 mm (blue **23G 25 mm**) for all ages; a longer **21G 38 mm** may be needed in some adults. Safety needles preferred.
- Follow Public Health England guidance for kit contents. *(Itemised list did not render.)*

### IM thigh injection — current technique (what changed)

| No longer done | Reason |
|---|---|
| Routine gloves | Not necessary (staff usually already gloved) |
| Alcohol-swab skin cleaning | No longer deemed necessary |
| Skin–needle-hub gap ("in case the needle breaks") | Risks subcutaneous, not IM, delivery |
| Aspiration / drawing back | No longer advised for IM thigh injections |
| Massaging the site afterwards | May dispel drug out of the muscle |

- **Opening the glass ampoule**: tap the top first to dispel drug from the neck; cuts and shattering occur with excess pressure; if it shatters, discard glass and contents, use another ampoule. *(Numbered injury-minimisation steps did not render.)*

### Management, debrief, reporting

- Follow the **Resuscitation Council UK anaphylaxis algorithm** (ABCDE; prompt IM adrenaline; repeat at 5 min; call for help using **SBAR/SBARD**). *The algorithm figure and the numbered management list did not render in the retrieved text.*
- **Refractory anaphylaxis**: beyond "repeat at 5-minute intervals until adequate response", the paper defers to the Resuscitation Council UK algorithm (repeat IM adrenaline, IV fluid challenge, specialist/critical-care escalation) — no dedicated refractory section rendered in the retrieved text.
- **Debrief** + significant event analysis (the latter best done later).
- **Documentation**: circumstances immediately before onset (trigger), acute features, time of onset.
- **Reporting**: suspected drug-related anaphylaxis → **MHRA Yellow Card**; fatal anaphylaxis in practice may require **CQC** notification.

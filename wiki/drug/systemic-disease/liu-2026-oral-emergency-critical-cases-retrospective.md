## Three-line Summary

Single-centre retrospective review of 92 critical cases rescued in a Chinese tertiary hospital oral-emergency department between January 2006 and September 2025 (mean age 47, range 4–88, median 49; 62 male, 67.4%).

Maxillofacial haemorrhage (35/92, 38.0%) and cardiovascular accidents (22/92, 23.9%) accounted for 61.9% (57/92) of rescues, followed by upper airway obstruction (17) and vasovagal syncope (13) together 32.6% (30/92); ECG monitoring 98.9%, oxygen 96.7% and venous access 78.3% dominated supportive care, rescue succeeded in 91.3% (84/92) with 8 deaths.

In hospital-based oral emergency the critical load is bleeding and cardiovascular events rather than syncope, so emergency drills there must centre on haemorrhage control and airway rescue; the abstract reports no transfer/hospitalisation rate and no procedure-trigger distribution, and single-centre Chinese tertiary referral limits extrapolation to community clinics.

## Summary

This is a 20-year retrospective chart review of the patients who actually needed *rescue* in a hospital oral-emergency department — the Department of Oral Emergency at Peking University School and Hospital of Stomatology, Beijing (January 2006 – September 2025, n=92 with complete records). Two diagnoses dominated: maxillofacial haemorrhage in 35 patients (38.0%) and cardiovascular accidents in 22 (23.9%), together 61.9% of all rescues; upper airway obstruction (17 cases) and vasovagal syncope (13 cases) formed the next tier at a combined 32.6% (30/92). The haemorrhage group was driven by maxillofacial trauma (20/35, 57.1%) with haemorrhagic shock in 17/35 (48.6%), and maxillofacial tumours were a recurring cause of both secondary bleeding (12/35) and airway obstruction (12/17); space infection caused 5/17 airway obstructions (29.4%). Supportive care was near-universal (ECG monitoring 98.9%, oxygen 96.7%, venous access 78.3%), tracheal intubation led the airway hierarchy, adrenaline was the most-used drug, all 15 cardiac-arrest patients received CPR (8 defibrillated), and 84/92 (91.3%) were rescued with 8 deaths.

The clinical reading is a denominator lesson. Vasovagal syncope is the most common *office* emergency — 46.3% of Polish dentists saw one in a year, and reviews put it above 60% of dental-office incidents — yet here it is 13 of 92 critical cases (14.1%), behind bleeding and cardiovascular events. What separates a dental-office faint from an oral-emergency rescue is not frequency but the presence of haemorrhage, airway compromise or a cardiac event, which is exactly what this cohort is made of. The abstract reports neither the transfer/hospitalisation rate nor a procedure-level trigger distribution (both 미보고), so it cannot answer whether a given dental procedure precedes these events; and because it is one tertiary referral centre whose case mix is skewed by trauma, tumour bleeding and space infection, the proportions should not be transplanted onto community practice.

## Key Contributions

- Supplies a hospital-side denominator that dental-office surveys cannot: the disease composition of *genuine critical* oral-emergency cases over 20 years (n=92).
- Quantifies the case mix — haemorrhage 38.0% and cardiovascular accidents 23.9% (61.9% combined), airway obstruction 17, VVS 13 — and the aetiological split inside haemorrhage (trauma 57.1%, haemorrhagic shock 48.6%, tumour 34.3%).
- Documents the real resuscitation bundle and its outcome (ECG 98.9%/O₂ 96.7%/IV 78.3%; intubation as primary airway measure; adrenaline first-line drug; 91.3% success, 8 deaths with 3 from airway-obstruction cardiac arrest).
- Supports the preparedness argument already established elsewhere: oral departments need drugs, equipment and regular drills, because the critical events that arrive are bleeding, airway and cardiac — not faints.

## Methodology

- **Design**: Retrospective analysis of complete medical records; single tertiary centre.
- **Setting / period**: Department of Oral Emergency, Peking University School and Hospital of Stomatology, Beijing, China; January 2006 – September 2025.
- **n**: 92 critically ill patients with complete records.
- **Variables**: primary diagnosis, age, sex, rescue measures and drugs used, disease outcome (success/death).
- **Analyses reported**: disease composition, key characteristics of clinical treatment, outcome — descriptive only; no multivariable model or era stratification in the abstract.
- **Case definition / total visit denominator**: not stated in the abstract.

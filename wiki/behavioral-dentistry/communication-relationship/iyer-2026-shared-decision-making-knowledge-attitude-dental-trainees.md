## Three-line Summary

Cross-sectional, self-administered questionnaire study (n=176: 3rd-year students/D3 46, 4th-year students/D4 57, interns 38, residents 35; mean age 24.17 ± 2.13) at a single dental college in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia (Aug 2024–Feb 2025), assessing dental trainees' knowledge and attitude toward shared decision-making (SDM).

Knowledge of SDM was "less than satisfactory" across all groups; 6 of 7 attitude items differed significantly by educational level, with the least-experienced (D3) students far more likely to agree patients should trust clinicians to decide unilaterally (OR 13.00, p=0.01, CI 2.21–74.30) and that SDM threatens their clinical expertise (OR 4.20, p=0.05, CI 1.98–17.90); one reported D4 estimate (OR 1.17, p=0.02, CI 1.17–14.50) has a point estimate identical to its own confidence-interval lower bound — an internal inconsistency present in the source's own Table 4, reproduced here as printed rather than silently corrected.

Sample size is modest and split four ways (smallest cell D3 n=46), single-institution/single-city, self-reported attitudes rather than observed consultations, and most confidence intervals are very wide — treat point estimates as directional, not precise.

## Summary

According to PubMed, ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1155/ijod/1543236); PMID 42027399; PMCID PMC13101796) this cross-sectional study surveyed 176 dental students and residents (D3, D4, interns, residents) at a single Riyadh dental college between August 2024 and February 2025, using a validated self-administered questionnaire (content validity S-UA 0.86, test-retest ICC 0.82) covering seven knowledge items and seven attitude items on shared decision-making (SDM). The headline finding is a split: **knowledge** of SDM was assessed as "less than satisfactory" across the board, largely flat across training levels — but **attitude** was not flat at all. Six of seven attitude items differed significantly by educational level, and in every significant case the direction ran the same way: the least-experienced trainees (D3, and to a lesser extent D4) held views most protective of clinician authority and most resistant to patient involvement — that patients should trust clinicians to decide unilaterally, that SDM threatens clinical expertise, that SDM is time-consuming and "unrealistic," and that it is "challenging" because patients might ask them to decide on the patient's behalf.

This matters for a specific reason the paper itself does not frame explicitly: **a knowledge gap and an attitude gap are different kinds of problem.** Not knowing what SDM is can be fixed by a lecture. Believing that involving the patient undermines your own authority is a belief about status and competence, not information, and beliefs of that kind are typically more durable and less responsive to a single curricular intervention. That the belief is strongest in the least experienced trainees — the group with the least invested professional identity to defend — argues against a simple "confidence grows into openness" story and toward something closer to a defensive posture forming early and (per the more favorable attitudes seen in interns and residents) softening only with sustained clinical exposure. The paper's own conclusion gestures at this ("a generational gap... interns and residents exhibiting a more favorable attitude") but does not draw the sharper distinction between what a lecture fixes and what it does not.

Read against the wiki's other holdings, this paper supplies a piece [[overviews/treatment-planning-decision-variability-overview]] explicitly names as missing: that overview shows a clinician's own treatment recommendation (extraction vs. retention, post-treatment apical periodontitis) varies roughly 3× by training level, and argues that this instability is the strongest available reason to institutionalize structured SDM rather than leave "recommend, then discuss" to each clinician's judgment. This paper shows that the population whose recommendations are least anchored — the least experienced — is also the population most likely to believe that inviting the patient into the decision undermines their professional standing. The unstable anchor and the resistance to correcting for it by structural means sit in the same population.

**One number in the source requires a caveat rather than a citation.** Table 4 reports, for "patients should trust clinicians to make all decisions on their behalf," a D4 "somewhat agree" result of OR 1.17 (p=0.02, 95% CI 1.17–14.50) — a point estimate numerically identical to its own confidence-interval lower bound. This is not a typical regression output (an OR sitting exactly at the edge of its own interval) and it is reproduced verbatim in the paper's abstract, so it is the number the authors intended to report, not a table-layout slip. It most plausibly reflects a mistyped conversion from the reported β=1.42 (exp(1.42)=4.14, not 1.17) or a transposed digit, but the source supplies no correction. This wiki reproduces the figure exactly as printed and flags it rather than silently fixing it — treat the direction (D4 more likely to somewhat agree than residents) as credible, but not the specific magnitude 1.17.

## Key Contributions

- First study (per the authors) assessing SDM knowledge and attitude specifically along the dental training pipeline (undergraduate D3/D4 → intern → resident) rather than among already-practicing clinicians — the first regional data point on whether the next generation is ready to practice SDM, not just whether current clinicians endorse it.
- Separates knowledge from attitude and shows they behave differently: knowledge is uniformly weak and largely insensitive to training stage; attitude is sharply stratified by training stage. The obstacle to SDM adoption in this population is not what trainees don't know — it's what they believe about what patient involvement does to their authority.
- Multinomial regression against a residents reference category isolates the specific beliefs driving the least-experienced group's resistance: distrust of shared authority, perceived threat to expertise, and perceived time cost — rather than reporting only an aggregate "favorability" score.
- Validated instrument (Lynn's S-UA content validity 0.86, test-retest ICC 0.82, pilot n=9 excluded from analysis) strengthens confidence that the attitude stratification reflects genuine group differences rather than measurement noise, even though the individual effect-size estimates remain imprecise (see Results).

## Methodology

Descriptive, STROBE-guided cross-sectional study at a single site (College of Dentistry, King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia), IRB-approved (KAIMRC IRB/0451/23). Nonprobability, purposive sampling of all senior dental students in the D3 and D4 undergraduate years plus interns and residents — i.e., **four** training-level strata, not the two implied by the title's shorthand "senior students and residents." Sample size (n=176) estimated via G*Power from a prior-literature 27% proportion, 95% CI, 90% power; response rate skewed slightly female (56.25%). Questionnaire adapted from two prior published instruments, distributed via Google Forms (Aug 2024–Feb 2025) with weekly reminders; 7 knowledge items (yes/no/not sure) and 7 attitude items (5-point Likert). Pearson's chi-square screened for association between knowledge/attitude items and exploratory variables (gender, GPA, education level); items significant at p<0.05 were carried into multinomial logistic regression with **residents** as the reference education-level category and **strongly disagree** as the reference response category (SPSS v20).

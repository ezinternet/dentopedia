## Summary

Omi and Mishina's Genesis review frames the osteoclast as the central effector of alveolar bone remodeling and argues that the alveolar bone cannot be treated as a generic sample of skeletal bone. The alveolar bone — the alveolar process that holds the teeth and supports oral functions — is set apart by three features: its embryonic cellular origin, its form of ossification, and the presence of teeth and periodontal tissues; the review's stated rationale is that understanding this distinctiveness is what makes oral homeostasis maintainable. On the disease side the authors identify excessive osteoclastic resorption as a prominent feature of jaw bone disease and name periodontitis as the exemplar, then make the therapeutic link explicit — inhibiting osteoclast formation and the bone-resorptive process is the target of intervention, and understanding the resorption mechanism is a precondition for treating these diseases effectively. The review closes with perspectives on osteoclast-targeted therapies and regenerative approaches. Because this record is the abstract of a narrative review (no systematic search, no appraisal, no synthesis), the page carries no effect sizes or pooled estimates, and the abstract names no specific signalling pathway or drug.

## Key Contributions

- **Alveolar bone is not generic skeletal bone.** The review's organising premise: the alveolar bone is distinguished by its embryonic cellular origin, its form of ossification, and the presence of teeth and periodontal tissues — so remodeling principles cannot simply be imported from general bone biology into the jaw without accounting for these differences.
- **The osteoclast as central effector, defined by balance.** Large, multinucleated, hematopoietic in origin, responsible for bone resorption; bone homeostasis is framed as resting on the equilibrium between osteoclastic resorption and osteoblastic formation, not on either cell alone.
- **The clinical hinge is excess resorption.** Excessive osteoclastic resorption is named as a prominent feature of jaw bone disease with periodontitis as the exemplar — this is the step that makes the cell biology matter to dentistry.
- **Mechanism precedes therapy.** The stated therapeutic target is inhibition of osteoclast formation and the bone-resorptive process, and the stated precondition for effective treatment of jaw bone disease is understanding that mechanism.
- **Two forward directions, both only as perspectives.** Osteoclast-targeted therapies *and* regenerative approaches for jaw bone disease — neither is specified in the abstract (no agent, material, or technique named), so the regenerative arm is the least defined part of the announced scope.

## Three-line Summary

This systematic review synthesized evidence from 1 randomized controlled trial and 2 cohort studies (total N=623 posterior endodontically treated teeth) comparing survival against fracture between full-coverage crowns and direct resin composite restorations. Pooled survival rates ranged from 94% to 100% for crowns and 91.9% to 100% for resin composite, with no significant difference observed for teeth with up to 3-surface loss. Current evidence suggests that direct resin composite restorations may be a viable alternative to crowns in posterior endodontically treated teeth with minimal to moderate structural loss, though generalization is limited by study heterogeneity and confounding by indication.

## Summary

This systematic review assessed the clinical evidence comparing survival rates against fracture between full-coverage crowns and direct resin composite restorations in endodontically treated posterior teeth. Following a comprehensive search of PubMed, ScienceDirect, Web of Science, MEDLINE, and SCOPUS from 1980 to 2016, 3 studies met inclusion criteria: 1 randomized controlled trial (Mannocci et al., 2002; LOE 2), 1 prospective cohort (Cagidiaco et al., 2007; LOE 3), and 1 retrospective cohort (Dammaschke et al., 2013; LOE 3). The pooled sample comprised 507 teeth restored with crowns and 116 teeth with resin composite restorations. Survival rates against fracture ranged from 94% to 100% for crowns and from 91.9% to 100% for resin composites. The RCT demonstrated that premolars with 2–3 surface loss had >90% survival at 3 years for both restoration types, with no significant difference between groups. Cohort studies confirmed high survival for resin composites in teeth with 1–3 surface loss, but these teeth also had more remaining structure—a potential confounding factor. The authors concluded that current evidence suggests no significant difference in fracture survival between crowns and resin composites in posterior endodontically treated teeth with minimal to moderate tooth structure loss, but emphasized the need for long-term RCTs stratified by tooth type and remaining structure.

## Key Contributions

- Systematic review synthesizing all available clinical studies (RCT + cohort) on crown vs. resin composite survival in endodontically treated posterior teeth.
- Reported pooled survival ranges: 94–100% for crowns, 91.9–100% for resin composites across 623 teeth.
- Provided evidence that teeth with ≤3 surface loss may be successfully restored with direct resin composite without compromising fracture survival.
- Identified key confounding factors: tooth type (premolar vs. molar), number of remaining walls, post placement, and indication bias.
- Highlighted the need for future high-quality RCTs with long-term follow-up stratified by these factors.

## Methodology

This systematic review followed a PICO framework: Population — endodontically treated posterior permanent teeth with mature root formation; Intervention — non-cuspal coverage direct resin composite restoration; Comparison — full-coverage crown; Outcome — survival rate against fracture. A literature search was conducted across 5 electronic databases (PubMed, ScienceDirect, Web of Science, MEDLINE, SCOPUS) from 1980 to 2016, restricted to English-language clinical studies. Seventeen keywords and MeSH terms were used with Boolean operators. Inclusion criteria required clinical studies comparing survival rates between the two restoration types, including randomized/non-randomized controlled trials or cohort studies. Exclusion criteria eliminated case reports, case series, literature reviews, and studies combining anterior/posterior teeth or failing to report restoration-specific survival. A manual search of cited references yielded 3 additional articles, but only 9 passed title/abstract screening. Full-text review excluded 6 studies for insufficient data (e.g., combined numbers with amalgam, mixed anterior/posterior, or aggregated restoration types). The final 3 studies were assessed for level of evidence using the Oxford Centre for Evidence Based Medicine (OCEBM) criteria. Due to clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies (different designs, follow-up periods, tooth types, post placement protocols), meta-analysis was not performed; data were synthesized narratively.

## Results

| RCT (Mannocci 2002) — Crown vs. Resin, 3-year | >90% vs. >90% | NR | No significant difference | – |
| Cohort (Cagidiaco 2007) — Crown vs. Resin, 2-year | 100% vs. 100% | NR | NR | – |
| Cohort (Dammaschke 2013) — Crown vs. Resin | 94% vs. 91.9% | NR | NR | – |

The included studies were too heterogeneous for meta-analysis. Most teeth had ≤3 surface loss. In the cohort studies, resin composite restorations were preferentially placed in teeth with less structural loss, which may have favorably biased their survival estimates. Post placement varied: fiber posts were used in all teeth in Cagidiaco et al. and Mannocci et al., while Dammaschke et al. included fiber posts, cast metal posts, or no posts. The authors noted that the high survival of resin composites without posts in Dammaschke et al. (1–2 surface restorations) suggests posts may not always be necessary for favorable outcomes.

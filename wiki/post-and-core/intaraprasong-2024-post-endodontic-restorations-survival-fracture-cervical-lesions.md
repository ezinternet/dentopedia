## Three-line Summary

This retrospective cohort study (n=175 ETT, 155 patients) compared fracture survival of endodontically treated anterior teeth with cervical lesions and pulpal involvement restored with resin composite (n=125) versus crowns (n=50) over a mean 32.9-month recall period.

No significant difference in survival against fracture was found between resin composite (85.6%) and crown (88%) groups (p=0.361). Additional tooth structure loss (class III/IV or contralateral class V) was a significant prognostic factor with 7.25-fold higher fracture risk (HR 7.25; 95% CI 1.68-31.30; p=0.008).

For single-surface cervical lesions with pulpal involvement, survival was 96.15% (resin composite) and 100% (crown), significantly higher than teeth with additional tooth loss (78.08% and 80.65%, respectively; p<0.05). Resin composite can be recommended for simple cervical lesions, but caution is needed for teeth with additional defects.

## Summary

This retrospective cohort study from Mahidol University evaluated 175 endodontically treated anterior teeth affected by cervical lesions with pulpal involvement, comparing those restored with resin composite (n=125) versus full-coverage crowns (n=50) over a mean follow-up of 32.9 months. The primary outcome was tooth fracture, with secondary outcomes including fracture pattern and restorability. Overall survival was 86.3%, with no significant difference between the resin composite (85.6%) and crown (88%) groups (p=0.361). The most common failure mode was crown-root fracture, occurring in 78% of resin composite and 83.3% of crown group failures. Multivariable non-proportional hazard analysis identified additional tooth structure loss (class III, IV, or contralateral class V cavities) as a significant prognostic factor, conferring a 7.25-fold increased fracture risk (HR 7.25; 95% CI 1.68-31.30; p=0.008). Subgroup analysis revealed that single-surface cervical lesions had significantly higher survival (96.15% resin composite, 100% crown) compared to teeth with additional defects (78.08% resin composite, 80.65% crown; p<0.05). The authors conclude that for teeth with only a cervical lesion and pulpal involvement, resin composite restoration provides equivalent fracture survival to crown placement, but additional defects dramatically increase fracture risk regardless of restoration type.

## Key Contributions

- First clinical evidence showing resin composite restorations provide equivalent 33-month fracture survival to crowns for anterior ETT with simple cervical lesions and pulpal involvement
- Quantified fracture risk associated with additional tooth structure loss in anterior ETT (HR 7.25)
- Demonstrated that single-surface cervical lesions with pulpal involvement can be successfully managed with direct resin composite restorations without posts
- Showed crown-root fracture predominates in both restoration types, suggesting need for ferrule design
- Provided evidence that full-coverage crowns do not eliminate fracture risk when additional defects are present
- Established clinical recommendation against unnecessary crown placement for simple cervical lesions

## Methodology

This retrospective cohort study included 175 endodontically treated anterior teeth from 155 patients treated at Mahidol University's endodontic clinic between 2009-2022, with inclusion requiring complete root formation, occlusal function, and minimum 12-month recall. Teeth restored with resin composite (n=125) or crowns with prefabricated fiber posts (n=50) were analyzed using dental records and radiographs. Operators included undergraduates, postgraduates, and restorative dentists following standard protocols. Endodontic treatment involved rubber dam isolation, crown-down preparation, calcium hydroxide medication, and obturation. Resin composite restorations featured intraradicular extension (1-2 mm apical to cervical margin), while crown restorations used prefabricated fiber posts with resin cement. Statistical analysis employed Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, Tarone-Ware tests, and multivariable non-proportional hazard models adjusting for tooth location, posterior support, proximal contact, cervical root dentine ratio, and crestal bone level. Fisher's exact tests assessed baseline comparability between groups, which were balanced except for crestal bone level (p=0.025).

Crown-root fracture occurred most frequently in both groups: 77.8% (14/18) in resin composite and 83.3% (5/6) in crown. In the resin composite subgroup analysis by cavity type, survival was 96.15% for single-surface class V, 83.33% for class V + class III/IV, and 68% for class V + contralateral class V (p<0.05 for group A vs. others). The 5-year cumulative survival curves showed the crown group dropped sharply to 66% at 54-60 months, while resin composite remained stable at ~80%.

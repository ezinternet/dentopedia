## Three-line Summary
Critical/narrative review (7 databases searched inception–October 2020 plus hand-searched gray literature; no PRISMA/PROSPERO, no risk-of-bias assessment) synthesizing orthodontic clinical trials on intraoral scanner (IOS) accuracy, reproducibility, duration, patient comfort/preference, and operator experience versus conventional (alginate/PVS) impressions.

Accuracy and duration results across the included studies were contradictory — some found IOS as accurate as alginate, others found conventional impressions significantly more precise, with no consistent time advantage either way; patients generally preferred digital scanning and reported reduced gag reflex (two studies found 100% patient preference for IOS), but operator experience/learning curve was repeatedly identified as a major, insufficiently characterized confound.

Clinical implication: IOS adoption in orthodontics is feasible and generally patient-preferred, but the evidence base is heterogeneous and operator-dependent — the authors themselves call for more high-quality comparative studies rather than drawing a firm accuracy/efficiency conclusion.

## Summary
This is a self-labeled "critical review" (not a formal systematic review) that searched seven databases (PubMed, CENTRAL, Cochrane Reviews, Scopus, Web of Science, Clinical Trials, Proquest) from inception to October 2020, plus hand-searched gray literature, to synthesize orthodontic clinical trials on intraoral scanner (IOS) performance. It covers accuracy, reproducibility, duration, patients' time perception, comfort, operator experience/learning curve, and preference, comparing IOS against conventional alginate/PVS impressions. The dominant finding across nearly every domain is heterogeneity: accuracy and duration results are genuinely contradictory across the primary studies reviewed, with some trials favoring IOS and others favoring conventional impressions depending on device, arch, and measurement type. Patient-reported outcomes are more consistent — patients generally preferred digital scanning and reported reduced gag reflex — but operator experience emerged repeatedly as a confound the underlying literature does not adequately isolate. The paper does not apply risk-of-bias or GRADE-style appraisal, and its Discussion freely mixes author clinical opinion with the literature synthesis, which is characteristic of a narrative rather than systematic review.

## Key Contributions
- Synthesizes orthodontic-specific IOS literature (primary studies 2013–2019) across a broader set of clinical parameters (accuracy, reproducibility, duration, time perception, comfort, operator experience, preference) than prior accuracy-only or preference-only systematic reviews from overlapping authorship.
- Documents genuinely contradictory accuracy findings: e.g., Lythos digital models were reported as accurate as conventional impressions for occlusal/linear measurements, while other trials found conventional materials significantly more precise than digital; maxillary scans were reported less accurate than mandibular scans.
- Reports concrete patient-preference figures from underlying primary studies: two independent questionnaire studies found 100% patient preference for IOS over conventional impressions; an orthodontist survey found only 34% planned to switch to digital study models (29% no, 37% undecided), with iTero the most popular device among digital users (86%).
- Identifies operator experience/learning curve as a recurring, device-dependent factor — e.g., iTero showed a faster learning rate but longer average scan time than TRIOS, while TRIOS had a slower learning rate but shorter, experience-independent mean scan time.
- Lists the specific IOS devices evaluated in the underlying orthodontic literature: TRIOS Classic, TRIOS 3 Mono (3Shape), LAVA C.O.S. (3M ESPE), CEREC Omnicam (Sirona), iOC (Cadent), CS3600 (Carestream), iTero Element (Align), and Lythos (Ormco).

## Methodology
Critical/narrative review, not a systematic review — no PRISMA statement, no PROSPERO registration, no formal risk-of-bias appraisal despite a systematic-style multi-database search. Seven databases searched from inception to October 2020 (no restriction on study design, year, or location), plus gray-literature hand-searching and reference-list checking. Eligible studies evaluated IOS clinical parameters (accuracy, reproducibility, duration, operator experience) and person-reported outcomes (time perception, comfort, preference); animal studies, non-comparative studies, and dry-skull/phantom/reference-model studies were excluded. Most included studies were cross-over or parallel-group clinical trials.

## Results
| Domain | Finding |
|---|---|
| Accuracy | Contradictory across studies — some show IOS as accurate as alginate; others show conventional impressions significantly more precise. Maxillary scans less accurate than mandibular. |
| Reproducibility | Generally excellent; in vivo vs. ex vivo reproducibility comparable; indirect alginate showed statistically higher reproducibility than direct digital, but both clinically equivalent for diagnostic use. |
| Duration | Contradictory overall; alginate chairside time typically shorter than digital in most studies; powder-free next-gen scanners reduce both chairside and processing time. |
| Patient time perception | IOS generally perceived as more comfortable in terms of time, even when objectively longer. |
| Comfort | Digital methods more comfortable than conventional overall; gag reflex reduced/absent with digital in all 3 studies examining it; powder-related discomfort in 14–18% of patients scanned with LAVA/CEREC. |
| Operator experience | Significant, device-dependent factor; learning curve differs by device and by patient intraoral characteristics. |
| Preference | Majority prefer digital (100% in two studies); minority studies found conventional preferred; orthodontist adoption intent mixed (34% yes / 29% no / 37% undecided). |

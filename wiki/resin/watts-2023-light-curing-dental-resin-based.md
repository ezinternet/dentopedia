## Three-line Summary

Watts (2023) provides a foundational narrative review of the photophysics and photochemistry underlying light-curing of dental resin-based composites, including bulk-fill materials. The review explains photon wave-particle duality, Beer-Lambert attenuation, Norrish Type I vs Type II photoinitiator mechanisms, LED-LCU technology (monowave vs polywave), and critically evaluates the reciprocity hypothesis. Clinical implications focus on LCU selection, depth-of-cure verification, and the serious consequences of under-curing.

## Summary

Clinicians frequently use light-curing as a routine step without understanding its underlying science. This review by Watts DC (University of Manchester) bridges the gap between photophysics and clinical practice for dental resin-based composites (RBCs), with emphasis on bulk-fill materials.

**Nature of light:** Light has wave-particle duality. Each photon carries energy E = hν. Blue photons (~470 nm) carry enough energy to excite photoinitiator molecules. The electromagnetic spectrum context situates visible light between UV and IR.

**Radiometric units:** Irradiance (mW/cm²) is the power per unit area incident on the target. Radiant exposure (dose, mJ/cm²) = irradiance × time. The distinction between radiant exitance (at the LCU tip) and irradiance (at the target) is clinically important because beam divergence reduces dose at distance.

**RBC formulation:** Dimethacrylate monomers (bis-GMA, UDMA, TEGDMA) polymerize via C=C → C-C conversion. Inorganic fillers (~0.1–0.2 wt% photoinitiator concentration). Bulk-fill composites achieve DoC ≥ 4 mm through optimized PI mixtures and light transmission.

**LED-LCU technology:** First dental LED-LCU proposed 1995, viable by 1999. Modern LEDs are 30–40% energy-efficient. Monowave devices emit ~470 nm blue only; polywave/multi-chip devices add ~410 nm violet. The 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded for blue LED invention.

**Photon-composite interaction:** Beer-Lambert law governs exponential irradiance decay with depth. Scattering at resin-filler interfaces is the dominant attenuation mechanism for well-formulated composites. Blue light (470 nm) penetrates deeper than violet (410 nm) due to less scattering. Particles >470 nm scatter light; nanoparticles (100 nm) do not.

**Photoinitiator mechanisms:**
- *Norrish Type II (CQ/amine):* Photon excites CQ → excited state reacts with amine co-initiator → free radicals. CQ absorbs ~470 nm; residual CQ/amine causes yellow discoloration ("bleaching" upon curing is desirable).
- *Norrish Type I (TPO, BAPO):* Direct photolytic bond cleavage → free radicals; absorbs ~410 nm violet; no amine needed; no discoloration concerns.

Both types initiate free-radical addition polymerization: initiation → propagation (radical + monomer chain growth) → termination.

**Degree of Conversion (DC):** Typically 60–70% for dimethacrylate composites. 100% unachievable due to vitrification—rapid glass-transition during polymerization entraps residual monomers and limits chain mobility.

**Depth of Cure (DoC):** Bulk fill defined as DoC ≥ 4 mm. Bottom/top Vickers hardness ratio should be >95%. Under-curing at depth creates: monomer elution, matrix breakdown, color instability, reduced bond strength, increased bacterial colonization, secondary caries.

**Reciprocity hypothesis:** Assumption that dose = irradiance × time gives equivalent outcomes regardless of how irradiance and time are traded off. Watts concludes this is over-optimistic for most formulations. Ultra-rapid curing (3–5 s) requires specially designed RBCs with advanced PI systems. Reciprocity may apply only to Type I PI systems or specific viscosity ranges.

**Shrinkage:** C=C → C-C conversion causes densification/shrinkage. Fillers reduce bulk shrinkage. Stress arises in cavity-confined adhesively bonded restorations; incremental layering mitigates this for conventional composites; bulk-fill composites are designed to reduce shrinkage stress.

**Heat generation:** Modern high-power LCUs can generate heat sufficient to damage mucosa or pulp. Polymerization itself is exothermic. Avoid prolonged high-power exposures.

## Methodology

Narrative review; no systematic search methodology. Integrates physics, chemistry, materials science, and clinical guidelines. Written by a single senior expert (Watts DC, University of Manchester). References span foundational science (Planck, Beer-Lambert) through clinical LED-LCU trials and bulk-fill studies.

## Results

- DC = 60–70% typical maximum for dimethacrylate RBCs
- Bulk fill DoC ≥ 4 mm requires optimized PI + high light transmission formulation
- Violet (410 nm) penetrates less than blue (470 nm) in composites — polywave may be suboptimal for deep bulk fill
- Reciprocity fails for many conventional composites; specialty-formulated RBCs required for ≤5 s cure
- Beam non-uniformity (hot/cold spots) in many commercial LCUs degrades curing predictability
- Post-vitrification hardness continues to develop over ~1 month; water sorption softens surface layers

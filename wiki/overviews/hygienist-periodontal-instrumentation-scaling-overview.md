## 한국어 핵심요약

> [!summary] 한국어 핵심요약
> - **논지(thesis)**: 치위생사(Dental Hygienist, DH) 스케일링·치주기구조작(periodontal instrumentation)을 14편으로 **네 전선(front)**에서 종합 — ①효과성·적응증(indication), ②술자의 몸(ergonomics·근골격계질환), ③에어로졸·감염관리, ④체어사이드 안전 2건(항응고·교정브라켓). "스케일링" 한 단어에 서로 다른 임상질문 네 개가 겹쳐 있다.
> - **적응증 경계가 최우선**: 저위험 건강 성인의 **루틴 스케일링·폴리싱(routine scale & polish)**은 치은염·치주낭깊이(Probing Pocket Depth, PPD)·삶의질에 이득이 거의 없음(치석만 소폭↓; Cochrane 고신뢰도, RCT 2편·n=1,711) — 반면 진단된 치주염의 **치료적 스케일링·치근활택술(Scaling and Root Planing, SRP)**은 크고 임상적으로 의미 있는 이득. 이 둘을 뭉뚱그리면 안 된다.
> - **효과성은 실제 진료로 일반화됨**: DH 95명(클리닉 59곳)이 실제 진료에서 낸 치주낭 폐쇄율 **~69–72%**가 효능시험(efficacy) 벤치마크와 일치; 환자교육 선행 1회 시술(Guided Periodontal Infection Control, GPIC)이 다회 내원 SRP와 **비열등(non-inferior)**하면서 총 시술시간 **~17%↓**(134 vs 161분).
> - **비반응 예측인자**: 흡연(승산비 Odds Ratio, OR 0.56)·고령(OR 0.97/년)·구치(molar, OR 0.41)·기저 PPD(OR 0.33/mm)가 폐쇄 확률을 독립적으로 낮춤 → 금연상담·과잉시술 회피의 근거(treatment group 자체는 유의차 없음).
> - **술기 습득은 빠름**: 8.5시간 사전임상 교육만으로 학생이 타이포던트(typodont)에서 숙련 DH 수준(치면세정률 67.4%)에 도달(gartenmann, 초록만 확보).
> - **실제 임상은 병용**: 유럽치주과학연맹(European Federation of Periodontology, EFP) 회원 2,008명 중 **94.4%가 손기구+기계식 병용**(Gracey 큐렛 + 초음파) — hand-vs-ultrasonic 이분법이 아님; 단 기구 마모 점검은 <50%로 품질관리 공백.
> - **술자 부담은 실재하며 과소관리됨**: 압전형 초음파 스케일러 측방력이 권장 상한 **0.5 N을 경질치석에서 시술시간의 54–56% 초과**(숙련자도; 힘은 치석 경도가 결정, 직종 무관·수직성분 우세) — 의원성(iatrogenic) 치근 손상과 술자 근피로를 동시에 시사.
> - **설계로 완화 가능(단 근거는 초기)**: 적응형(adaptive) 손잡이·부착식 실리콘 슬리브가 표면근전도(surface Electromyography, sEMG) 근작업량↓·편안함/피로도↑·촉각 보존 — 그러나 전부 typodont/pilot(n=10–20). **근골격계질환(Musculoskeletal Disorder, MSD) 보유 자체**가 기구와 무관하게 근작업량 ~2배·피로도 ~99% 악화(wink)로 최대 변수.
> - **에어로졸: 기구 유무보다 종류·위치가 결정**: 초음파 물방울 >99%가 12–200 µm·>65%가 <1 m/s; 포집효율 구강외국소흡인(Extraoral Local Extractor, ELE) 96% > 고용량흡인(High-Volume Evacuation, HVE) 88% > 타액흡입기 63%, 단 노즐 14→18 cm에 96→56%로 급락; **능동추종형(dynamic) > 고정형(static)**; 하이스피드 핸드피스가 스케일러보다 입자 3.4배; 구강외흡인의 주 이득은 **보조인력 보호**(오염원은 타액 아닌 수관물 시사).
> - **체어사이드 안전 2건**: (항응고) 비타민K길항제(VKA) 203초·이중항혈소판요법(Dual Antiplatelet Therapy, DAPT) 190초로 출혈시간 최장이나 **거즈 압박만으로 전원 지혈 → 스케일링 전 중단 불필요**(pesce); (교정) 초음파가 베이스 작은 설측브라켓 전단결합강도를 임상 최소치(6–8 MPa) 밑으로 떨어뜨림 → 소형 설측브라켓 주변 초음파 시간·압력 최소화(scribante).
> - **근거 등급 주의**: 최강은 lamont(Cochrane 고신뢰)·tomasi(대규모 effectiveness RCT); **vadvadgi는 본문 서술 수치와 표 수치가 불일치**(표에 "illustrative purposes only" 명시, Methods 미기재)로 점추정치를 신뢰해 인용 불가; 술자측 근거(ergonomics·force·aerosol)는 대부분 typodont·bench·pilot로 외적타당도 공백.

## Three-line Summary

Synthesis of 14 papers on hygienist-delivered scaling and periodontal instrumentation across four fronts — effectiveness/indication, operator ergonomics, aerosol/infection control, and two chairside adjuncts. The central indication boundary: routine scale-and-polish confers little or no periodontal benefit in low-risk healthy adults (Lamont Cochrane, high certainty, n=1711), whereas therapeutic SRP in diagnosed periodontitis produces large, real-world-reproducible benefit (Tomasi, 95 hygienists, ~69–72% pocket closure, non-inferior to multi-visit protocols at ~17% less chair time).

On the operator side, piezoelectric scaling force exceeds the recommended 0.5 N ceiling 54–56% of the time on hard calculus even for experienced clinicians (Stutzer), and hygienist musculoskeletal-disorder burden is high — adaptive handles and add-on silicone sheaths reduce sEMG muscle work and improve comfort while preserving tactile feedback (Lin, Wink, Dang), though all operator-side evidence is typodont/bench/pilot scale.

For aerosols, device type and positioning govern droplet escape more than mere suction presence (dynamic HVE/ELE outperform static/low-volume; ELE efficiency falls 96%→56% over a 4 cm nozzle shift; Ou, Piela, Senpuku); two adjuncts round it out — continue anticoagulants/antiplatelets for scaling (gauze pressure suffices despite 3–4× bleeding time; Pesce), and limit ultrasonic exposure on small-base lingual orthodontic brackets to avoid bond failure (Scribante).

## Why these 14 papers belong together

The topic sweep was "치위생사 스케일링 술기" — dental-hygienist scaling technique. But "scaling" is not one clinical question; it is at least four, and these papers separate cleanly along them. **Front 1 (effectiveness & indication)** asks whether hygienist instrumentation works and *when it is even indicated*. **Front 2 (the instrument–hand interface)** asks what the same stroke costs the operator's body and the patient's root surface, through the shared currency of **applied force**. **Front 3 (aerosols)** asks what the stroke releases into the room. **Front 4** collects two narrow but common chairside-safety questions a hygienist actually faces — a patient on anticoagulants, and a patient in lingual orthodontic appliances. Read together they describe the full envelope around a single scaling appointment: *does it help, does it hurt (operator or tissue), does it contaminate, and what breaks it.*

A running theme across Fronts 2–3 is an **external-validity gradient**: the effectiveness evidence (Front 1) is anchored by a Cochrane review and a 615-patient real-world RCT, whereas most operator- and aerosol-side data come from typodont manikins, bench rigs, and n=10–20 pilots. That gradient should calibrate how hard each finding is pushed into practice.

## Front 1 — Effectiveness & the indication boundary

### The indication boundary comes first

The most consequential distinction in the whole set is **routine prophylaxis vs. therapeutic instrumentation**, and it is easy to blur because both are "scaling done by a hygienist."

- **Routine scale-and-polish in low-risk healthy adults**: the Cochrane review (2 UK general-practice RCTs, n=1711, 2–3 years) found **high-certainty evidence of little or no effect** on gingivitis (6-monthly SMD −0.01; 12-monthly SMD −0.04), probing depth, or oral-health-related quality of life, whether delivered 6-monthly, 12-monthly, or not at all. The only consistent signal was a **small calculus reduction** (6-monthly SMD −0.32; 12-monthly −0.19), of unclear clinical importance. No trial measured adverse effects; none compared dentist vs. hygienist delivery. This is the evidentiary base for **risk-based rather than fixed-interval recall**.
- **Therapeutic SRP in diagnosed periodontitis** is a different population and a different effect size entirely — which the next studies quantify.

The practical error the boundary guards against: citing "scaling has no benefit (Cochrane)" to a periodontitis patient, or conversely defending fixed 6-monthly recalls for a healthy low-risk adult as if they were disease treatment.

### Non-surgical therapy works — and generalizes to real hygienist practice

The strongest real-world evidence is the Swedish multi-centre effectiveness trial: **95 dental hygienists across 59 clinics** treated 615 periodontitis patients under a non-inferiority design, comparing a **guided, education-first single session (GPIC)** — patient education until full-mouth plaque <30%, then one full-mouth ultrasonic debridement — against **conventional multi-visit SRP (CNST)**.

- **Pocket closure (PPD ≤4 mm) at 6 months was ~69–72% overall with no significant between-group difference** (shallow 5–6 mm sites: 72% GPIC vs 75% CNST; deep ≥7 mm sites: 30% vs 33%). This reproduces the ~70% benchmark from prior *efficacy* trials — the headline result is that specialist-calibrated outcomes **hold up in ordinary hygienist hands**.
- **GPIC was markedly more efficient**: total treatment time 134.0±40.1 vs 160.9±61.3 min (p<.001, ~17% less); instrumentation per closed pocket 9.5 vs 14.5 min (p=.001); 100% completed in a single session vs 29% for CNST; more patients needed no local anaesthesia (70.0% vs 59.9%, p<.001). No adverse events in either arm.
- **Independent negative predictors of pocket closure**: baseline PPD (OR 0.33/mm), current smoking (OR 0.56), older age (OR 0.97/year), and molar vs. anterior tooth (OR 0.41). Treatment group and BMI were **not** significant — i.e., *which* protocol mattered less than *smoking, age, tooth type, and starting depth*. This directly motivates smoking-cessation counselling and realistic expectations for deep molar sites.

A second RCT (n=120, moderate-to-severe disease, India + Saudi Arabia) compared **traditional periodontal surgery** (flap surgery/grafts, by surgeons) against **non-surgical SRP delivered by trained hygienists**. Per its tables and PubMed-indexed abstract, surgery achieved a numerically larger pocket-depth reduction (6.8→3.7 mm, Δ−3.1 vs non-surgical 6.7→4.0 mm, Δ−2.7) and attachment gain (Δ−2.1 vs Δ−1.6 mm), while the hygienist-delivered non-surgical arm was **better tolerated** (pain 2.1 vs 3.6; discomfort 2.9 vs 4.2 on a 1–10 scale) with similar satisfaction (8.4 vs 7.8). This is consistent with the standard hierarchy (non-surgical first-line, surgery for residual pockets) plus a modest surgical margin on hard endpoints and a comfort advantage for non-surgical care.

> [!warning] Data-quality caveat on the surgery-vs-non-surgical RCT
> The surgery-vs-non-surgical numbers above must be cited **loosely, not as precise point estimates**. The paper's PMC full text is internally inconsistent: its own narrative Results paragraphs report a *different* set of PPD/CAL/patient-outcome values than its Tables 1–3, the tables are captioned "arbitrary and for illustrative purposes only," the Methods section leaves template placeholders unfilled, and **no between-group p-values or confidence intervals are reported** despite the stated statistical plan. Use it for the *direction* of the trade-off (surgery a bit more reduction, non-surgical better tolerated) — not for the exact millimetres.

### Skill is acquired fast; real practice combines modalities

Two supporting papers frame the *operator competence* and *what actually gets used*:

- **Skill acquisition**: a shortened **8.5-hour pre-clinical course** brought three cohorts of undergraduate students from layperson-level hand-scaling performance (29.8–42% of tooth surface cleaned, indistinguishable from laypeople at 27.9%) to **61.7–79.5% — statistically indistinguishable from experienced hygienists (67.4%)**, P<.001 vs. baseline. Basic hand-scaling competence is reachable quickly, at least on a typodont; the ~70% ceiling even for experts is a notable reminder that "complete" calculus removal is aspirational.
- **Real-world modality mix**: a survey of **2008 EFP members** found **94.4% combine hand + mechanical instruments** rather than using either alone — Gracey curettes the most popular hand instrument, ultrasonic scalers the preferred mechanical device, with 42.4% alternating standard and micro/slim ultrasonic inserts. The "hand vs. ultrasonic" framing is largely a false dichotomy in practice. The survey also surfaces a **quality-control gap**: only 46.1% systematically check hand-instrument wear and 41.3% check insert wear (more experienced clinicians check more often) — worn instruments/inserts silently degrade the debridement the effectiveness trials assume.

## Front 2 — The instrument–hand interface: force, ergonomics, and MSD

Force is the hinge between two problems that look separate but share a cause: **iatrogenic root-surface damage** (too much force on the tooth) and **operator musculoskeletal strain** (too much force from the hand). The papers in this front measure both ends of the same stroke.

### Applied force routinely exceeds the recommended ceiling

An in-vitro study measured force during piezoelectric ultrasonic debridement (EMS PIEZON EN-060) by **10 hygienists and 6 dentists** on soft (~4.6 HV) and hard (~24 HV) artificial calculus using a 3-axis force-sensor rig:

- Mean total force **nearly doubled on hard vs. soft calculus** — hygienists 0.63 N vs 0.34 N (1.85×, p=0.04); dentists 0.57 N vs 0.28 N (2.04×, p=0.06).
- **Force did not differ between professions** (soft p=0.26; hard p=1.00) — *calculus hardness, not who holds the instrument, drives force*.
- Force was **predominantly perpendicular** to the surface (2.7–13.7× the parallel component), reframing the usual "lateral force ≤0.5 N" guidance.
- Critically, trained operators **exceeded the 0.5 N recommended limit 14–23% of the time on soft calculus and 54–56% on hard calculus** — force compliance is *not* guaranteed by training and experience alone, which is the argument for instrumented force-feedback aids in both clinic and education.

(Note the in-vitro rig likely *underestimates* true in-vivo forces, and one institution withdrew consent, shrinking the sample — so the exceedance rates are conservative.)

### The ergonomic cost is high, and design can lower it

Dental hygiene carries among the highest occupational burdens of musculoskeletal disorders (MSD) and carpal tunnel syndrome of any profession. Three studies from the same UC Irvine group test whether **handle/sheath design** reduces the physiological cost, using surface EMG (four hand/forearm muscles: APB, FDI, FPL, EDC), grip dynamometry, and VAS comfort/fatigue on typodont models:

- **Adaptive curette handle, bench study (12 hygienists)**: a silicone-covered, bendable (≤30°) Barnhart 5/6 handle vs. a conventional rigid one won on *every* axis — significantly lower total and per-muscle sEMG, **preserved pinch/grasp strength** (vs. a significant post-scaling drop with the rigid handle), better comfort/fatigue VAS, less joint hypermobility, and correct 70° blade angulation achieved **~2× as often**. Experience, handedness, and sequence had no effect.
- **Adaptive handle, pilot in-vivo (n=10, 5 with MSD / 5 without)**: the adaptive handle again improved comfort/fatigue in *both* groups and cut muscle work in non-MSD testers, **without sacrificing tactile feedback** (arguing against a tactile trade-off). The stratification exposed the dominant variable: **MSD status itself roughly doubled muscle work and worsened comfort ~68% and fatigue ~99% regardless of which curette was used**. Design helps, but an already-injured operator carries a large fixed penalty.
- **Add-on silicone sheath (20 hygienists + 10 dentists)**: a ~1 mm removable sheath rolled onto a **piezoelectric ultrasonic scaler** and a **dental micromotor** significantly reduced sEMG muscle work (hygienists p=0.0079 no-MSD / p=0.0028 MSD; dentists anterior p=0.0154 / posterior p=0.001) and improved comfort/fatigue on nearly every measure. MSD-affected hygienists' comfort/fatigue moved substantially toward healthy levels (finger comfort/fatigue remained significantly worse even with the sheath). This extends the ergonomic benefit **from hand instruments to power-driven devices**, and as a cheap retrofit.

**How much to trust this front**: all three are typodont/manikin (not live patients), unblinded to instrument identity (appearance precludes blinding), and pilot-scale; the sheath study also had an unavoidable age gap between healthy (36.1 y) and MSD (49.3 y) hygienists. The mechanism (larger contact area spreading load; vibration damping; thermal/cushioning; healthier grip) is plausible but not isolated — several handle variables change at once. The direction is consistent and the objective/subjective measures concord, but confirmation needs larger, longer, patient-based trials (the authors note some are underway).

## Front 3 — Aerosols & infection control during scaling

Ultrasonic scaling is an aerosol-generating procedure (AGP). Three studies converge on one operational message: **device type and positioning matter more than the mere presence of suction**, and the highest-value mitigations are point-of-source.

- **Droplet characterization + capture efficiency (manikin, optical DIH+LSI)**: ultrasonic-scaler droplets are **>99% within 12–200 µm and >65% travel <1 m/s, omnidirectionally** — slow and local, which favours point-of-source suction over whole-room ventilation. Capture efficiency ranked **extraoral local extractor (ELE) high-flow at 14 cm = 96% (best single device) > HVE 88% > saliva ejector 63%**, with ELE low-flow at 74%. Combining ELE with SE or HVE added nothing (94–95%). Positioning was decisive: **ELE fell from 96% to 56% when the nozzle moved just 14→18 cm** (and 74%→38% at low flow). A hands-free ELE can outperform HVE without a chairside assistant — but only if positioned correctly (and it raised chairside noise 81.1→86.8 dB).
- **Suction-device taxonomy (manikin, real-time particle sensors)**: across ultrasonic scaling *and* high-speed handpiece use, **every** suction device beat no-suction (p<0.001), but only **dynamic (instrument-following) high-volume devices — standard HVE, Purevac® — brought counts to near/below background**, while **static/hands-free devices (DryShield®, ReLeaf™) and standard low-volume suction left periodic particle "spikes"** at the labial/incisal regions. Also useful: without suction, the **high-speed handpiece generated 3.4× more particles/min than the ultrasonic scaler** — the scaler is not the worst offender in a restorative-plus-hygiene practice.
- **Biological endpoint + who is protected (field study, 3 volunteers)**: measuring ATP (RLU) and CFU rather than particle counts, both oral suction and oral+extraoral suction cut contamination vs. none, but **extraoral suction's distinct added benefit appeared specifically at the position behind-and-left of the chair head — where a right-handed operator's chairside assistant stands**. Oral-bacteria (BHI) plates showed no group differences while waterline-favoring (R2A) plates did, implying the simulated-scaling droplets were **dominated by ultrasonic-scaler waterline water, not saliva**. So extraoral suction's main role is **protecting the assistant**, and waterline quality (see the infection-control overview) partly governs what the aerosol even contains.

**Boundary**: all three are simulation/field studies on manikins or non-scaling volunteers; none demonstrates reduced *infection* (only reduced particles/contamination), and inter-operator and patient factors (saliva, breathing, coughing) are largely untested.

## Front 4 — Two chairside safety adjuncts

Two single-paper angles don't fit the fronts above but are exactly the kind of question a hygienist meets in a routine chair.

### Anticoagulated/antiplatelet patients — do not interrupt therapy for scaling

A cross-sectional study (n=93) adapted the **Ivy bleeding-time test to the mouth**, timing the first bleeding-on-probing site during a routine hygiene session across six drug groups. Mean intraoral bleeding time:

| Group | Mean (s) | vs. control |
|---|---|---|
| Control (drug-free) | 50 | — |
| Factor Xa inhibitor (apixaban/rivaroxaban) | 98 | ns |
| Single antiplatelet (SAPT) | 105 | ns |
| Direct thrombin inhibitor (dabigatran) | 120 | p=0.004 |
| Dual antiplatelet (DAPT) | 190 | p<0.001 |
| Vitamin-K antagonist (warfarin/acenocoumarol) | 203 | p<0.001 |

The clinically decisive result: **VKA and DAPT bleeding times were 3–4× control, yet no group ever needed anything beyond gauze pressure** — a statistically significant prolongation did *not* become a procedure-disrupting event. This supports **uninterrupted anticoagulant/antiplatelet therapy for routine scaling**. Time-since-last-dose (<5 h vs ≥5 h) made no significant within-group difference; the authors *speculate* (explicitly non-validated) that scheduling ~2–3 h before the next NOAC dose could avoid peak anticoagulation without interrupting medication. Limits: single-centre, unpowered (no prior data for a power calculation), and periodontal parameters (BOP%, PD, plaque index) were not recorded.

### Lingual orthodontic brackets — small bases are vulnerable to ultrasonic debonding

An in-vitro study (200 bovine incisors) tested whether ultrasonic instrumentation (Mini Piezon G1 tip, 8 W, 15 s/side) weakens orthodontic bracket bonds. **Ultrasonic instrumentation significantly reduced shear bond strength in the three small-base lingual brackets (TTR, Idea, 2D; p<0.05) — some values falling below the clinically accepted 6–8 MPa minimum — while the large-base lingual bracket (STB) and the vestibular control (Victory) were unaffected.** Bracket base/mesh area correlated linearly with the magnitude of the drop, and ultrasonic instrumentation also raised the adhesive remnant index (more adhesive left on the bracket). The takeaway is procedural, not prohibitive: **minimize ultrasonic time/pressure directly on the bases of small-mesh lingual brackets**, rather than avoiding ultrasonic scaling in orthodontic patients. Limits: bovine (not human) enamel and a single tip/power protocol.

## Cross-cutting tensions & how confidently to cite

1. **"Scaling" is four questions, not one.** The single biggest interpretive risk is conflating routine prophylaxis (Lamont: little benefit in low-risk healthy adults) with therapeutic SRP (Tomasi/Vadvadgi: large benefit in disease). Match the evidence to the indication.
2. **Force is the shared root of two harms.** Stutzer shows force is set by calculus hardness and frequently overshoots the safe ceiling; that same overshoot is both an iatrogenic-root-damage driver and an operator-strain driver, which is why the ergonomics papers (handle/sheath) and the tissue-safety literature are two views of one variable.
3. **Evidence strength is uneven.** High confidence: Lamont (Cochrane, high certainty) and Tomasi (615-patient effectiveness RCT). Low/qualified confidence: Vadvadgi (internal numeric inconsistency — cite direction only), Gartenmann and Laleman (abstract-only ingests), and the entire operator-/aerosol-side set (typodont, bench, n=10–20 pilots; manikins can't confirm infection or long-term MSD outcomes).
4. **Design mitigations are promising but unproven at the outcome level.** Adaptive handles/sheaths reliably lower *measured strain* on a typodont; none yet shows reduced *MSD incidence* in practice. Sell them as low-risk ergonomic hedges, not proven prevention.

## Evidence Gaps / Open Questions

- **No head-to-head on delivery role.** Lamont explicitly found *no* trials comparing dentist vs. hygienist/therapist delivery; the effectiveness case for hygienist-delivered care is inferential (Tomasi shows it works *in* hygienist hands, not that it equals dentist delivery in a controlled comparison).
- **Operator-side outcomes are all surrogates.** sEMG muscle work, grip force, and VAS on a typodont are *proxies*; no study shows an adaptive handle or sheath reduces actual MSD/CTS incidence, sick leave, or career longevity. The MSD-status penalty (Wink) suggests prevention must start before injury — untested.
- **Force-feedback is recommended but not trialled.** Stutzer argues for instrumented force feedback in training and practice; whether such aids actually lower exceedance rates or iatrogenic root loss is unstudied here.
- **Aerosol studies stop at particle/contamination counts.** None links mitigation to infection outcomes; patient-derived variables (real saliva, breathing, coughing, calculus load) and inter-operator variability are largely uncontrolled, and manikin waterline-dominated droplets may not represent real patient aerosols.
- **Vadvadgi cannot settle surgery vs. non-surgical magnitudes.** Its internal inconsistency means the surgery-vs-hygienist-SRP effect sizes remain, in this holding, unresolved beyond direction.
- **NOAC scheduling is a hypothesis, not a protocol.** Pesce's 2–3 h-before-next-dose window is an underpowered secondary, author-flagged as speculation.
- **Bracket data are bovine + single-protocol.** Scribante's bond-loss thresholds await human-enamel and multi-tip/power confirmation before precise clinical limits can be set.
- **Instrument-wear quality gap is documented but not linked to outcomes.** Laleman shows <50% check wear; no paper here quantifies how much worn instruments/inserts degrade real debridement effectiveness.

## Clinical Takeaways

1. **Decide the indication before the technique.** Routine scale-and-polish is not disease treatment — for low-risk healthy adults, move to risk-based recall (Lamont); reserve the "scaling clearly helps" claim for diagnosed periodontitis, where it strongly does.
2. **Trust hygienist-delivered non-surgical therapy as a first-line, efficient option.** ~70% pocket closure is achievable in ordinary practice, and a structured education-first single session can match multi-visit SRP with ~17% less chair time and less anaesthesia (Tomasi). Expect worse response at deep molar sites and in smokers, and counsel accordingly.
3. **Combine modalities and maintain the instruments.** Real practice is hand + ultrasonic together (Gracey + piezo/insert); systematically check instrument and insert wear — a majority currently don't (Laleman).
4. **Respect force — for the tooth and for your hand.** Even experts exceed the 0.5 N ceiling most of the time on hard calculus (Stutzer); build force awareness, and treat adaptive handles / add-on silicone sheaths as low-risk ergonomic hedges that lower measured strain without losing tactile feedback (Lin, Wink, Dang). If you already have an MSD, expect a large fixed penalty and prioritise load reduction.
5. **For aerosols, choose and *position* the device.** A dynamic, instrument-following high-volume evacuator (or a correctly positioned high-flow extraoral extractor within ~14 cm) beats static/low-volume options; position matters as much as presence, and extraoral suction's main payoff is protecting the chairside assistant (Ou, Piela, Senpuku). Remember the high-speed handpiece out-aerosolizes the scaler.
6. **Keep anticoagulants/antiplatelets going for scaling** — even VKA/DAPT bleeding (3–4× control) is controlled by gauze pressure alone; interruption is not warranted for routine hygiene (Pesce).
7. **Ease off the ultrasonic on small lingual brackets** — reduce time/pressure on small-mesh-base lingual orthodontic brackets to avoid bond strength dropping below the clinical minimum (Scribante).

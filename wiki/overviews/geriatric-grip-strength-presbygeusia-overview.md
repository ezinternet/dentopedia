## Three-line Summary

Two-axis synthesis (3 cohort/review papers): Axis 1 — grip strength as a systemic mortality biomarker, converging across PURE (n=139,691, 17 countries; Leong 2015) and UK Biobank (n=502,293; Celis-Morales 2018), both reporting HR 1.16 per 5 kg grip reduction for all-cause mortality; Axis 2 — presbygeusia as a real but clinically modest entity (12/15 studies positive; Ponzo 2024 narrative review), affecting sour/bitter thresholds while sweet is preserved, via five peripheral/central mechanisms.

Grip strength outperforms systolic blood pressure as a mortality predictor in both cohorts; muscle weakness thresholds (≤26 kg men; ≤16 kg women) confer elevated cardiovascular, respiratory, and cancer mortality; sour and bitter taste thresholds rise measurably from age 60–70 but the dietary impact is modest (genetics ~20% of food-preference variance) compared with social/cultural factors.

For geriatric dental practice: a one-minute Jamar dynamometer reading captures physical frailty candidates who may need medical referral or nutritional support; taste complaints in older denture/prosthetic patients should be parsed as presbygeusia baseline versus drug-induced dysgeusia, and denture use itself mechanically reduces taste stimulation independent of aging.

## Summary

This synthesis integrates three papers — two large prospective cohorts on grip strength mortality and one systematic-style narrative review on presbygeusia — to build a geriatric dental assessment framework around two independent but complementary biomarkers of aging.

**Grip strength as systemic mortality predictor.** The PURE study (Leong 2015, n=139,691, 17 countries, median 4.0 year follow-up) and the UK Biobank cohort (Celis-Morales 2018, n=502,293, aged 40–69, mean 7.1 year follow-up) converge on a single finding: every 5 kg reduction in grip strength raises all-cause mortality risk by HR 1.16. Both studies found grip strength to be a stronger predictor of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality than systolic blood pressure — the standard office biomarker for risk stratification. The UK Biobank study extends these findings to respiratory (HR 1.24–1.31) and cancer (HR 1.10–1.17) mortality, and demonstrates that adding grip strength to a standard office risk score improves the C-index by ΔC=+0.013 for all-cause mortality.

**Presbygeusia as a real but modest entity.** Ponzo (2024) reviewed 15 observational studies of healthy elderly (≥65 years vs. younger controls), appraised with ROBINS-I (overall moderate risk of bias). Twelve of the 15 studies support presbygeusia's existence as a distinct phenomenon in healthy aging: sour and bitter taste thresholds rise measurably beginning around age 60–70, while sweet perception is relatively preserved. Five mechanistic pathways are identified (see Axis 2 below). Despite these measurable changes, the impact on actual food choices is modest — social, cultural, and psychological factors explain far more variance in elderly dietary behavior than taste threshold shifts, and genetics account for only ~20% of food-preference variation.

Together, these findings suggest a two-part geriatric dental screening protocol: a grip strength measurement to detect physical frailty candidates for medical referral, and a structured taste-complaint history to distinguish normal presbygeusia from pathological (drug-induced, radiation-induced, or disease-related) dysgeusia.

## Axis 1 — Grip Strength as a Systemic Mortality Biomarker

### Summary of the two cohort studies

| Feature | Leong 2015 (PURE) | Celis-Morales 2018 (UK Biobank) |
|---|---|---|
| n | 139,691 | 502,293 |
| Countries | 17 (low/middle/high income) | UK (England/Wales/Scotland) |
| Age at baseline | Adults (broad range) | 40–69 years |
| Follow-up | Median 4.0 years | Mean 7.1 years |
| Deaths | 3,379 | 13,322 |
| All-cause mortality HR per 5 kg ↓ | 1.16 (1.13–1.20) | 1.16–1.20 |
| CVD mortality HR per 5 kg ↓ | 1.17 (1.11–1.24) | 1.19–1.24 |
| Respiratory mortality HR per 5 kg ↓ | Not reported | 1.24–1.31 |
| Cancer mortality HR per 5 kg ↓ | Not a primary finding | 1.10–1.17 |
| vs. systolic BP | Grip stronger predictor | Grip stronger predictor |
| Weakness thresholds | Not specified | ≤26 kg (men); ≤16 kg (women) |
| Prediction improvement | — | ΔC +0.013 (all-cause), +0.012 (CVD) |

### Key finding: grip strength vs. systolic blood pressure

Both cohorts independently confirm that grip strength is a **stronger predictor** of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality than systolic blood pressure. This is a counterintuitive finding, because systolic BP is routinely measured in every medical and many dental settings, while grip strength is not. The implication is that grip strength is measuring something systemic blood pressure does not — aggregate skeletal muscle mass and neuromuscular integrity, which together proxy the body's biological age more accurately than a single hemodynamic measure.

### Muscle weakness thresholds (Celis-Morales 2018, FNIH criteria)

- Men: ≤26 kg → elevated hazard for CVD, respiratory, and cancer mortality
- Women: ≤16 kg → same pattern

The grip×age interaction in the UK Biobank data shows that hazard ratios are larger in younger participants (≤55 years), implying grip strength captures biological aging rather than pure chronological decline — a finding that extends the relevance of frailty screening into mid-life patients, not just octogenarians.

### Measurement in dental settings

Grip strength is measured with a Jamar dynamometer in under one minute. No special training is required beyond instruction to squeeze maximally. The test adds minimal chair time, requires no consumables, and produces a quantitative value that can be flagged against the FNIH thresholds above. A below-threshold reading warrants notation in the chart, brief verbal flag to the patient, and — depending on the clinical context — a recommendation to discuss frailty risk with their primary care physician.

## Axis 2 — Presbygeusia: Age-related Taste Decline

### Evidence summary

Ponzo (2024) reviewed 15 observational studies (837 records screened; ROBINS-I appraisal; all restricted to healthy elderly without confounders). The 12/15 positive studies span decades (Schiffman 1979 through Sato 2022), reflecting a consistent replicated signal:

| Taste quality | Age-related change |
|---|---|
| Sour | Threshold rises with age; electrogustometry elevation from ~age 60 |
| Bitter | Threshold rises; reduced salivary mucin impairs bitter-receptor activation |
| Sweet | Relatively preserved across most studies |
| Salt | Controversial; 3 studies show no decline (Drewnowski 1996, Wang 2020, Wayler 1990) |
| Umami (amino acid) | Thresholds ~2.5× higher in elderly (75–87y) vs. young (17–27y) |

### Five mechanistic pathways

1. **Peripheral structural**: Reduced fungiform papillae (버섯유두, Fungiform Papillae) density and vascularization with age; fewer taste buds per papilla; taste bud stem-cell and homeostasis decline.
2. **Salivary**: Decreased salivary flow and altered mucin glycosylation — mucin normally concentrates bitter compounds at receptor surfaces; age-related mucin changes impair this delivery, blunting bitter perception.
3. **Genetic/ion channel**: ENaC (SCNN1B) and TRPV1 gene polymorphisms affect suprathreshold salt and sour perception independent of structural aging.
4. **Central**: Altered brain activation patterns during taste processing (EEG/fMRI evidence); the CNS component is distinct from and additive to peripheral decline.
5. **Mechanical and olfactory**: Denture use reduces direct mucosal taste stimulation; retro-nasal olfactory epithelium decline diminishes flavor complexity (olfaction contributes up to 80% of perceived flavor); reduced mastication vigor lowers mechanical taste stimulation.

### Clinical relevance of presbygeusia

Despite measurable threshold changes, actual dietary impact is **modest**. Socio-cultural factors — income, education, cooking knowledge, living situation, social eating frequency — explain far more variance in elderly food preference and caloric intake than sensory threshold shifts. Genetics account for only approximately 20% of food-preference variation in the elderly.

Clinical implication: a complaint of "food tastes different since I got my dentures" may reflect both **mechanical presbygeusia amplification** (denture covering palatal taste buds; reduced occlusal force reducing chewing stimulation) and **baseline aging changes** that were subclinical before the prosthesis was placed. Neither component alone requires pharmacological treatment. However, in a patient already at nutritional risk (low body weight, polypharmacy, social isolation), taste blunting may compound intake inadequacy — warranting nutritional screening and possible dietitian referral.

## Dental Clinical Relevance

### Grip strength screening protocol

1. **Who to screen**: Patients ≥65 years, or any patient with visible sarcopenic features (low muscle mass, slow gait, difficulty rising from chair) regardless of chronological age.
2. **How**: Jamar dynamometer; dominant hand; best of three trials; compare against thresholds (men ≤26 kg; women ≤16 kg per FNIH criteria).
3. **Below threshold**: Document in chart. Discuss with patient — frame as a "fitness check" finding, not a diagnosis. Recommend discussion with primary care physician. Consider co-screening for oral frailty indicators (tooth count, xerostomia, chewing/swallowing difficulty; see [[overviews/oral-frailty-geriatric-synthesis]]).
4. **Integration with oral frailty**: Low grip strength + low tooth count + xerostomia = high-probability frailty cluster requiring multidisciplinary management.

### Presbygeusia and prosthetic patient counseling

1. **Pre-prosthetic consent**: Inform patients that denture placement may amplify pre-existing (often subclinical) taste changes by mechanical coverage of palatal taste buds and reduced chewing stimulation. This is not a defect of the prosthesis; it is a predictable physiological consequence.
2. **Complaint triage after prosthesis delivery**: Sour/bitter blunting → likely presbygeusia ± mechanical; sweet absent or food "tastes like nothing" → consider drug-induced dysgeusia (check medication list, especially ACE inhibitors, metronidazole, metformin, diuretics, antibiotics) or zinc deficiency.
3. **Not every taste complaint needs treatment**: Some sour/bitter blunting is normal aging. Over-investigation increases patient anxiety without benefit. The clinical threshold for referral is: metallic/phantom taste, complete ageusia, or taste loss onset acutely corresponding to a new medication or systemic event.
4. **Nutritional risk flag**: Taste blunting + recent weight loss + polypharmacy = nutritional risk requiring dietitian input, independent of the dental prosthetic workup.

## Three-line Summary

Two complementary studies examined handheld dental X-ray units from clinical and objective-physics angles: Geibel 2025 (n=127, Nomad Pro 2, German nursing homes, prospective quality assessment) and Ruiz 2025 (n=48, Eagle X-ray vs Focus fixed, 2 CMOS sensors, in-vitro ImageJ gray-value analysis).

Handheld units achieved 80% defect-free images, 86% periapical diagnostic adequacy, and revealed clinically important unexpected findings in 61% of cases; simultaneously they produce systematically higher brightness and lower contrast than fixed units across both sensor platforms (p<0.0001).

Handheld units are diagnostically adequate for mobile/domiciliary care and close a diagnostic gap for immobile patients, but their characteristic image signature (brighter, lower-contrast) means sensor-specific window/level interpretation protocols are warranted.

## 세줄요약

휴대형 장치는 80% 결함 없음, 86% 치근단 진단 가능, 61%에서 예상 외 임상적 중요 부수소견 확인; 동시에 고정식 대비 밝기 높고 대비 낮은 체계적 차이 (p<0.0001) 가 센서 무관하게 존재.

휴대형 장치는 이동 불가 환자의 방문진료에 진단적으로 적합하나, 특유의 영상 특성(고밝기·저대비)은 센서별 window/level 해석 프로토콜의 도입을 정당화함.

## Summary

Handheld portable intraoral X-ray devices occupy a critical niche in dentistry: they enable radiographic diagnosis for patients who cannot attend a conventional practice (nursing-home residents, homebound patients, hospital inpatients). Until recently, evidence supporting their clinical use rested primarily on dosimetric and operator-safety data — systematic evaluation of their diagnostic image quality in real domiciliary settings, and objective characterization of their image physics relative to fixed units, was sparse.

Two 2025 studies address this gap from complementary directions. Geibel et al. ask the clinical question: *Are radiographs taken with a handheld unit in a nursing-home setting actually diagnostically adequate?* Ruiz et al. ask the physics question: *How does the image produced by a handheld unit differ, objectively, from one produced by a fixed unit on the same sensor?*

Together they provide the most complete current picture of handheld-unit diagnostic quality: clinical performance is adequate — matching or exceeding published benchmarks for stationary units — while the image produced is measurably and systematically different in brightness and contrast. These two facts are not in tension: the difference exists but does not prevent diagnosis; however, it does mean that interpretation habits calibrated on fixed-unit images may require adjustment when reading handheld images.

## Axis 1 — Clinical Quality in Nursing-Home Domiciliary Care (Geibel 2025)

### Study Design

Prospective quality-assessment study (not a randomized trial; no head-to-head comparator). Device: Nomad Pro 2 (Dexis/KaVo, 60 kV/2.5 mA, CE-marked). Setting: German nursing homes, routine domiciliary dental care for patients unable to attend a conventional practice. Sample: n=127 single-tooth radiographs, all taken by one experienced dentist. Grading: 3 independent raters of varying specialization (oral & maxillofacial surgeon, oral surgeon, second-year trainee) using the German Federal Dental Association quality-criteria catalogue. No external funding; no conflicts of interest.

### Key Results

| Quality criterion | n/127 | % |
|---|---|---|
| No qualitative defects | 101 | **80%** |
| Periapical bone diagnostic | 109 | **86%** |
| Field of view adequate for indication | 107 | **84%** |
| Unexpected important secondary findings | 78 | **61%** |
| Motion artefacts | 5 | 4% |
| Overexposure | 11 | 9% |

The 61% secondary-finding rate deserves emphasis: it represents diagnoses that would not have been possible without portable radiography for this population (root remnants, apical osteolysis, periapical pathology). In the German legal context, surgical procedures performed without radiographic justification are legally contestable — so this figure also quantifies the medico-legal value of the technology.

### Comparison to Published Benchmarks

Geibel 2025 compared its outcomes to a 2012 study of wall-mounted intraoral units and other stationary/inpatient benchmarks. On no metric did the handheld unit perform significantly worse:

- Diagnostically relevant defect-free rate: **83%** (handheld) vs. **83%** (stationary)
- German quality criteria met: **>96%** (handheld) vs. **83%** (non-mobile benchmark)
- Field-of-view adequacy: **84%** (handheld) vs. **86%** (non-mobile)

The shorter exposure time of the Nomad Pro 2 (0.12–0.16 s vs. 0.25–0.32 s for stationary units) may actually reduce motion artefacts in limited-compliance patients — the 4% motion-artefact rate supports this.

### Limitations

- Single operator, single device model — rater consistency tested but operator generalizability limited.
- Descriptive comparison to published benchmarks rather than a within-patient randomized head-to-head.
- PSP (Phosphor Storage Plate) handling issues (misalignment in 43%, quality issues in 47%) are likely attributable to the domiciliary care environment rather than the device itself — important for protocol design.

## Axis 2 — Image Physics vs Fixed Units (Ruiz 2025)

### Study Design

In-vitro experimental comparative study. No human/animal subjects. Equipment: Eagle X-ray handheld (60 kVp/2.5 mA/0.45 s, platform-mounted to eliminate operator hand-movement as a variable) vs Focus fixed unit (60 kVp/7 mA/0.16 s); near-identical mAs achieved by protocol. Sensors: size-2 CMOS Digora Toto and size-1 CMOS Snapshot, tested separately. Phantoms: acrylic block (brightness/noise/uniformity) + aluminum step-wedge (contrast). Analysis: ImageJ gray-value ROI measurements, Student's t-test (α=5%, 95% power). Total n=48 radiographs.

### Key Results

| Parameter | Digora Toto | Snapshot | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brightness | Handheld higher (p<0.0001) | Handheld higher (p<0.0001) | Consistent |
| Contrast | Handheld lower (p<0.0001) | Handheld lower (p<0.0001) | Consistent |
| Noise | Handheld **higher** (p<0.0001) | Handheld **lower** (p=0.001) | Sensor-dependent |
| Uniformity | NS (p=0.661) | NS (p=0.445) | No difference |

The brightness and contrast findings are the cleanest signal: across both sensor platforms and both phantom types, handheld equipment produces brighter, lower-contrast images. The noise pattern is more complex — the direction inverts depending on which sensor is used, implicating sensor-internal signal-processing (scintillator material, detector electronics, software gain curves) as an interacting variable.

### Why Does This Happen?

The authors attribute the brightness/contrast difference to generator design and tube output characteristics intrinsic to handheld battery-powered devices vs. mains-powered fixed units, even when mAs is matched. Battery-powered generators may have less stable tube voltage, different filtration, or different focal-spot characteristics that shift the gray-value distribution without necessarily degrading spatial or diagnostic resolution.

### Clinical Implications of the Physics Finding

The gray-value differences are statistically significant but the authors argue they are likely clinically imperceptible, consistent with prior literature showing handheld equipment does not compromise caries-lesion diagnostic accuracy. However:

1. **Window/level habits**: Clinicians accustomed to fixed-unit images may systematically under-adjust brightness when reading handheld images, reducing apparent contrast further.
2. **Sensor choice matters**: The noise direction reverses between sensors — Digora Toto increases noise with handheld use, Snapshot decreases it. A "handheld-appropriate" sensor choice based on noise characteristics alone would favor Snapshot, but this must be weighed against contrast and brightness preferences.
3. **Understudied pathologies**: Caries detection is well-validated; root resorption, periodontal bone loss, and fracture detection with handheld units are not adequately studied. Lower contrast could theoretically reduce sensitivity for subtle bone-level changes.

## Decision Table — When to Use a Handheld X-ray Unit

| Clinical scenario | Recommendation | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Nursing-home / domiciliary care patient unable to attend practice | **Use handheld** | 80% defect-free; 61% additional diagnostic yield; legal justification of surgery possible; only available option |
| Hospital inpatient or immobilized patient | **Use handheld** | Same clinical rationale; operator-safety protocols required (Berkhout 2015) |
| Routine in-office periapical or bitewing | **Prefer fixed unit** | No access barrier; fixed unit provides higher contrast that may benefit subtle pathology detection |
| Screening for caries in mobile populations | **Handheld acceptable** | Prior literature: caries diagnostic accuracy uncompromised by brightness/contrast shift |
| Suspected root resorption, fine bone loss, fracture | **Prefer fixed or CBCT** | Ruiz 2025: lower contrast with handheld; these diagnoses may be more contrast-sensitive; evidence gap |
| Post-surgical verification in domiciliary patient | **Handheld with PSP protocol** | Adequate; PSP alignment issues (43%) highlight need for explicit handling protocol in non-clinic environments |

## Gap Analysis and Future Research

The two 2025 papers together close the gap on *whether* handheld units work in domiciliary care (Geibel: yes) and *how* their image differs physically (Ruiz: brighter, lower-contrast). The gaps that remain:

1. **Diagnostic accuracy for non-caries pathologies** — root resorption, early bone loss, vertical fractures — with handheld units vs fixed. Ruiz identifies this explicitly; Geibel does not address it.
2. **Multi-operator generalizability** — Geibel used a single experienced operator. Real-world domiciliary care involves varying skill levels; the PSP misalignment rate (43%) suggests protocol-adherence is the key variable.
3. **Sensor selection guidance** — Ruiz shows sensor choice interacts with device type on noise characteristics; no current evidence ranks sensors specifically for handheld use across a broader pathology panel.
4. **CBCT equivalent in portable format** — the existing dose-optimization literature ([[overviews/dental-imaging-radiation-dose-optimization-overview]]) is built around fixed CBCT/panoramic/intraoral units; no portable CBCT equivalent currently fills the domiciliary 3D-imaging gap.

> - 세 전신질환(소아 만성콩팥병, 당뇨·골다공증, 전신경화증)은 각자 고유한 기전으로 구강 건강과 임플란트 예후를 손상시키며, 공통 관리 원칙(염증 조절, 금기약물 회피, 전신과 협진)과 질환별 특이 대처가 동시에 필요하다.
> - 소아 만성콩팥병(Chronic Kidney Disease, CKD): 타액 요소 상승·pH 완충으로 우식은 역설적으로 적지만(caries-free 40% vs 대조군 8.5%), 법랑질 형성저하(31–83%)·치은출혈(95.8%)·치주낭(70.3%)·약물성 치은비대가 두드러지며, 이식 후 우식 방어력이 사라진다.
> - CKD 치과 관리 핵심: 투석 다음날 시술(헤파린 소실, 혈액량 최적), 불소 보충 금기(신 배설 저하), 이식 후 6개월 선택적 치료 보류, NSAIDs·아미노글리코시드·독시사이클린 회피.
> - 당뇨(Diabetes Mellitus, DM)는 AGE-RAGE·산화스트레스·M1 지속 염증으로 골치유 *대사 경로*를 손상시켜 골은 형성되나 임플란트에 통합되지 못하게 하고, 골다공증(Osteoporosis, OP)은 *구조*를 악화시켜(BV/TV·BIC 감소, 흡수 우세 리모델링) 초기 고정력을 떨어뜨린다.
> - 두 질환 모두 잘 조절하면 임플란트 생존율 >90% 달성 가능; 나노토포그래피(<25 nm) 티타늄 표면처리가 대식세포 M1→M2 전환·Wnt/BMP/Nrf2 경로를 통해 생체활성 코팅 없이 손상된 골치유를 회복시키는 유망 전략.
> - 전신경화증(Systemic Sclerosis, SSc): 소구증(microstomia) 52–80%로 접근성 제한이 최대 난관이며, 구강건조증 70%, 심한 치주염(dcSSc 90% III/IV기), 하악과두 흡수(10–50%), 삼차신경통이 뒤따른다.
> - SSc 임플란트는 혈관병증·골질 사전평가 시 생존율 영향 없이 성공 가능; 구강건조증에 필로카르핀·타액 대용제, 소구증에 입 벌리기 운동·단축 기구 필수, 치은비대 시 사이클로스포린→타클로리무스 전환 우선.
> - 공통 임상 시사: 전신질환 자체보다 *질환 조절 상태*가 임플란트·치주 예후를 결정; 생물지표(GCF MMP-9, HbA1c, 타액 요소)가 위험 계층화에 활용되며, 전신과 협진·다학제 접근이 모든 축에서 선행 조건이다.

## Three-line Summary

Narrative synthesis of three systemic diseases — pediatric CKD, diabetes + osteoporosis (implant context), and systemic sclerosis — each creating distinct but partially overlapping oral/dental burdens covered by three companion reviews (Elhusseiny 2024, Guadarrama Bello 2026, Sharma 2024).

Pediatric CKD paradoxically protects against caries (salivary urea buffering) while causing heavy enamel defects (31–83%) and gingival disease (bleeding 95.8%); diabetes impairs peri-implant healing metabolically (AGE-RAGE, M1 inflammation) while osteoporosis degrades bone structure (BIC/BV/TV reduction), yet both allow >90% implant survival when controlled; systemic sclerosis produces microstomia (52–80%), xerostomia (70%), and severe periodontitis (90% stage III/IV in dcSSc) with no disease-modifying cure.

Across all three: disease-control status (not diagnosis alone) is the key predictor of oral outcomes, NSAIDs must be avoided in CKD patients, titanium nano-surface modification offers a coating-free strategy to rescue healing in metabolically compromised hosts, and pre-implant vasculopathy/bone-quality assessment is mandatory in SSc.

## Summary

Three common systemic diseases each impose distinct — and clinically significant — oral and peri-implant burdens that are inadequately covered in general dental practice. This overview synthesizes the evidence from three companion narrative reviews into a three-axis framework for clinical decision-making:

- **Axis 1 — Pediatric CKD**: an oral-medicine axis where salivary biochemistry creates a paradoxical caries advantage but massive developmental and periodontal disadvantages.
- **Axis 2 — Diabetes + Osteoporosis → Implants**: an osseointegration axis where two mechanistically distinct metabolic impairments both converge on peri-implant bone failure, but surface engineering can partially rescue the deficit.
- **Axis 3 — Systemic Sclerosis (SSc)**: a fibrosing-autoimmune axis where physical access restriction (microstomia), salivary failure (xerostomia), and progressive periodontitis demand symptom-specific, low-tech interventions because no disease-modifying treatment exists.

A common thread: **disease-control status, not diagnosis, drives oral prognosis**. The poorly-controlled diabetic, the actively-flaring SSc patient, and the recently transplanted CKD child all represent different risk tiers than their well-managed counterparts — and the dental plan must be staged accordingly.

## Axis 1 — Pediatric CKD: Paradox, Defects, and Staged Dental Protocol

### The Caries Paradox

Children with CKD consume high-carbohydrate, protein-sparing diets — a cariogenic dietary pattern by any conventional risk model. Yet CKD children are *less* caries-prone than healthy controls: caries-free rate 40% vs 8.5%, and mean DMFT 0.5–0.85. The mechanism is salivary:

- Elevated salivary urea (11.6 vs 3.6 mmol/L) is hydrolyzed to ammonia + CO₂ → raises oral pH above enamel demineralization threshold
- Increased salivary buffering capacity (pH 6.4 vs 5.6)
- Mild antibacterial effect of urea
- Reduced S. mutans and lactobacilli counts

**This protection is not durable.** Post-successful-transplant, renal function normalizes, salivary urea drops, the biofilm shifts cariogenic, and previously caries-free children become at-risk. Caries surveillance must intensify after transplant.

### High-Burden Manifestations Despite Low Caries

| Manifestation | Prevalence / Finding | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Enamel defects / hypoplasia | 31% (Koch) to 83% (Nunn); 57–58% in aggregated data | Hypocalcemia, low active vitamin D, high phosphate/PTH/fluoride during primary mineralization |
| Primary canine hypoplasia | 19% vs 3% controls | Timing overlap: enamel mineralization during CKD onset |
| Gingival bleeding | 95.8% | Uremic platelet dysfunction + poor hygiene from xerostomia |
| Periodontal pockets | 70.3% | Compound: immune dysregulation, calculus load, gingival fragility |
| Gingival index | 1.88 vs 0.92 controls | Correlated with anemia (p=0.0002) and dialysis duration (p=0.007) |
| Heavy calculus | Very common | Salivary urea/pH/phosphorus precipitates calcium phosphate/oxalate |
| Drug-induced gingival overgrowth | 16.7% | Cyclosporine A (dose-dependent in first 6 post-transplant months); also nifedipine |
| Delayed tooth eruption | Reported | Growth retardation from CKD, steroid therapy |
| Xerostomia | Common | Fluid restriction, polyuria, drug side effects |
| Radiographic changes | Loss of lamina dura, ground-glass bone, hypercementosis | Renal osteodystrophy |
| Oral infections post-transplant | HSV 0–11.3%; candidiasis 4–43% | Immunosuppression |

### Staged Dental Management Protocol

**Pretreatment (all CKD patients)**
- Thorough medical history + nephrology consult
- Coagulation assessment before bleeding procedures (uremic platelet dysfunction)
- Modify drug doses for reduced renal clearance
- Antibiotic prophylaxis before invasive procedures; corticosteroid supplementation if on steroids
- Preferred local anesthetic: amide type (lidocaine)
- Avoid: aminoglycosides, tetracyclines, doxycycline (nephrotoxic)
- Analgesics: paracetamol preferred; **avoid aspirin** (antiplatelet) and **NSAIDs** (prostaglandin suppression → hypertension in renal failure); opioids generally safe (liver-metabolized)

**Hemodialysis-specific**
- **Treat the day after dialysis** (heparin effect lasts 3–5 h; intravascular volume optimal)
- Layered hemostasis: topical thrombin + sutures + oxidized regenerated cellulose + microfibrillar collagen; desmopressin; conjugated estrogens; tranexamic acid rinse 10–15 mg/kg/day
- HIV/HBV/HCV screening + LFTs before surgery
- Avoid tight chair postures (vascular access at risk)
- Recall interval: 3–6 months

**Restorative considerations**
- Bonded composite or full-coverage for enamel hypoplasia (not all defective teeth need filling)
- **Fluoride supplementation contraindicated** (fluoride is renally retained in CKD → fluorosis risk)
- Standard topical fluoride (varnish, gel): use cautiously; avoid fluoride supplements

**Post-renal transplant**
- Eliminate all oral infection foci *before* transplant
- **Defer elective care for the first 6 months post-transplant** (immunosuppression peak, healing impaired)
- Drug-induced gingival overgrowth: cyclosporine A → tacrolimus substitution preferred over surgical excision alone; rigorous hygiene instruction
- mTOR inhibitor (sirolimus/everolimus) stomatitis → topical corticosteroids
- Oral HSV → acyclovir; candidiasis → chlorhexidine / nystatin / fluconazole
- Heightened oral-cancer surveillance (Kaposi sarcoma, SCC): immunosuppression-driven

## Axis 2 — Diabetes and Osteoporosis: Implant Bone Healing and Surface Rescue

### Two Mechanistically Distinct Impairments

Both diabetes and osteoporosis compromise peri-implant bone healing, but via non-overlapping primary pathways:

| Feature | Diabetes | Osteoporosis |
|---|---|---|
| Primary target | Bone *repair process* (metabolic) | Bone *quantity and quality* (structural) |
| Key driver | AGE-RAGE axis, oxidative stress, sustained M1 inflammation | Resorption-dominant remodeling (high osteoclast / insufficient osteoblast) |
| Cytokine signature | TNF-α ↑, IL-1β ↑, VEGF ↓ | RANKL ↑, OPG ↓, estrogen deficiency (in post-menopausal OP) |
| Bone formation | Bone forms but fails to integrate with implant surface | Delayed new bone formation; trabecular deterioration |
| Preclinical readout | STZ rats / diabetic pigs: reduced BIC, reduced BMD at 4–12 wks; bone volume ↑ but direct BIC ↓ | OVX rats: decreased BIC, BV/TV, removal-torque / push-out strength |
| Clinical outcome | Higher peri-implantitis, marginal bone loss (MBL) in uncontrolled disease | Lower primary stability; slightly higher MBL; survival often >90% with protocols |
| Control threshold | Well-controlled → approaches healthy success rates | Good patient selection → survival >90% |

### Quantifying the Diabetes-Implant Risk (SR/MA Evidence)

The qualitative "higher peri-implantitis, higher MBL in uncontrolled disease" claim above is backed by pooled numbers from three companion SR/MAs on diabetes and dental implants. Al-Ansari et al. (2022), the largest meta-analysis in this space (89 publications, 68,290 implants total — 5,510 in diabetic vs 62,780 in non-diabetic patients), found diabetic patients carry **77.7% higher implant failure odds** (OR 1.777, p<0.001); Type 1 DM carried a further **4.5-fold greater risk than Type 2** (OR 4.477, p=0.032), and MBL ran **+0.776 mm greater** in diabetics (p=0.027), widening by an additional +0.032 mm per month of follow-up — but critically, the failure-risk difference reached statistical significance **only in the maxilla, not the mandible**, meaning posterior mandibular implants appear comparatively low-risk even in diabetic patients while maxillary sites need stricter HbA1c monitoring and longer surveillance. Enteghad et al. (2024) situate these numbers within a bidirectional, apparently causal DM–periodontal/peri-implant disease relationship driven by the AGE-RAGE axis, oxidative stress, and shared cytokine signaling, and conclude that **glycemic control level — not the diabetes diagnosis itself** — is the primary clinical gating factor for both periodontal treatment response and peri-implant maintenance, consistent with this axis's "control status, not diagnosis" thesis. Shahi et al. (2026), the most current SR and direct successor to Al-Ansari et al. (2022), is positioned as the evolving HbA1c-stratified evidence anchor for diabetic implant candidacy, though its pooled quantitative estimates remain pending final publication (article-in-press at time of ingest).

### Shared Inflammatory Convergence

Despite distinct upstream pathways, both conditions converge on a shared downstream inflammatory state at the bone-implant interface: elevated TNF-α, IL-1β, and RANKL/OPG imbalance. This shared pathway is why surface modifications that reduce inflammation benefit both patient groups.

### Surface Modification as a Rescue Strategy

The review by Guadarrama Bello et al. (2026) maps physico-chemical surface modifications to the specific molecular pathways they rescue:

| Surface strategy | Mechanism | Pathway rescued |
|---|---|---|
| Nanoporous titanium (<25 nm nanotopography) | Modulates macrophage polarization M1→M2; alternative adhesion in impaired cells | Inflammation resolution; intrinsic mechanotransductive signaling |
| SLA (sand-blasted, large-grit, acid-etched) | Increases surface area, protein adsorption | Osteoblast adhesion/differentiation |
| Nanotubes (TiO₂) | Sustained drug delivery; osteoblast elongation | BMP/Smad pathway activation |
| Bioactive coatings (HA, BMP-2, strontium) | Biomimetic mineral scaffold | Wnt/β-catenin, VEGF-driven angiogenesis |
| UV photo-functionalization | Increases hydrophilicity, fibronectin adsorption | PI3K/Akt cell survival; Nrf2 oxidative-stress defense |

Key finding: **nanoscale topography alone (<25 nm) can reduce inflammatory response and provide alternative adhesion in genetically/metabolically impaired cells**, without requiring bioactive coatings. This is a coating-free rescue strategy — important for clinical scalability.

### Clinical Decision Framework for Compromised Hosts

- **Poorly controlled diabetes (HbA1c >8–9%)**: elevated risk for peri-implantitis and early failure; optimize glycemic control before elective implant placement; staged protocols; shorter healing intervals avoided.
- **Well-controlled diabetes (HbA1c <7–8%)**: implant survival approaches healthy controls; proceed with standard protocols; note potential for delayed healing.
- **Osteoporosis on bisphosphonates**: consider MRONJ risk separately (see `drug/mronj/` category); primary stability may be lower — use wider/longer implants, bone condensing protocols.
- **General osteoporosis (no bisphosphonates)**: survival >90% with patient selection; primary stability monitoring essential; consider bone densification osteotomy (osseodensification) to improve BV/TV at osteotomy site.
- **Surface preference for compromised hosts**: prefer nanostructured implant surfaces over machined/smooth surfaces; surface choice becomes a modifiable risk factor when systemic disease cannot be fully corrected.

## Axis 3 — Systemic Sclerosis: Fibrosis, Access, and Symptom Management

### Disease Background

Systemic sclerosis (SSc; scleroderma) is an autoimmune fibrosing connective tissue disease driven by vascular injury → hypoxia (HIF-1α) → fibrogenic cascade (TGF-β, ET-1, IL-1α) → fibroblast → myofibroblast conversion → collagen-rich ECM remodeling. Two subtypes with distinct orofacial profiles:

- **lcSSc** (limited cutaneous, 64%): slower progression; more likely to develop secondary Sjögren's syndrome (sicca overlap); CREST features
- **dcSSc** (diffuse cutaneous, 28%, higher mortality): more severe microstomia, more severe periodontitis; higher perioral fibrosis

Because of the characteristic oral presentation, dentists are often the first clinicians to recognize SSc.

### The Microstomia Problem

Microstomia (소구증, perioral skin/muscle fibrosis → reduced mouth opening) affects **52–80% of SSc patients** and is 2.5× more handicapping in dcSSc than lcSSc on the MHISS (Mouth Handicap in Systemic Sclerosis) scale. It is **not the skin-thickness score but microstomia** that best correlates with oral complications — argued to be the primary clinical trial endpoint.

Functional impact:
- Impaired mastication and deglutition → malnutrition risk
- Severely restricted oral hygiene access
- Physical barriers to dental examination and instrument access
- Makes standard impression trays, bite-block sizing, and suction device placement problematic

### Full Orofacial Manifestation Map

| Domain | Manifestation | Prevalence / Severity |
|---|---|---|
| Perioral | Microstomia, facial skin rigidity (mask-like), telangiectasia | 52–80%; 2.5× worse in dcSSc |
| Salivary / xerostomia | Gland fibrosis → sicca syndrome; secondary Sjögren's | 70% sicca; ≤14% true secondary Sjögren's (lcSSc association) |
| Periodontitis | Worse than controls: attachment loss, recession, plaque/gingival indices, GCF inflammatory markers | Stage III/IV: 90% dcSSc vs 60% lcSSc |
| PDL widening | Radiographic widening in >1/3 patients | Collagen excess ± increased occlusal force |
| TMJ / bone | Restricted movement, condylar resorption, mandibular angle/coronoid/zygomatic resorption | Condylar resorption 10–50%; secondary apertognathia reported |
| Neuropathy | Trigeminal neuralgia (may precede diagnosis), dysgeusia (bitter/sour/metallic) | More prevalent than general population |
| Biomarkers | GCF MMP-9, CXCL-4 correlate with early-onset (<2 y) disease and periodontal parameters | IL-6 does NOT correlate |
| Drug side effects | Candidiasis (corticosteroids), mouth ulcers (methotrexate), gingival hyperplasia (calcium-channel blockers) | Drug-specific |

**Key observation**: gingival tissue does NOT develop the fibrotic phenotype of skin, attributed to scar-less gingival healing and fibroblast resistance to myofibroblast differentiation. SSc periodontitis is multifactorial (xerostomia, hygiene restriction from microstomia, microbiome dysbiosis, microangiopathy, immune dysregulation) rather than fibrosis-driven at the gingival level.

### Management Strategies

**Microstomia**
- Daily mouth-stretching exercises (passive/active) → improves interincisal distance; evidence-base is small but consistent
- Severe cases: commissurotomy (risk of scarring/recurrence); foldable silicone oral prostheses
- Clinical adaptation: use short/narrow instruments, smaller impression trays, mouth props; schedule shorter appointments to reduce fatigue

**Xerostomia**
- Sialagogues: pilocarpine (preferred), anetholtrithione
- Salivary substitutes: carboxymethylcellulose-based sprays/gels
- Raise caries vigilance (xerostomia overrides any innate buffering advantage)
- Fluoride varnish recommended (unlike CKD, no renal fluoride-retention concern in SSc)

**Periodontitis**
- Aggressive periodontal maintenance: 3-monthly recall intervals
- Address compounding factors: manage xerostomia, assist oral hygiene technique
- GCF MMP-9 / CXCL-4 as early-disease biomarkers; no validated treatment target yet

**Drug-induced gingival overgrowth** (calcium-channel blockers, cyclosporine)
- Preferred intervention: switch cyclosporine A → tacrolimus (systemic modification, before surgical excision)
- Calcium-channel blocker-induced: discuss substitution with prescribing physician

**Dental implants in SSc**
- Survival reported unaffected by SSc diagnosis; can improve OHRQoL
- Prerequisites: thorough preoperative vasculopathy assessment (compromised healing from microangiopathy), bone-quality evaluation (mandibular resorption risk), detailed follow-up protocol
- Contraindication: severe vasculopathy (relative)
- No special implant surface recommendation from current evidence; use standard protocols with heightened monitoring

**No disease-modifying therapy** exists for SSc. All oral management is symptom-specific.

## Cross-Axis Comparison

| Feature | Pediatric CKD | Diabetes + Osteoporosis | Systemic Sclerosis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary oral mechanism | Salivary biochemistry ± developmental defects | Impaired bone healing (metabolic / structural) | Fibrosis → physical restriction + multifactorial disease |
| Caries risk | Low (paradoxical; reverses post-transplant) | Increased (dry mouth, glycemic load) | Increased (xerostomia-driven) |
| Implant risk | Not primary focus; bleeding/healing concerns | Metabolic impairment of osseointegration | Vasculopathy; access restriction |
| Key contraindication | NSAIDs; fluoride supplements; first 6-month post-transplant elective care | Uncontrolled HbA1c as relative contraindication | Severe vasculopathy |
| Biomarker | Salivary urea, HbA1c (post-transplant) | HbA1c, RANKL/OPG | GCF MMP-9, CXCL-4 |
| Disease-control impact | Post-transplant: caries risk emerges | Controlled DM/OP: survival >90% | No disease-modifying Rx; management symptom-specific |
| Primary intervention | Staged dental protocol; nephrology coordination | Glycemic/BMD optimization + surface-modified implants | Microstomia exercises + symptom-specific dental adaptation |

## Three-line Summary

The history of fixed dental prosthetics traces a 100-year arc from cast gold dominance through metal-ceramic (PFM) hegemony, the all-ceramic transition, and into the current era of monolithic zirconia, driven by materials science advances, CAD/CAM digitalization, and clinical trial evidence accumulation.

Cast gold partial crowns set the longevity benchmark first (Krug 2024: 91.8% survival at 23.5yr, annual failure 0.7%); PFM crowns then expanded the standard for 40 years (Behr 2014: 10-year survival 95.9% posterior); the all-ceramic challenge proved successful with leucite/LD achieving equivalent outcomes (Sailer 2015 SR, 67 studies) and monolithic zirconia ultimately equaling PFM without veneering risk (Pjetursson 2026 SR+MA).

Today's material hierarchy — monolithic zirconia or lithium disilicate for most crowns, Ti-base CAD/CAM abutments for implant restorations — represents the evidence-based synthesis of seven decades of prosthetic materials development.

## 세줄요약

치과 고정성 보철 100년 역사: 전금속관(골드크라운) 선구기 → 금속도재관(PFM) 황금기 → 전세라믹(LD·알루미나) 도전기 → 모놀리식 지르코니아 완성기로 이어지는 재료과학·디지털·임상증거의 융합.

골드 부분크라운은 23.5년 생존율 91.8%(Krug 2024)로 최장기 기계적 내구성 증명; PFM은 10년 생존율 95.9%(Behr 2014)로 황금기준 확립; 모놀리식 지르코니아는 Pjetursson 2026 SR+MA에서 PFM과 동등 성능 확인 — 베니어링 없이 PFM 대체.

현재 단단관 1차 선택 = 모놀리식 3Y/4Y-TZP(구치) + LD(전치); CAD/CAM·Ti-base 지대주로 보철 워크플로우 디지털화 완성.

## Era 0: The Cast Metal Era (Early 1900s — present)

### Historical Foundation

Before the introduction of ceramic veneering, dental restorations relied entirely on cast metal alloys. Full cast metal crowns (전금속관, Cast Metal Crowns) and cast gold partial crowns (주조금 부분크라운, Cast Gold Partial Crowns, CGPC) represented the pinnacle of restorative dentistry for most of the 20th century.

Gold alloys were the material of choice due to their unique combination of properties: excellent castability, controlled hardness comparable to natural enamel (minimizing antagonist wear), tarnish resistance, biocompatibility, and predictable marginal adaptation. The cast gold partial crown — covering only the compromised surfaces while preserving healthy tooth structure — anticipated modern minimally invasive principles by decades.

### Long-term Clinical Evidence

The most comprehensive long-term dataset for cast gold partial crowns comes from [[inlay/krug-2024-ceramic-inlay-cast-gold-partial-crown-longterm]], a retrospective cohort from the University of Würzburg (N=325 patients, mean follow-up 13.9 years, up to 24.8 years):

| Material | Survival at ~15yr | Survival at 23.5yr | Annual Failure Rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ceramic in-/onlay (CIO, Empress I) | 93.9% | 91.7% | 0.5%/yr |
| Cast gold partial crown (CGPC) | 92.6% | 91.8% | 0.7%/yr |

**Key finding**: Both materials showed remarkably similar long-term survival despite being fundamentally different in composition and fabrication. CGPC's primary failure mode was endodontic complications (8.8%), not mechanical failure — reflecting that the metal itself is essentially indestructible under normal occlusal loads; failures are biological, not material.

### Current Status and Clinical Niche

Cast gold restorations remain clinically relevant despite declining use, with specific indications where their biomechanical properties are unmatched:
- **Bruxism/parafunction**: gold's ductility dissipates impact energy without fracture risk
- **Minimal preparation**: CGPC requires less tooth reduction than full crowns, preserving tooth structure
- **Antagonist protection**: gold wears comparably to natural enamel, protecting opposed dentition
- **High occlusal force zones**: second molar regions where ceramic fracture risk is highest

The decline of gold restorations since the 1980s reflects cost (gold prices), esthetic demands, and the rise of equivalent-performance ceramic alternatives — not evidence of inferior longevity. Among dental materials, cast gold's clinical record over 24+ years remains unmatched for mechanical durability.

## Era 1: The Metal-Ceramic Epoch (1950s–2000s)

### Origins and Establishment

Porcelain-fused-to-metal (PFM) technology was introduced in the 1950s, fusing dental porcelain to a cast metal framework to combine the structural reliability of metal with the esthetics of ceramic. For four decades, PFM became the unchallenged standard for single crowns and fixed dental prostheses (FDPs).

The fundamental engineering advantage of PFM lay in the metal framework's tensile strength absorbing occlusal loads while the veneering porcelain provided esthetics. This design resolved the brittleness problem of earlier all-porcelain crowns (jacket crowns), which failed unpredictably under posterior occlusal stress.

### Long-term Clinical Evidence

The most comprehensive single-center PFM dataset comes from [[prosthetic-materials/behr-2014-pfm-precious-alloy-crowns-clinical-performance]], a retrospective cohort of 997 precious alloy PFM crowns followed over 25 years (1984–2009):

| Outcome | 5-year | 10-year |
|---|---|---|
| Anterior survival | 96.4% | 92.3% |
| Posterior survival | 97.5% | 95.9% |
| Free of chipping | 98.9% (ant.) / 98.2% (post.) | — |
| Free of periodontitis | 85.8% | 72.2% |

Key pattern: technical complications (chipping, loss of retention) concentrated in early years; periodontitis risk accumulated continuously — a distinction that shapes modern recall protocols. The 10-year posterior survival of 95.9% became the benchmark that all subsequent materials are measured against.

### Limitations Driving Change

Despite excellent survival, PFM carried inherent limitations that motivated the all-ceramic movement:
- **Metallic substructure opacity**: gingival gray shadow with gingival recession; difficult to achieve natural translucency at crown margins
- **Allergenic potential**: base-metal alloys (nickel, chromium) in non-precious variants raised biocompatibility concerns
- **Radiographic opacity**: obscures periapical pathology assessment
- **Laboratory complexity**: veneering porcelain application required highly skilled technicians

## Era 2: The All-Ceramic Transition (1990s–2015)

### First-Generation All-Ceramics

The 1990s and early 2000s saw the clinical introduction of several all-ceramic systems targeting PFM's esthetic limitations:

1. **Feldspathic/silica-based porcelain crowns** — highest translucency, closest to natural enamel; insufficient strength for posterior use
2. **Leucite-reinforced ceramics (e.g., IPS Empress)** — improved fracture resistance; suitable for anterior single crowns
3. **Lithium disilicate (LD) glass-ceramics (e.g., IPS e.max)** — introduced ~1998; flexural strength ~400 MPa; suitable for posterior single crowns and anterior 3-unit FDPs
4. **Glass-infiltrated alumina (In-Ceram Alumina)** — high-strength core ceramic for anterior FDPs
5. **Densely sintered alumina (Procera AllCeram)** — CAD/CAM milled high-purity alumina cores

### The Pivotal Systematic Review: Sailer 2015

[[prosthetic-materials/sailer-2015-allceramic-metalceramic-fdps-survival-complication-sr]] (67 studies, 4,663 metal-ceramic + 9,434 all-ceramic SCs, PubMed/Embase/CENTRAL 2006–2013) provided the first large-scale head-to-head comparison:

| Material | 5-year Survival | Verdict vs PFM |
|---|---|---|
| Metal-ceramic | 94.7% | Reference |
| LD glass-ceramic | 96.6% | Equivalent ✓ |
| Glass-infiltrated alumina | 94.6% | Equivalent ✓ |
| Densely sintered alumina | 96.0% | Equivalent ✓ |
| Densely sintered zirconia | 92.1% | Equivalent survival, BUT: significantly more framework fractures + loss of retention (p<0.001) |
| Feldspathic/silica-based | Significantly lower | Not recommended posterior ✗ |

**Historic significance**: This review established that LD glass-ceramics and oxide ceramics had matched PFM's clinical performance — legitimizing the all-ceramic paradigm. It also identified that early zirconia (veneered design) carried a chipping burden that limited its clinical application.

## Era 3: The Zirconia Revolution (2010–present)

### Why Zirconia?

Zirconia (ZrO₂) offered a unique combination unmatched by earlier ceramics:
- **Phase transformation toughening**: martensitic tetragonal→monoclinic transformation at crack tips absorbs fracture energy (transformation toughening mechanism)
- **Opaque white color**: radiographically identifiable; esthetically neutral substructure for veneering
- **Biocompatibility**: mirror-polished zirconia achieves least bacterial adhesion among dental materials (Ban 2021); superior soft tissue response vs metals
- **CAD/CAM machinability**: pre-sintered ("green-state") zirconia blocks can be milled efficiently, enabling digital workflows

### Classification Framework: Ban 2021

[[prosthetic-materials/ban-2021-classification-properties-dental-zirconia-implant]] established the definitive 12-type classification of dental zirconia across four axes:

| Axis | Options |
|---|---|
| Yttria content | 3Y-TZP / 4Y-TZP / 5Y-TZP |
| Color | Monochromatic / Polychromatic |
| Composition | Uniform / Hybrid |
| Architecture | Monolayer / Multilayer |

The yttria content axis is clinically most critical:

| Yttria | Flexural Strength | Translucency | LTD Resistance | Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3Y-TZP | ~1,200 MPa | Low | Good | Posterior crowns, frameworks, implant abutments |
| 4Y-TZP | ~800 MPa | Medium | Moderate | Anterior/posterior crowns, esthetic cases |
| 5Y-TZP | ~500 MPa | High | Lower | Low-load anterior cases only |
| ATZ | Highest (~2,000 MPa) | Opaque | Very good | Implant fixtures |

### The Veneering Problem and Monolithic Solution

First-generation zirconia restorations used a veneered design (zirconia framework + hand-layered veneering porcelain) to achieve esthetics. This design suffered the same vulnerability as PFM: veneering ceramic chipping (delamination). The rate was clinically unacceptable in early zirconia generations.

The engineering solution was **monolithic design**: milling the entire crown from a single translucent zirconia block, eliminating the ceramic-ceramic interface entirely. This required developing higher-yttria zirconia with sufficient translucency for esthetic cases.

### Translucent Zirconia: Kui 2023

[[prosthetic-materials/kui-2023-translucent-zirconia-fixed-prosthodontics-overview]] synthesized the clinical properties of modern translucent zirconia types:

- **Optical**: 4Y/5Y-TZP achieves translucency comparable to lithium disilicate; structural color (opalescence, fluorescence) added through dopants
- **Adhesion**: surface treatment (air abrasion 50 µm alumina) + modern self-adhesive resin cements achieve adequate bond strength for all types
- **Implant superstructures**: 3Y/4Y-TZP preferred for implant-supported crowns and FDPs; monolithic design eliminating chipping risk

### Speed Sintering and Clinical Workflow: Aboushelib 2026

[[prosthetic-materials/aboushelib-2026-translucent-zirconia-monolithic-mechanical-performance]] resolved a key clinical question — can speed sintering (enabling same-day restorations) be safely applied?

- **3Y-PSZ**: speed sintering has no significant effect on flexural strength (p=0.10) → **safe for same-day workflows**
- **4Y-PSZ**: speed sintering significantly reduces strength (p<0.001) → **conventional sintering required**
- **Grinding (chairside occlusal adjustment)**: significantly reduces BOTH types → must be followed by polishing
- **Glass bead treatment**: significantly improves 3Y-PSZ strength → preferred surface preparation for adhesive protocols

### The Updated Evidence: Pjetursson 2026 + Romandini 2026

[[prosthetic-materials/pjetursson-2026-metal-ceramic-all-ceramic-crowns-survival-sr-ma]] (Part 1, single crowns) and [[prosthetic-materials/romandini-2026-allceramic-metalceramic-fdps-multiunit-survival-sr-ma]] (Part 2, multi-unit FDPs) together cover the entire fixed prosthetic landscape from 2014–2024.

**Single crowns (Pjetursson 2026):** Monolithic zirconia performs equivalently to metal-ceramic when veneering is avoided — the definitive current verdict closing the clinical case begun in 2015.

**Multi-unit FDPs (Romandini 2026, 41 studies, n=2,132):**

| Material | 5-Year Survival | vs MC |
|---|---|---|
| Veneered dense-sintered Zr | 92.9% | NS |
| Metal-ceramic (reference) | 91.3% | — |
| Glass-infiltrated alumina | 88.4% | NS |
| Monolithic dense-sintered Zr | 87.9% | NS |
| LD glass-ceramic | 82.5% | p<0.05 (sig. lower) |

Key contrast with single crowns: LD FDPs have significantly lower survival than MC in multi-unit scenario (p<0.05), while LD single crowns are equivalent. Framework fractures >10% at 5yr for LD + glass-infiltrated alumina. Monolithic Zr: least chipping, MC-equivalent survival — **preferred all-ceramic option for multi-unit FDPs**.

### First Clinical Evidence for 5Y-PSZ Multi-unit FDPs

[[prosthetic-materials/guth-2024-high-translucent-zirconia-fdp-3year-prospective]] (Güth 2024, prospective pilot, N=22, posterior 3-unit 5Y-PSZ, 3yr) fills a critical gap — no prior clinical data existed for high-translucent cubic zirconia in multi-unit FDPs:

- 100% survival, **zero mechanical complications** (zero chipping)
- All FDI criteria acceptable or better
- Material: Lava Esthetic (800 MPa, 1,200 HV, 216 GPa)
- Protocol: air abrasion 50 μm + RelyX Universal cement

This is the first evidence that 5Y-PSZ, previously considered only for anterior single crowns due to lower strength, can survive posterior multi-unit loading without mechanical failure at 3 years.

## Era 4: CAD/CAM and Digital Integration (2015–present)

The adoption of CAD/CAM technology transformed prosthetic workflows independently of material advances, but accelerated zirconia's dominance:

### Key Workflow Changes

1. **Digital impressions** replacing conventional silicone — dimensional accuracy improved; patient comfort enhanced
2. **CAD design** replacing wax-up — standardized anatomical libraries; parametric design
3. **5-axis milling** of monolithic restorations — same-day crowns possible (3Y-PSZ only); reduced laboratory steps
4. **Ti-base abutments** becoming standard for implant restorations — [[prosthetic-materials/strasding-2024-cadcam-implant-supported-fdps-material-abutment]]: Ti-base provides superior fracture resistance vs. monolithic zirconia abutments; enables ceramic crown customization atop a standardized titanium interface. For customized ceramic abutments without Ti-base, [[prosthetic-materials/humm-2023-zirconia-titanium-abutments-13year-rct]] (RCT 13yr, n=40, posterior) confirms 100% technical survival of customized zirconia abutments, equivalent periimplant outcomes vs Ti abutments — the longest RCT evidence for ceramic abutment longevity

### Material Selection Algorithm (2024)

Based on the cumulative evidence:

| Indication | First Choice | Alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Posterior single crown (tooth) | Monolithic 3Y/4Y-TZP | LD glass-ceramic |
| Anterior single crown (tooth) | LD glass-ceramic | Monolithic 4Y/5Y-TZP |
| Implant-supported posterior crown | Monolithic 3Y-TZP + Ti-base | LD + Ti-base |
| Implant-supported anterior crown | LD + Ti-base | Monolithic 4Y-TZP + Ti-base |
| Multi-unit implant FDP | Zirconia framework | Titanium-ceramic |
| Implant fixture | Titanium (standard) | ATZ zirconia (selected cases) |

---

## Evidence Gaps and Future Directions

1. **Long-term 4Y/5Y-TZP data**: most translucent zirconia studies have <5-year follow-up; LTD behavior under intraoral conditions beyond 10 years unknown
2. **Zirconia implant fixtures**: promising osseointegration data (comparable to titanium), but long-term survival RCT data limited to <10 years
3. **3D printing (additive manufacturing)**: photopolymerization-based ceramic printing advancing; may challenge milling for complex geometries — clinical outcomes data nascent
4. **Bioactive ceramics**: research-stage materials incorporating antibacterial or osteoconductive properties into prosthetic ceramics

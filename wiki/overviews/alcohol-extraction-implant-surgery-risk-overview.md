## 한국어 핵심요약

> [!summary] 한국어 핵심요약
> - **질문**: 음주가 발치·임플란트 수술에 어떤 주의사항을 만드는가. 이 페이지는 위키가 보유한 10편(메타분석 3·후향 3·동물 2·SR 1·논평 1)으로만 답한다.
> - **결론 1 — "음주 여부"는 쓸모없는 변수다.** 전체 음주군의 임플란트 실패 교차비 (Odds Ratio, OR)는 0.87 (95% 신뢰구간 (Confidence Interval, CI) 0.50–1.51)로 중립이지만, **과량 음주는 OR 2.78 (2.08–3.91)**, 저·중등도는 OR 0.40 (0.21–0.76)이다. 즉 신호는 "과량"에만 있고, 저용량의 역방향 연관은 저자 스스로 교란으로 규정한다.
> - **결론 2 — 두 가지 결과(outcome)를 섞지 마라.** 임플란트 **실패**의 과량 음주 OR 2.78, 임플란트 **주위염**의 OR **2.07 (1.49–2.85, I²=0.0%, 3편)**. 같은 방향, 다른 결과. 주위염 쪽은 3편뿐이라 "제한적 근거(limited evidence)"가 저자들의 표현이다.
> - **결론 3 — 용량 구배는 실제로 관찰된다.** 후향 코호트에서 주위염 발생률이 **과량 46.2% vs 저·중등도 11.5%**였고, 중등도 음주 −79.1% (P=0.0365)·저용량 −51.3% (P=0.026). 발현 시점은 **31.3 ± 22.2개월**, 대부분 첫 2년 내다. (단 이 논문은 표본 수를 기재하지 않았다 — 인용 시 주의.)
> - **결론 4 — 기전은 두 갈래다.** ①골형성: 래트에서 음주 **기간**이 길수록 직접 골-임플란트 접촉률 (Bone-to-Implant Contact, BIC)이 낮아지고 결손은 치근단부에 집중된다. ②숙주면역: 메타분석이 드는 기전은 호중구·대식세포·T세포 기능 저하 → 주위염 취약.
> - **결론 5 — 가역성 근거가 하나 있다.** 마우스에서 수술 **직후** 음주를 끊으면 임플란트 주위 골형성과 계면 강도가 대조군 수준으로 회복됐다. 단 이는 정형외과(경골) 모델이고, **"수술 전 며칠 금주"를 정당화하지는 않는다** — 실험의 금주는 수술 시점에 시작됐다.
> - **결론 6 — 출혈은 생각보다 안 난다.** 간경변 환자 190내원/333치아에서 술중 비정상출혈 6.31%·술후 12건이었고 **전부 국소지혈로 조절**됐다. 7편 SR도 출혈 0–8.9%. 혈소판 >16,000/mm³·국제정상화비 (International Normalized Ratio, INR) <3이면 술전 수혈이 필요하지 않다는 것이 두 연구의 결론이다. 혈소판 **수**는 출혈의 16%만 설명한다(기능이 문제).
> - **결론 7 — 감염 합병증에서는 음주가 유의하다.** 악골 골수염 54명에서 주요 합병증이 음주군 41.2% vs 비음주군 10.8% (p=0.025). 단 흡연(p=0.018)·영양실조(p=0.027)와 같은 표 안에 있고 다변수 보정이 없어 **세 변수를 한 덩어리("숙주 예비력")로 읽어야 한다**.
> - **결론 8 — 술전에 물어야 하는 것은 치유가 아니라 금단이다.** 과량 음주력은 알코올 금단 증후군 (Alcohol Withdrawal Syndrome, AWS) 위험이며, 술후 합병증·재원기간·비용을 올린다. 치과에서 할 일은 프로토콜 시행이 아니라 **선별과 의뢰**다.
> - **결론 9 — 가장 큰 근거 공백: 건성발치와 (Dry Socket, Alveolar Osteitis).** 발치 후 최대 흔한 합병증인데, 건성발치와 위험인자 문헌(36편 확인)은 **음주를 변수로 측정하지 않는다** — 흡연·경구피임제·난발치가 지배적이다. "음주가 건성발치와를 늘린다"는 이 위키는 **주장할 수 없다**.
> - **실행 요약**: (a) 음주는 "예/아니오"가 아니라 **양·패턴**으로 문진 (b) 과량군은 흡연과 같은 자리에 두고 술전 상담 (c) 간경변 병력은 INR·혈소판 확인 + 국소지혈 계획, 수혈 기본값 아님 (d) 과량 음주력은 금단 위험으로 보고 의뢰 (e) "가벼운 음주가 임플란트를 보호한다"는 어떤 형태로도 환자에게 말하지 않는다.

## Three-line Summary

Synthesis of 10 held papers answering what alcohol changes about tooth extraction and implant surgery: three meta-analyses / systematic reviews, three retrospective cohorts, two animal experiments, one review-commentary, and one contradicting cohort.

The consistent finding is that **dose, not exposure**, carries the signal — heavy drinking raises implant failure (OR 2.78, 2.08–3.91) and peri-implantitis (OR 2.07, 1.49–2.85) while pooled "any drinking" is null (OR 0.87); bleeding in cirrhosis and jaw-osteomyelitis complications are the other two clinical surfaces alcohol touches, and both are more manageable than usually assumed.

The decisive gap is dry socket: the alveolar osteitis risk-factor literature does not measure alcohol at all, so this wiki cannot claim an alcohol–dry-socket link — and the one reversible finding (abstinence at surgery restores osseointegration in mice) is an orthopaedic model that does not license a specific pre-operative abstinence window.

## 세줄요약

보유 10편(메타분석·SR 3, 후향 코호트 3, 동물실험 2, 논평 1, 반대 코호트 1)으로 "음주가 발치·임플란트 수술에서 무엇을 바꾸는가"를 종합.

일관된 결론은 **"음주 여부"가 아니라 "용량"**이 신호라는 것 — 과량 음주는 임플란트 실패 (교차비 (Odds Ratio, OR) 2.78, 95% 신뢰구간 (Confidence Interval, CI) 2.08–3.91)와 임플란트 주위염(OR 2.07, 1.49–2.85)을 올리지만 전체 음주군은 중립(OR 0.87)이고, 간경변 출혈과 악골 골수염 합병증이 음주가 닿는 나머지 두 임상면이다.

결정적 공백은 **건성발치와** — 발치와 위험인자 문헌이 음주를 아예 측정하지 않아 이 위키는 음주-건성발치와 연관을 주장할 수 없고, 유일한 가역성 근거(마우스 수술 직후 금주)는 정형외과 모델이라 특정 술전 금주 기간을 정당화하지 못한다.

**Excluded by design.** Smoking-only habit papers (see [[implants/survival/fan-2024-smoking-early-implant-failure-sr-ma]] for that axis), and alcohol-as-solvent or polyvinyl-alcohol materials papers, which dominated naive PubMed queries and are not about drinking.

**What "alcohol" means in each study is not the same variable.** It ranges from a chart-noted "alcohol habit" (binary, Fenelon), through self-reported light/moderate/heavy strata (Bhatia), post-hoc pooled dose subgroups (Boroomand), to 10–15% ethanol in drinking water (Döring) and unstated rat dosing (de Deco). No held study reports grams of ethanol per day. This is the single biggest reason the literature cannot produce a threshold.

## 1. Three outcomes, three different messages

Alcohol does not have one dental effect; it has three, and they are managed differently.

| Outcome | Best held estimate | Practical consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Implant failure** | Heavy drinking OR 2.78 (2.08–3.91); overall OR 0.87 (0.50–1.51) | Ask about amount; heavy drinkers are a risk group |
| **Peri-implantitis** | OR 2.07 (1.49–2.85), I² = 0.0%, 3 studies | Same conversation, later timepoint; maintenance matters more |
| **Extraction bleeding (cirrhosis)** | 0–8.9% events, all locally controlled | Local haemostasis plan; not a transfusion decision |
| **Jaw osteomyelitis complications** | 41.2% vs 10.8% (p = 0.025) | Host-reserve flag before aggressive jaw surgery |
| **Post-operative withdrawal** | Direction only (↑ complications, LOS, cost) | Screen and refer; this is a medical problem |

The clinical error to avoid is answering "is alcohol a problem?" as a single yes/no. A patient can be at negligible bleeding risk, meaningful failure risk, and serious withdrawal risk at the same time.

## 2. Dose is the variable — and the low-dose result is not good news

Three independent sources converge on the same shape:

- Pooled data: overall OR 0.87 (null) → heavy OR 2.78 → low-to-moderate OR 0.40.
- A single cohort: 46.2% peri-implantitis in heavy drinkers vs 11.5% in light/moderate.
- Animal data: BIC falls monotonically with months of exposure.

The temptation is to read the OR 0.40 as "light drinking protects implants". Both the meta-analysis authors and this wiki reject that reading. The likely explanation is the healthy-drinker artefact — lighter drinkers are more often affluent, dentally attended, and non-smoking — plus the fact that nearly every included study is retrospective. **The usable asymmetry is: heavy drinking is a risk factor; light drinking is not evidence of protection.** Saying the second half out loud to a patient is a communication error with real consequences.

A second asymmetry matters more than it looks: because exposure was recorded as a binary "alcohol abuse" variable in the largest single cohort (2,537 implants, no association, P ≥ .12), the field's apparent disagreement is largely an artefact of measurement. Where dose was split, the signal appeared. Where it was collapsed, it vanished.

## 3. Mechanism — two pathways, not one

**Bone formation.** In rats, longer alcohol exposure produced progressively lower direct bone-to-implant contact, worst apically, with serum calcium unchanged and phosphate raised. That points at disturbed remodelling rather than simple mineral depletion, and it explains why chronicity — not a single heavy night — is what the clinical dose-response reflects.

**Host immunity.** The peri-implantitis meta-analysis attributes the association to reduced neutrophil, macrophage, and T-cell function, increasing infection susceptibility. This is a different pathway from bone formation, which is why heavy drinking plausibly raises *both* failure and peri-implantitis, and why an abstinence plan aimed at surgery does not automatically protect against late disease.

**Reversibility — the one experiment that asks the right question.** Mice given 10–15% ethanol for three months before a loaded porous titanium tibial implant showed reduced peri-implant bone formation and a weaker interface; when ethanol was withdrawn at implantation, both returned to control levels. Two cautions: the model is orthopaedic (no plaque, no transmucosal seal), and abstinence began *at* surgery, so nothing here specifies how many days of pre-operative abstinence are useful. It is evidence that the deficit is not permanent, not evidence for a protocol.

## 4. Extraction in the drinker who has become cirrhotic

Heavy drinking eventually presents as cirrhosis, and cirrhosis is where the extraction decision actually gets hard — thrombocytopenia, raised INR, and a patient who may need surgery soon.

Two held sources say the same counter-intuitive thing. Across seven studies, with platelets 16,000–216,000/mm³ and INR below 4, haemorrhagic events occurred in 0–8.9% and were almost all controlled with local measures. In the 190-visit primary series, unusual intra-operative bleeding (persisting >10 minutes after routine haemostasis) occurred in 6.31% of visits and post-operative complications in 12 more — every event controlled locally, none requiring escalation beyond local measures.

The predictor analysis is the more useful part: low platelet count was associated with intra-operative bleeding (P = 0.026) but explained only 16% of cases (adjusted R² = 0.16). The authors read the unexplained 84% as platelet *function*. For the chair, that means a normal-looking platelet count does not guarantee a dry field, and a low one does not mandate transfusion — it mandates a haemostatic plan and a way to bring the patient back.

**What this evidence does not say.** It does not say alcohol causes bleeding. Cirrhosis aetiology is not stratified in either source; a hepatitis-C or autoimmune cirrhotic is inside the same data. Citing these pages as "alcohol makes extractions bleed" is a misread of what was measured.

## 5. Infection: the jaw-osteomyelitis signal

Jaw osteomyelitis is rare, but it is the one held dataset where an alcohol habit sits inside a complication model with countable outcomes. Of 54 histologically confirmed cases, 17 (31.5%) suffered a major complication — pathological fracture, deep neck abscess, or cutaneous fistula. An alcohol habit was present in 41.2% of those versus 10.8% of the uncomplicated group (p = 0.025), with smoking (p = 0.018), malnutrition (p = 0.027), and implant-induced osteomyelitis (p = 0.003) alongside it.

Two readings are both defensible and both should be stated:

- **Strong reading:** in a patient facing aggressive jaw surgery, a documented alcohol habit is one of four pre-treatment flags for a complicated course, and it costs nothing to ask.
- **Weak reading:** with 54 patients, 17 events, and no multivariable model, the alcohol effect cannot be separated from the smoking and malnutrition it travels with. Treat them as a single "host reserve" cluster.

Either way, one detail is actionable: 41.2% of the complicated cases followed recent implant placement, and clinical bone exposure predicted a refractory course. A non-healing implant site in a heavy drinker is not a watch-and-wait situation.

## 6. The risk nobody screens for: withdrawal

The rest of this page is about healing. This section is about the patient who is physically dependent, where the perioperative danger is the cessation itself. Alcohol withdrawal syndrome after head and neck cancer surgery is associated with increased medical and surgical complications, longer hospitalisation, and higher costs; harm-reduction and addiction-medicine approaches can reduce morbidity and mortality, though they are not fully validated in that surgical population.

The transfer to dentistry is deliberately modest. Absolute withdrawal risk in a routine extraction is far lower than in major oncologic resection, and this wiki holds no protocol (no severity scale thresholds, no benzodiazepine dosing). What transfers is the *action*: when heavy intake is disclosed before a procedure involving sedation, a general anaesthetic, or an inpatient stay, that disclosure belongs with the patient's physician or addiction medicine — not just in the dental note.

## 7. The evidence gap: dry socket

Alveolar osteitis is the most common complication after extraction, and it is the question a patient is most likely to ask ("will drinking slow the healing?"). The wiki cannot answer it.

The dry-socket risk-factor literature that this wiki holds is dominated by smoking, oral contraceptives, surgical difficulty, and pre-existing infection; alcohol is not measured as a variable. That is a gap in the literature, not a finding of no effect — and the difference matters. A "no association" claim requires a study that measured exposure and found nothing; here there is no measurement at all.

What can honestly be said: alcohol impairs bone healing in animal models, and bone healing is what fills a socket, so a plausible mechanism exists; but no held clinical study tests the association. If this question matters clinically, the missing paper is a prospective cohort of extraction patients with quantified alcohol intake and a defined alveolar osteitis outcome.

## 8. Practical decision ladder

1. **Ask about amount and pattern, not presence.** "Do you drink?" produces the OR 0.87 null. "How many units, how many days a week, and have you ever stopped suddenly?" produces the signal.
2. **Stratify.** Heavy / dependent drinking → risk conversation + withdrawal screen. Light or occasional → no risk claim in either direction, and explicitly no reassurance that light drinking is protective.
3. **Before implant placement in a heavy drinker**, put alcohol in the same pre-surgical conversation as smoking, and be explicit that the failure signal is heavy intake (OR 2.78) while the peri-implantitis signal is separate (OR 2.07) and later.
4. **If the patient has cirrhosis**, check INR and platelets, and plan local haemostasis (suture, haemostatic agent, splint, pressure, reachable follow-up). Do not treat a moderately raised INR as an automatic transfusion indication — in the held evidence, local measures controlled every event.
5. **Before aggressive jaw surgery**, record the alcohol habit alongside smoking and nutritional status; if the site is implant-related or bone is exposed, lower the threshold for escalation.
6. **If heavy intake is disclosed and the plan involves sedation, GA, or admission**, hand the information to the medical team. That is the only withdrawal action this evidence base supports.
7. **Do not promise an abstinence timeline.** The mouse experiment stopped ethanol *at* surgery; there is no held human data on how many days of pre-operative abstinence help, or whether reduced intake substitutes for cessation.
8. **Do not claim an alcohol–dry-socket link.** No held study measures it.

## 9. What would change this page

- A prospective cohort with quantified ethanol intake (grams/day) and a hard implant outcome — the thing that would turn OR 2.78 into a threshold.
- Any study measuring alcohol as a dry-socket risk factor.
- A human study of pre-operative abstinence duration, not just cessation at surgery (the Döring experiment's explicit next step).
- A multivariable model separating alcohol from smoking and malnutrition in jaw infection.
- Peri-implantitis data with dose stratification — the current OR 2.07 rests on three studies with binary exposure.

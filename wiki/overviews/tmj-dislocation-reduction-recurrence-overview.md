## 한국어 핵심요약

> [!summary] 한국어 핵심요약
> - **범위**: 급성 악관절 탈구 (temporomandibular joint dislocation, TMJ dislocation) 의 **복원 (reduction)** 과 **재발 방지 (recurrence prevention)** 를 다룬 10편 종합 — 독일 진료지침 1편, 유럽 국제합의 1편, 체계적 리뷰·매핑 리뷰 3편, 후향 코호트 4편(n=11·42·83·260), 교육용 모델 1편. **메타분석은 10편 중 0편** — 어떤 통합 추정치도 존재하지 않는다.
> - **결론(thesis) 1 — 문헌의 관심과 임상 문제가 어긋나 있다**: 문헌은 복원 술기 논쟁에 쏠려 있으나 실제 임상 부담은 재발이다. 헬싱키 260명 코호트에서 **평생 2회 이상 탈구가 61.9%** 이고, 저자 스스로 재발성 탈구의 급성 복원은 *"일시적 처치"* 에 불과하다고 못박는다. 복원에 성공해도 그 환자의 다수는 다시 온다.
> - **결론(thesis) 2 — 재발은 외상·해부 질환이 아니라 동반질환 질환이다**: 전신질환 보유 시 재발 위험비 **1.57 (95% CI 1.27–1.94, p<0.001)**, 신경계 질환 시 **1.34 (1.12–1.60, p=0.003)** 로 유의한 반면 **고령 (1.15, p=.655)·여성 (1.09, p=.365) 은 유의하지 않다**. 외상은 전체 원인의 **6.2%** 뿐 — 과거 문헌의 20~60%와 크게 어긋난다. 기전도 갈린다: 자발성은 재발군의 71.2%, 외상은 비재발군의 81.2% (p=0.003) → **첫 외상성 탈구와 자발성 재발 탈구는 사실상 다른 질환**.
> - **최대 역설 — 가장 좋은 무작위 근거를 가진 술기가 합의에서 빠졌다**: 유일한 3군 무작위 비교 (Ardehali 2016, n=90) 에서 1차 성공률은 **손목축법 (wrist pivot method) 97% > 구강내 기존법 87% > 구외법 (extraoral) 67%** (손목축법 vs 구외법 p<0.01) 인데, 유럽 학회 합의 (ESTMJS) 는 손목축법을 **명시적으로 채택하지 않았다** — 근거가 반대여서가 아니라 **예비투표에서 20명 중 2명만 지지할 만큼 경험이 없어서**다. 독일 지침은 두 술기를 "동등"으로 본다. 즉 근거·지침·합의 3자가 같은 데이터에서 서로 다른 결론에 도달했고, 차이를 만든 변수는 데이터가 아니라 **술자 숙련도**다.
> - **훈련이 율속단계 (rate-limiting step) 다**: 독일 지침은 성공의 핵심 결정인자를 "술자 경험"으로 적고, 헬싱키 코호트는 환자가 응급실까지 오는 이유를 의뢰 기관의 숙련도 부족으로 본다(그런데 **69.2%는 아무 약물 없이 성공** — 장벽은 장비가 아니라 손이다). 바로 이 공백에 **67달러 두개골 모델** 1편이 들어온다 — 근거등급은 최하지만 손목축법·구외법을 손으로 연습하게 해주는 유일한 자료다.
> - **환자자가유도복원 (patient-guided reduction, PGR) — 독립 2편의 수렴**: 부에노스아이레스 n=42 (100%, 1.5–7분) 와 중국 장시 n=83 (100%, 평균 **150±52초**) 은 서로 독립된 코호트·다른 프로토콜이지만 같은 패러다임 — **술자는 일정하고 부드러운 견인만 유지, 움직임은 환자가 만든다**. 기전 주장도 동일: 급격한 힘이 유발하는 신장반사 (myotatic/stretch reflex) 를 피한다. 결정적으로 두 편 모두 **타 기관 히포크라테스법 실패 후 전원된 환자**(각각 55%·14.5%)를 전부 복원했다.
> - **그러나 PGR은 "1차 대체"가 아니라 "구조 (rescue) 적응증"까지만 주장 가능하다**: 둘 다 대조군 없음·단일 술자·통증 정량화 없음. 100%는 선택편향과 단일술자 설계가 부풀린 수치다. 다만 **실패한 복원 시도 자체가 다음 시도의 실패 위험인자**라는 기전 논증(통증 경험 후 방어적 근긴장)은 설득력이 있어, *같은 아픈 술기를 반복하지 말고 패러다임을 바꿔라*는 실천 지침은 성립한다.
> - **성공률을 횡단 비교하지 말 것 — 지연시간이 교란한다**: 이바단 n=11에서 히포크라테스법 단독 성공률은 **45% (5/11)** 로 86~87%와 크게 어긋나는데, 그 환자들은 1주·5일·3시간 경과 후 내원했고 1명은 결국 개방수술까지 갔다. 부에노스아이레스의 최난치 2례도 2시간·3시간 경과례(최대 15분·6회 반복), 장시의 난치 12례는 마지막 실패 시도 후 평균 4.2시간. **지연시간과 선행 실패 횟수를 맞추지 않은 성공률 비교는 술기 비교가 아니다.** 독일 지침의 3~4주 수술 전환점도 같은 축이다.
> - **재발 방지의 닻은 자가혈액 주사 (autologous blood injection, ABI) — 단, 고정 (immobilisation) 을 포함한 패키지로**: 최강 근거는 Hegab 2013 무작위시험 — ABI + 4주 악간고정술 (intermaxillary fixation, IMF) 이 절치간 거리 11 mm 감소·**재발 0/16**, ABI 단독은 8/16 재발. 주사 부위도 재현된다: 상관절강 단독 60% 무재발 vs 상관절강 + 관절주변 (pericapsular) 80% (Daif 2010). 반복 주사는 6/16 → 2/16 → 0/16으로 체감. **중요한 역설** — 2026 매핑 리뷰는 "주사 효과만 분리 가능" 게이트 때문에 Hegab 2013·Daif 2010을 **제외**했다. 즉 *주사 단독* 근거는 *주사+고정 패키지* 근거보다 약하다 → 임상 함의는 **ABI는 고정과 함께 쓰라**는 것(그것이 실제로 검증된 조합이다).
> - **덱스트로스 증식치료 (dextrose prolotherapy) — 진짜 모순이며, 대조군 유무가 갈랐다**: 독일 지침은 6개월 재발 없음 91%(무대조 30명 사례군)로 긍정적으로 적었고 매핑 리뷰의 분리가능 근거도 Zhou 2014(무대조 45명, 41/45)뿐이다. 반면 **위약 대조군을 가진 무작위시험 3편 전부에서 덱스트로스는 위약 대비 유의한 우위가 없었다**(Mustafa 2018·Cömert Kiliç 2016·Refai 2011) — 위약군도 같이 좋아졌다(52.3→43.4 mm). → 위키의 결론은 **"탈구 적응증에서 덱스트로스의 위약 초과 효과는 대조 근거로 입증되지 않았다"** 이며, 이는 지침의 서술보다 보수적이다.
> - **보툴리눔 독소 (botulinum toxin, BTX) — 가장 넓은 전문가 지지, 가장 좁은 대조 근거, 거의 없는 실사용**: 독일 지침은 **근거수준 V(전문가 합의)에 권고등급 A**를 부여하는 이례적 조합으로 외항교근 (lateral pterygoid) BTX를 밀고 신규 적응증 인정을 청원했고, ESTMJS도 허가외 (off-label) 전제 하 잠재 적응증으로 포함(GoR B), 소아 재발성에서는 **최다 보고 미세침습법**이다. 그런데 2026 매핑 리뷰는 **경로(근육내 vs 관절내)** 때문에 BTX를 통째로 제외했고, 실제 헬싱키 코호트 사용률은 **1.2% (3/260)**. → 권고와 실행 사이 간극이 크고, 효능 추론의 근거는 사례군뿐이다.
> - **수술 — 50년간 무작위시험 0편, 그러나 쓰인 곳에서는 듣는다 + 예후 게이트가 따로 있다**: RCT만 추린 체계적 리뷰에서 **수술 기법을 평가한 무작위시험은 한 편도 없다**. 실사용은 희박하다(관절융기절제술 (eminectomy) 1.9%, 5/260). ESTMJS의 **유일한 GoR A가 "비수술이 먼저 실패해야 한다"** 인 것은 이 공백의 정직한 반영이다. 소아 9편 중 수술 2편은 모두 최후수단이었고 둘 다 재발 0(6개월·36/28개월). 여기에 헬싱키가 더한 축이 **예후**다 — 코호트 28.1%가 연구기간 내 사망, 수술 예정 15명 중 일부 취소·**5명은 예정 수술 전 사망** → 고령·신경질환 재발 환자에서 대수술의 이득은 기대여명과 함께 저울질해야 한다.
> - **치과 진료실 자체가 원인의 한 축인데, 국제합의는 그 권고를 버렸다**: 헬싱키에서 **치과 진료·위내시경이 원인의 10.8%**, 장시에서 의인성 10/83. 독일 지침은 전신마취 전 탈구 병력·위험인자 문진과 기관내관 삽관 전후 하악 운동 확인을 GoR B로 권고했으나, **ESTMJS는 "마취과 소관"이라며 이 권고를 삭제했다.** 치과 위키의 입장: 발치·장시간 치료·내시경이 유발 인자로 명시돼 있고 실측 비중이 10%대이므로 **독일 지침의 술전 문진 규칙을 유지한다.**
> - **진단은 임상이 기본, 영상은 목적이 있을 때**: 첫 비외상성 전형례(양측·폐구 불가)는 병력 + 촉진으로 진단 가능 — 양 지침 일치, 헬싱키 실사용 영상 **32.7%**. 영상의 역할은 골절 배제·비전형 방향·치료계획이다. 단 **외상 병력이 있으면 복원 전 CT/MRI는 필수**이고, 골절 의심·확인 시 PGR은 금기(장시의 과두골절 2례는 수술 전 임시정렬에 그쳤다).

## Three-line Summary

Ten pages on TMJ dislocation split cleanly into two clinical problems that the literature conflates: getting the condyle back (technique) and keeping it there (prevention). None of the ten performed a meta-analysis, so no pooled estimate exists anywhere in this evidence base. The larger clinical burden is the second problem — 61.9% of a 260-patient Helsinki cohort had ≥2 lifetime dislocations, and that cohort's authors state that acute repositioning of a recurrent luxation is a temporary treatment only.

On technique, the sole randomized three-arm comparison (Ardehali 2016, n=90) ranks wrist pivot 97% > conventional intraoral 87% > extraoral 67%, yet the European consensus explicitly declined to recommend wrist pivot because only 2 of 20 voting members had experience with it — a documented case of consensus diverging from evidence on operator familiarity rather than on data. Two independent uncontrolled series of patient-guided reduction (n=42, 1.5–7 min; n=83, 150±52 s) each reduced 100% including every patient referred after failed Hippocratic attempts elsewhere, which supports a rescue indication but cannot support first-line replacement. Success rates are not comparable across series because delay and prior failed-attempt count are uncontrolled: a Nigerian series reporting 45% for the Hippocratic manoeuvre alone comprised patients presenting days to a week late.

On prevention, autologous blood injection combined with 4 weeks of intermaxillary fixation is the anchor (0/16 recurrences vs 8/16 for injection alone, Hegab 2013), site-dependent (pericapsular plus intra-articular 80% vs intra-articular alone 60% recurrence-free), and notably the 2026 mapping review excluded exactly those studies under an isolable-effect gate — so the package has better evidence than the injection alone. Dextrose prolotherapy is positive in uncontrolled series but not superior to placebo in any of three placebo-armed trials. Botulinum toxin carries the widest expert endorsement (grade A on level-V evidence in the German guideline) with the narrowest controlled evidence and 1.2% real-world use. Surgery has zero randomized trials across roughly fifty years, is performed in 1.9%, and in geriatric neurological patients must be weighed against life expectancy (28.1% cohort mortality; five patients died before planned surgery).

## 세줄요약

악관절 탈구 10편은 문헌이 뒤섞어 다루는 두 임상 문제로 깔끔히 갈린다 — 과두를 되돌리는 **복원 (reduction)** 과 다시 빠지지 않게 하는 **재발 방지 (recurrence prevention)**. 10편 중 메타분석은 0편이므로 이 근거 기반 어디에도 통합 추정치는 없다. 임상 부담이 큰 쪽은 두 번째다: 헬싱키 260명에서 평생 2회 이상 탈구가 **61.9%** 이고, 저자들은 재발성 탈구의 급성 복원을 "일시적 처치"로 규정한다.

술기 축에서 유일한 3군 무작위 비교는 **손목축법 (wrist pivot method) 97% > 구강내 기존법 87% > 구외법 (extraoral) 67%** 인데, 유럽 학회 합의 (ESTMJS) 는 투표자 20명 중 2명만 경험이 있다는 이유로 손목축법 권고를 **거부**했다 — 데이터가 아니라 술자 숙련도가 합의를 가른 기록된 사례. 환자자가유도복원 (patient-guided reduction, PGR) 독립 2편(n=42, 1.5–7분 / n=83, 150±52초)은 각각 100% 복원, 타 기관 히포크라테스법 실패 전원례도 전부 성공 → **구조 (rescue) 적응증은 지지되나 1차 대체는 불가**(대조군 없음·단일 술자). 성공률 횡단 비교는 성립하지 않는다: 히포크라테스법 단독 45%를 보고한 나이지리아 계열은 수일~1주 지연 내원 환자였다.

재발 방지의 닻은 **자가혈액 주사 (autologous blood injection, ABI) + 4주 악간고정술 (intermaxillary fixation, IMF)** 로 재발 0/16 (ABI 단독 8/16), 부위 의존적(관절강+관절주변 80% vs 관절강 단독 60% 무재발)이며, 2026 매핑 리뷰가 "효과 분리 가능" 게이트로 바로 그 연구들을 제외했으므로 **패키지가 주사 단독보다 근거가 좋다**. 덱스트로스 증식치료는 무대조 사례군에선 긍정이나 위약 대조 3편 전부에서 위약 대비 우위 없음. 보툴리눔 독소 (botulinum toxin, BTX) 는 전문가 지지가 가장 넓고(독일 지침은 근거수준 V에 권고등급 A) 대조 근거는 가장 좁으며 실사용은 1.2%. 수술은 약 50년간 무작위시험 0편, 실사용 1.9%, 고령·신경질환군에서는 기대여명과 함께 판단해야 한다(코호트 사망률 28.1%, 예정 수술 전 사망 5명).

## Scope and how to read this page

- **Question**: for an acute TMJ dislocation presenting to a dental or emergency service, what does the held evidence support for (a) reducing this episode and (b) preventing the next one — and where do the guideline, the consensus and the primary data disagree?
- **The ten pages**: one national clinical practice guideline, one international consensus recommendation, one RCT-restricted systematic review, one paediatric systematic review, one injection mapping review, four retrospective cohorts (n=11, 42, 83, 260) and one teaching-model letter. Designs are listed in the Evidence Map below.
- **No pooling anywhere**: none of the ten performed a meta-analysis. Every number on this page is a single-study figure or a guideline statement, reproduced as the member page prints it. Where a figure is internally inconsistent in its source, this page says so rather than resolving it.
- **Two axes, deliberately separated**: the technique literature (Axis 1) and the prevention literature (Axis 2) answer different questions on different populations, and most apparent disagreements between pages dissolve once the axis is fixed. Axis 3 identifies who recurs, Axis 4 covers diagnosis, Axis 5 the iatrogenic share.
- **What this page is not**: not a surgical-technique reference (no randomized surgical evidence exists in this set), not a TMD pain page. For masticatory pain, splints, laser and botulinum toxin in the *pain* indication see [[overviews/masticatory-muscle-pain-evidence-synthesis-2026]] and [[overviews/tmd-management-evidence-ladder]]; for intra-articular pharmacology in painful joints see [[overviews/tmj-pharmacological-intraarticular-treatment-overview]]. Dislocation is a mechanical emergency and shares almost no evidence base with those pages.

## Axis 1 — Acute reduction: what the numbers actually support

### 1.1 The comparative table, with its denominators attached

Every comparative first-try success figure in this evidence base comes from three trials inside one systematic review, plus a guideline's separate breakdown:

| Technique | Source | First-try success | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wrist pivot | Ardehali 2016 (n=30 of 90) | **97%** | p<0.01 vs extraoral; authors recommended it as first line |
| Conventional intraoral | Ardehali 2016 (n=30 of 90) | 87% | — |
| Conventional intraoral | Ardehali 2009 (n=29) | 86.2% (95% CI 73–100) | p=0.009 vs extraoral |
| Extraoral | Ardehali 2016 (n=30 of 90) | 67% | — |
| Extraoral | Ardehali 2009 (n=29) | 55.2% (95% CI 39–79) | the review notes the authors' conclusion is not in line with their own results |
| Extraoral, by laterality | German S3 guideline | **96.7% unilateral vs 54.5% bilateral** | reconciles the low pooled extraoral figures above |
| Conventional, supine vs sitting | Jiantao 2016 (n=40) | 100% in both arms | time 269±70 → 173±17 s (p<0.05); VAS 8.1±0.7 → 6.3±0.8 (p<0.01) |
| Hippocratic alone, late presentations | Okoje 2017 (n=11) | **45% (5/11)** | days-to-a-week delay; one case reached open reduction |
| Patient-guided (jaw movements) | Stolbizer 2020 (n=42) | 100%, uncontrolled | 1.5–7 min in 40/42; 55% had already failed Hippocratic elsewhere |
| Patient-guided + head coupling | Lin 2026 (n=83) | 100%, uncontrolled | 150±52 s; 14.5% had failed ≥2 Hippocratic attempts elsewhere |

Two readings fall out immediately. First, **the extraoral route is not a weak technique, it is a laterality-specific one** — the guideline's 96.7% unilateral against 54.5% bilateral explains why pooled extraoral numbers land in the 55–67% band in cohorts that are majority bilateral (57.3% in Helsinki, 88% in Buenos Aires, 10/11 in Ibadan). Both guidelines accordingly restrict it to unilateral cases or to raised bite/infection risk (dementia, hepatitis C, known intravenous drug use), and Lin 2026's extraoral variant was likewise reserved for extreme intraoral sensitivity. Second, **patient position is a free optimization**: the supine variant did not change success (100% in both arms) but cut reduction time by roughly a third and dropped pain by nearly two VAS points. The German guideline nevertheless recommends the sitting position with head stabilized, and the ESTMJS, after discussion, concluded the positional detail matters less than competent execution and left it to the operator — the honest resolution given one 40-patient trial.

### 1.2 The wrist pivot paradox — consensus diverging from evidence on familiarity

This is the single most instructive finding in the batch, because the divergence is documented rather than inferred:

- **The data** (Ardehali 2016, the only randomized three-arm comparison, n=90): wrist pivot 97%, the highest acute first-try figure anywhere in this evidence base, significantly better than extraoral, and the trial authors recommended it as first line.
- **The German guideline** (2018): the modified Hippocratic and wrist pivot methods are **at least equal** on success rate, reduction-related pain and reduction time — so neither is the default on superiority grounds.
- **The European consensus** (2021): recommends that any manual reduction attempt be made **according to the Hippocratic method**, and states plainly that ESTMJS members *have no experience* with the alternative repositioning procedures described in the literature. The page records that although the wrist pivot method is described at a comparatively high level of evidence and at least equal to the Hippocratic method, only **2 of 20** members in preliminary voting were prepared to support that recommendation.

Nothing in this chain is a contradiction of fact. The consensus did not find contrary data; it declined to recommend a technique its voters could not perform. That is a legitimate guideline decision — a recommendation to use an unfamiliar manoeuvre in an emergency is a recommendation to fail at it — but it has a clear corollary for the wiki: **the technique with the best randomized support is the one European specialists are least trained in, which makes this a training problem, not an evidence problem.** It is exactly why a $67 bench model that explicitly supports wrist pivot and extraoral practice earns a place in a synthesis otherwise built on guidelines and cohorts. The German guideline states that physician experience with the technique is the key determinant of success; the Helsinki cohort attributes emergency referrals to a lack of expertise at the referring centre while reporting that 69.2% of reductions succeeded with no medication at all. The binding constraint across all ten pages is hands, not equipment.

### 1.3 Patient-guided reduction — independent convergence, rescue-level claim

Two uncontrolled series, from different continents and overlapping but distinct periods, arrived at the same paradigm independently:

| | Stolbizer 2020 (Buenos Aires, 2011–2018) | Lin 2026 (Jiangxi, 2017–2022) |
|---|---|---|
| n | 42 (trauma excluded) | 83 consecutive (incl. lateral and posterior) |
| Operator load | constant gentle 45° downward-posterior pressure, thumbs behind last lower molars, **intensity and direction never varied** | continuous gentle downward traction, thumbs intraoral on external oblique ridges |
| Patient movement | up to 5 opening/closing, then up to 5 lateral movements | Step 1 opening + head **extension**; Step 2 closing + head **flexion** |
| Result | 100% (42/42); 1.5–7 min in 40/42 | 100% (83/83); 150±52 s, none reached the 300-s limit |
| Anaesthesia/sedation | none in any case | none in 81 simple cases |
| Prior Hippocratic failures reduced | 23/42 (55%) | 12/83 (14.5%), at mean 4.2 h after last failure |
| Stated mechanism | avoid the stretch (myotatic) reflex; relaxed elevators let the patient's own movement reposition the condyle | same, plus head movement alters the condylar-skull angle to clear the articular eminence |

The convergence is substantive, not cosmetic: two teams, two protocols, one mechanism claim, and in both series **every patient who had already failed the conventional manoeuvre elsewhere was reduced**. That subgroup is the only quasi-comparative signal either paper contains, and it is the one that transfers.

What does not transfer is superiority. Both are single-operator, single-centre, uncontrolled, with no force measurement and no prospective pain scale (Stolbizer records pain as absent without a numeric scale; Lin has chart narrative only). A 100% rate under those conditions is inflated by operator selection and by a lenient endpoint, and Lin's own page says the series should be read as a well-documented technique report, not evidence of superiority. Neither technique appears in either guideline — the German guideline predates both, and the 2021 consensus predates Lin and does not mention Stolbizer.

The defensible position for this wiki: **patient-guided reduction is an additional option with a rescue indication after a failed conventional attempt, and a plausible, cheap, teachable mechanism — not a first-line replacement.** Lin's two hard limits carry over unchanged: pre-reduction CT or MRI is mandatory in any traumatic presentation, and the technique is not for suspected or confirmed fracture (the two condylar-fracture cases achieved temporary palliative alignment only, pending surgery).

### 1.4 Delay and prior failed attempts — the variable nobody controlled

Success rates in this literature are not comparable, and the reason is visible in every cohort:

- **Okoje 2017's 45%** for the Hippocratic manoeuvre alone is far below the 86–87% from the randomized trials. Those 11 patients presented one week after a fall, five days after an assault, three hours after yawning; one failed the manoeuvre awake, then twice under general anaesthesia, then five days of class III traction wires, before open reduction.
- **Stolbizer's two hardest cases** were the two with the longest duration (2 h and 3 h), both referred after failed reduction elsewhere, requiring up to 15 minutes and 6 cycles against 1.5–7 minutes for the rest.
- **Lin's refractory 12** were a mean 4.2 hours (range 2–12 h) from their last failed attempt, with mean symptom duration in the whole cohort 4 hours.
- **The German guideline** states the principle directly — the earlier reduction is performed, the greater the chance of success — and fixes the crossover at **3–4 weeks**, past which manual reduction usually fails and surgical reduction should be considered. The ESTMJS repeats it as its own meta-recommendation: begin treatment as early as possible to limit degenerative change and preserve the chance that conservative or minimally invasive measures work.

Stolbizer adds the mechanism that makes this actionable rather than merely cautionary: **once a Hippocratic attempt has hurt, the patient's protective anticipatory tensing makes subsequent attempts likely to fail.** A failed painful attempt is therefore itself a risk factor for the next failure, which means the correct response to one failed conventional attempt is not a harder second attempt but a change of paradigm — to the patient-guided approach, or to medication/sedation per both guidelines, or to the extraoral route if the case is unilateral. Repeating the same painful manoeuvre is the one strategy the mechanism argues against, and it is what Okoje's Case 8 documents the cost of.

## Axis 2 — Recurrence prevention

### 2.1 Autologous blood injection is the anchor — as a package with immobilisation

The strongest single result in the entire evidence base is Hegab 2013, a randomized three-arm trial the RCT-restricted review rates as the best-supported regimen:

| Arm (n=16 each) | Interincisal reduction at 1 yr | Recurrences |
|---|---|---|
| ABI alone | 8.5 mm | **8/16** |
| IMF alone | 9.13 mm | 3/16 |
| **ABI + 4 weeks IMF** | **11.0 mm** | **0/16** |

All groups p<0.0001 for the within-group change; ABI and IMF alone did not differ significantly from each other. Two further findings replicate and extend it. **Injection site matters**: adding pericapsular tissue to the superior joint space raised the recurrence-free rate from 60% to 80% (Daif 2010, n=30), and only the pericapsular group kept the condyle behind the articular eminence on open-mouth radiographs. **Repeat injection escalates to control**: 6/16 recurred after the first ABI, 2/16 after a second, 0/16 after a third. The mapping review's independent entries point the same way — 0/14 recurrence-free at one year (Oshiro, ~5 mL across superior compartment plus pericapsular) and 18/21 within 36 months (Yoshida, same protocol).

Then comes the batch's second instructive paradox. The 2026 mapping review applied an **isolable-effect gate** — an injection study counted only if its effect could be read independently of simultaneous surgery, arthroscopy or post-procedural immobilisation — and that gate **excluded Hegab 2013 and Daif 2010**, the two studies above, as its largest single exclusion block. The review's own authors flag this as the paper's most consequential limitation and say it narrows clinical representativeness.

This is not a contradiction between the two reviews; they answered different questions. But the combination yields a clinical instruction neither states alone: **the evidence for ABI *plus immobilisation* is stronger than the evidence for ABI alone, so ABI should be given with immobilisation, because that is the combination that was actually tested.** The only comparative study that survives the isolable gate — Machon 2018, double-blind, n=40, intra-articular plus periarticular versus periarticular alone — found the fuller protocol numerically better but **not statistically significant**, which is precisely what one expects when the immobilisation component has been stripped out. Both guidelines already point this way: the German guideline and the ESTMJS both recommend immobilisation after ABI or capsular-ligament surgery, with the ESTMJS specifying the goal as limiting maximum opening and explicitly **not** rigid fixation.

### 2.2 Dextrose prolotherapy — a real contradiction, resolved by comparator

| Source | Finding | Design |
|---|---|---|
| German S3 guideline | 91% no recurrence over 6 months | **one uncontrolled case series, 30 patients** |
| Hoppe 2026 mapping review | 41/45 free of dislocation or subluxation beyond 6 months after a single 2 mL injection of 50% dextrose | **uncontrolled prospective case series** (Zhou 2014) |
| Abrahamsson 2020 (RCT-only SR) | **Not superior to placebo** for mouth opening, pain or luxation frequency in any of three placebo-armed trials; no concentration outperformed another | Mustafa 2018 (n=37, 4 arms), Cömert Kiliç 2016 (n=26), Refai 2011 (n=12) |

The resolution is clean: **every positive prolotherapy figure in this evidence base comes from an uncontrolled series, and every placebo-controlled trial is null.** The placebo arms improved too — in Mustafa 2018 mouth opening fell 52.3 → 43.4 mm on placebo against 54.3 → 39.4, 52.1 → 41.2 and 54.0 → 39.4 mm on 10%, 20% and 30% dextrose, with no significant between-group difference and no recurrence in any arm at four months. Cömert Kiliç 2016 showed 46.3 → 43.7 versus 46.1 → 43.3 mm at one year, also not significant. (Refai 2011's page prints a within-arm p=0.039 alongside "NS between groups" on six patients per arm — an internal inconsistency this page reproduces rather than resolves.)

**The wiki's bottom line is therefore more conservative than the guideline's framing: a placebo-exceeding effect of dextrose prolotherapy in the dislocation indication has not been demonstrated on controlled evidence.** This is the same caveat already attached to the uncontrolled prolotherapy pages elsewhere in the wiki — see [[tmj/park-2024-dextrose-prolotherapy-tmd]] and [[pdrn/choi-2026-efficacy-prolotherapy-temporomandibular-joint-disorders]], both of which report large gains on pain and opening rather than on recurrence. Note also that the sclerosant with the largest reported series in this indication is not dextrose at all but **5% sodium morrhuate** (Xu 1992, n=105, 92.5% excellent-or-good at ≥2 years, extra-articular 0.5 mL per point) — a 1992 uncontrolled series carrying the one subgroup signal in the whole injection literature: poorer results when recurrence was associated with cerebrovascular disease.

### 2.3 Botulinum toxin — widest endorsement, narrowest evidence, negligible use

The four positions held in this batch do not line up:

- **German S3 guideline**: botulinum toxin into the lateral pterygoid, no further dislocations over ≥5 months in case series of up to 21 patients — graded **GoR A on LoE V (expert consensus)**, with the authors petitioning for recurrent dislocation to be recognized as a new indication. A grade-A ("shall") recommendation resting on level-V evidence is an anomaly worth naming explicitly.
- **ESTMJS consensus**: BTX remains **off-label**, manufacturer warnings shall be observed (one of only a handful of hard statements), but it **should nonetheless be included as a potential indication** for recurrent dislocation — GoR B, a step down from the German grade.
- **Paediatric SR**: BoNT-A into the lateral pterygoid is the **most commonly reported** minimally invasive option in children, with ABI close behind — though outcomes are mixed, including one child who recurred 4 months after injection and again 4 months after a repeat before achieving 9 months free.
- **Injection mapping review**: BTX is **excluded entirely** — not for lack of literature but because the recurrent-luxation BTX studies are **intramuscular rather than intra- or periarticular**, failing the review's route criterion.
- **Real-world use**: BoNT-A into masticatory muscles was considered for 6 and given to **3 of 260 patients (1.2%)** in the Helsinki cohort, which explicitly states it cannot support any efficacy inference.

Read together: BTX has the broadest expert endorsement and the thinnest controlled support of any option on this page, is invisible in the injection evidence map for a reason of route rather than of merit, and is almost never actually used. For injection anatomy of the target muscle see [[botulinum-toxin/bae-2025-ultrasonographic-assessment-lateral-pterygoid-muscle]]; for the entirely separate BTX *pain* indication see [[overviews/btx-orofacial-applications-overview]], which must not be read as evidence for this one.

### 2.4 Surgery — zero randomized evidence, rare use, and a prognosis gate

- **The void**: across roughly fifty years of publication, the RCT-restricted review found **no trial at all evaluating a surgical technique** — not eminectomy, not blocking or redressment procedures, not capsulorrhaphy. The review explains the gap mechanistically: recurrent luxation is too rare to recruit, and sham-surgical controls are ethically questionable.
- **The guideline positions**: the German guideline documents eminectomy as best-documented among the open procedures but grades the recurrence pathway GoR 0 on LoE IIIa–IV+; the ESTMJS makes its **only GoR A recommendation** the statement that non-surgical methods must have failed before any minimally invasive or open intervention. Given a literature with no surgical trials, that single strong recommendation is the honest one.
- **Where it is used, it works**: in the paediatric review, invasive treatment appears in 2 of 9 studies — one eminectomy, one Dautrey's procedure — both as last resort after conservative and injection therapy failed, and both recurrence-free at follow-up (6 months; 36 and 28 months). The review's notable conclusion is that **the literature does not support treating age alone as a contraindication to surgery** in children. Okoje's Case 6, a 26-year-old with short condyles and ~10 prior episodes who recurred immediately after maxillomandibular fixation, resolved completely with bilateral eminectomy.
- **How rarely it is used**: eminectomy in **1.9% (5/260)** in Helsinki; one open reduction in 11 in Ibadan. The divergence between those two services in surgical end-point care is most plausibly surgical capacity rather than disease severity.
- **The prognosis gate the cohort adds**: 15 patients had surgery planned, some cancelled for anaesthetic contraindications, and **five died before their planned surgery**; cohort mortality was 28.1%, with mean time to death 884 days in the recurrent group against 489 in the non-recurrent. The authors' conclusion is explicit — in geriatric patients with neurological conditions and recurrent luxation, the benefit of major surgery must be weighed carefully against limited life expectancy. This is a dimension absent from both guidelines.

## Axis 3 — Who recurs: a comorbidity disease, not a trauma or anatomy disease

The Helsinki cohort is the only page here with risk estimates, and it reorders the intuition:

| Predictor of recurrent luxation | RR (95% CI) | p |
|---|---|---|
| **Any general condition present** | **1.57 (1.27–1.94)** | **<0.001** |
| **Neurological condition present** | **1.34 (1.12–1.60)** | **0.003** |
| Geriatric age | 1.15 (0.86–1.27) | 0.655 |
| Female sex | 1.09 (0.89–1.33) | 0.365 |
| Cardiovascular condition | — | 0.954 |

Three consequences follow. **First, the demographic signals are noise.** Despite a cohort that is 58.1% female with 38.1% aged ≥65, neither sex nor geriatric age predicted recurrence; comorbidity did. **Second, mechanism separates the two entities**: spontaneous dislocation accounted for 71.2% of recurrent cases while injury accounted for 81.2% of non-recurrent ones (p=0.003) — a traumatic first dislocation and a spontaneous recurrent one behave as different clinical problems, and the recurrence measure here is lifetime (≥2 episodes) rather than a follow-up window, which makes it immune to the short-follow-up problem that renders most recurrence figures incomparable. **Third, trauma is not the expected mechanism**: 6.2% against the 20–60% quoted from earlier series, which argues against reflexive imaging on a trauma assumption while the same cohort flags that the adequacy of fracture exclusion remains unresolved.

The qualitative pages corroborate the neuro-pharmacological axis case by case. Okoje's single idiopathic patient was on thioridazine plus diazepam; a 40-year-old on phenytoin and phenobarbitone presented with her seventh episode after a fall during a seizure. Lin recorded drug-induced dystonia in 3 of 83. The paediatric review found that multidisciplinary involvement — psychology, neurology — recurs in the cases that resolved, and Helsinki's proposed mechanism is altered muscular tonus, with epilepsy, connective-tissue disorders and oromandibular dystonia all described as risk factors.

**Chairside translation**: at a *first* dislocation, take a neurological history and review the drug list (antipsychotics, antiepileptics, anything dystonia-inducing), because that is the variable that predicts whether this patient returns — and hand over a recurrence-prevention plan rather than treating a successful reduction as the end of the episode. A neurological condition was present in 31.2% of the Helsinki cohort.

## Axis 4 — Diagnosis and imaging

Both guidelines converge, and the cohorts show the convergence is already practice:

- **Clinical diagnosis suffices** for a first-time non-traumatic episode — history plus inspection and palpation, with the cardinal signs being inability to close the jaws with impaired occlusion and an empty joint socket on examination (German guideline, GoR 0; ESTMJS Rec. 1). The Helsinki cohort reports inability to close the mouth in 94.2% and used imaging in only 32.7% overall.
- **Imaging is purposive, not routine**: to exclude facial fracture, when symptoms admit other differential diagnoses, and in the post-acute phase to assess pathogenesis and plan further treatment (both guidelines). In Helsinki, not using radiography was associated with the recurrent group (66.9%, p=0.019) — recurrent cases are recognized clinically because the patient and the clinician have seen it before.
- **Two hard limits from the newest cohort**: pre-reduction CT or MRI is **mandatory in any traumatic presentation**, and patient-guided reduction is **not recommended where fracture is suspected or confirmed**. Lin's own protocol deviation is instructive — 12 of 83 lateral cases (14.5%) were treated without pre-reduction imaging because of severe distress and a clear history of prior failed reduction, with post-reduction CT obtained in all 12 to confirm repositioning and exclude occult fracture.
- **Atypical directions exist and are managed the same way**: lateral 19.3% and posterior 6.0% in Lin's series, against 100% anterior in Buenos Aires (by exclusion) and 11/11 anterior in Ibadan. Atypical directions usually indicate high-energy trauma, which routes back to mandatory imaging.

## Axis 5 — The iatrogenic share, and the one recommendation the consensus dropped

This axis matters more to a dental practice than to a surgical one, and it is where the two guidelines part company.

- **The dental chair is a documented trigger**: dentist visit or gastroscopy accounted for **10.8%** of the Helsinki cohort's mechanisms, and Lin recorded iatrogenic causes in **10 of 83**. The German guideline names intubation, endoscopy and prolonged dental treatment such as tooth extraction explicitly, and the ESTMJS discussion identifies general anaesthesia with oral intubation plus endoscopic and laryngoscopic procedures requiring wide mouth opening as the most frequently described iatrogenic cause.
- **The German guideline's response** (GoR B; LoE IV–V+): ask about previous dislocations and risk factors before any general-anaesthesia procedure, and check functional jaw mobility before and after intubation.
- **The ESTMJS discarded both recommendations**, on the basis that they belong to anaesthetists as part of pre-anaesthetic assessment.

For a dental knowledge base the German position is the one to keep. The procedures named include extraction and prolonged dental treatment, the measured iatrogenic share is around one in ten, and the screening question costs nothing. A patient with a prior dislocation, a neurological condition, or a dislocation-predisposing drug list is identifiable before the appointment starts — and both guidelines agree that dislocation is easily missed in sedated, demented, post-traumatic and communication-limited patients, which is the same population a long appointment puts at risk.

## Synthesized decision ladder

**At presentation — acute, non-traumatic, typical (bilateral, cannot close):**
1. Diagnose clinically; image only to exclude fracture or when the presentation is atypical or traumatic. Reduce **as early as possible** — delay is the strongest modifiable determinant of success in every page here.
2. First attempt **without any medication** (both guidelines; 69.2% succeed this way in practice). Use the technique you can actually perform competently — the consensus position, and the reason the German guideline names operator experience as the key determinant.
3. If the case is **unilateral**, the extraoral route is a strong option (96.7%); if bilateral, it is not (54.5%).
4. Consider **supine positioning** — no success penalty, roughly a third less time and nearly two VAS points less pain on the one trial that tested it.

**If the first attempt fails — change something, do not simply repeat:**
5. Switch paradigm rather than escalating force: **patient-guided reduction** has two independent series in which every externally-failed patient was reduced, no anaesthesia, no iatrogenic injury. A failed painful attempt makes the next attempt more likely to fail through protective guarding.
6. Then escalate pharmacologically per both guidelines — muscle relaxants and/or analgesics, then sedation, then general anaesthesia.
7. Past **3–4 weeks** of persistence, manual reduction usually fails: route to surgical reduction.

**After successful reduction — the step the literature under-serves:**
8. Standard aftercare as practised: restriction of mouth opening, soft diet 2–3 weeks, jaw bandage, chin support when yawning. Avoid rigid fixation (ESTMJS Rec. 21).
9. **Decide whether this is a recurrence case**, because 61.9% are. Take a neurological history, review drugs, and remember that spontaneous mechanism predicts recurrence while traumatic mechanism predicts the opposite.
10. For recurrent or habitual dislocation, non-surgical first (an ESTMJS GoR A). The best-supported regimen is **autologous blood injection into superior joint space plus pericapsular tissue, combined with immobilisation** — and repeat injection is a legitimate strategy before surgery (6/16 → 2/16 → 0/16). Do not promise a dextrose-prolotherapy benefit that placebo-controlled trials have not shown. BTX is reasonable and widely endorsed but off-label with case-series-only support.
11. Escalate to surgery only after non-surgical and minimally invasive failure. Eminectomy is the best-documented procedure and has no randomized evidence. In geriatric patients with neurological disease, weigh the operation against life expectancy.
12. **Prevent the iatrogenic episode**: screen for prior dislocation and risk factors before general anaesthesia or a long appointment, and check jaw mobility before and after intubation.

## Evidence gaps (stated, not inferred)

- **No surgical RCT exists** for any technique — eminectomy, blocking/redressment, capsulorrhaphy — and the review names the obstacles (rarity prevents recruitment; sham surgery is ethically questionable).
- **No head-to-head trial of patient-guided reduction against the Hippocratic method.** Both PGR series are single-operator and uncontrolled; the only comparative signal is the externally-failed subgroup.
- **No meta-analysis anywhere in these ten pages**, so no pooled estimate of any effect exists in this evidence base.
- **Follow-up never exceeds 12 months** in the randomized injection literature, against a 36-month recurrence-free benchmark that no included study met.
- **Quality of life was measured by no included study**, and the paediatric review's pre-specified mandibular-opening-path measure was reported by **none** of its 9 studies.
- **Isolable BTX evidence for this indication does not exist** — the available studies are intramuscular and fall outside the injection evidence map's route criterion.
- **Real-world preventive uptake is too sparse to support inference**: BoNT-A 1.2% and surgery 1.9% in the largest cohort.
- **Follow-up attrition is severe in low-resource settings** — 63.6% lost before two weeks in Ibadan, leaving recurrence and long-term function unmeasured.
- **Paediatric evidence is 9 studies and 10 tabulated patients in 25 years**, described by its own authors as an unsatisfactory level of scientific attention.

## What this page does not claim

- That any reduction technique is **superior** to another outside the one randomized three-arm comparison (wrist pivot vs extraoral, p<0.01). The 100% rates from the two patient-guided series are uncontrolled and single-operator.
- That patient-guided reduction should **replace** the Hippocratic method as first line. The supported claim is an additional option with a rescue indication.
- That the **European consensus is wrong** to decline the wrist pivot method. It is a defensible decision about operator competence; the point is that it is a training constraint, not a finding about efficacy.
- That **dextrose prolotherapy is useless** — only that superiority over placebo has not been demonstrated in the dislocation indication on the three placebo-armed trials held here.
- That **BTX does not work** for recurrence prevention — only that its endorsement rests on expert consensus and case series, and that no isolable intra/periarticular evidence exists because the route is intramuscular.
- Anything about **TMD pain** management. Dislocation is a mechanical emergency; the pain literature is a separate evidence base on separate pages.

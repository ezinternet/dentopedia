> - **MMA계 레진 시멘트**: PEEK에는 복합레진계 레진 시멘트보다 MMA(Methyl Methacrylate)계 레진 시멘트(예: Super-Bond, Beautylink)가 더 적합하며, AB+PT 전처리와 병용해야 내구성을 보장.
> - **나노세라믹 임플란트 크라운 변연격차 (Marginal Gap)**: 동일 디지털 설계에서 밀링(Milling, 절삭가공) 크라운은 3D프린팅 대비 유의하게 작은 변연격차 (34.08 vs 59.71 µm, 노화 후 37.76 vs 63.83 µm; p<0.001). 두 방식 모두 120 µm 임상 허용 기준 내에 있어 임상적으로 사용 가능하나, 밀링이 더 나은 안전 여유를 제공.
> - **나노세라믹 노화 후 변연격차 증가**: 두 군 모두 열·기계적 노화 후 변연격차가 유의하게 증가하며(군내 p<0.001), 이는 시멘트 층 저하에 기인한 것으로 해석됨.
> - **캐비티 라이너 (Cavity Liner) 효과 없음**: 코크란 SR+MA (8 RCT, 700명 이상) — 구치부 Class I/II 복합레진 하방 캐비티 라이너는 술후 과민증 감소에 일관된 효과 없음(저질 근거), 수복물 수명 개선도 없음(1–2년 실패율 동일).

## Three-line Summary

Three independent in-vitro/SR studies covering PEEK surface pre-treatment for MMA-based luting, hybrid nanoceramic CAD-CAM fabrication route selection, and cavity liner indication under posterior composite.

AB+PT (airborne abrasion + non-thermal plasma) is the only PEEK pre-treatment achieving durable bond after aging; milled nanoceramic crowns showed significantly smaller marginal gaps than 3D-printed (34 vs 60 µm; p<0.001) while both stayed within 120 µm; and a Cochrane SR (8 RCTs) found cavity liner under posterior composite does not reduce postoperative hypersensitivity or improve longevity.

Clinical translations: PEEK must be sandblasted then plasma-treated chairside immediately before luting; choose milling over 3D printing for nanoceramic implant crowns when precision is critical; stop placing cavity liners under posterior composite by default — justify each case individually.

## 세줄요약

PEEK 합착 전처리·하이브리드 나노세라믹 제조 방식·구치부 복합레진 하방 캐비티 라이너 적응증을 각각 다룬 세 독립 연구.

AB+PT 병용만이 노화 후에도 내구적인 PEEK 결합강도 달성; 밀링 나노세라믹 크라운이 3D프린팅보다 유의하게 작은 변연격차(34 vs 60 µm; p<0.001), 두 방식 모두 120 µm 허용 기준 내; 코크란 SR(8 RCT)에서 캐비티 라이너는 술후 과민증 감소나 수복물 수명 개선에 일관된 효과 없음.

임상 적용: PEEK는 합착 직전 체어사이드 AB→PT 시행 후 MMA계 시멘트 사용; 나노세라믹 임플란트 크라운에는 밀링 선택; 구치부 복합레진 하방 캐비티 라이너는 루틴 사용 중단 — 증례별 이유로만 정당화.

## Summary

Three recently available or recently synthesized evidence sources address distinct but complementary material-handling questions that arise frequently in restorative and implant-prosthetic practice:

1. **PEEK bonding** (Mukaibo et al. 2026, in vitro, n=244 CAD-CAM PEEK specimens): Polyether ether ketone (PEEK) is chemically inert — it lacks reactive functional groups, and untreated PEEK bonded with a dual-cure MMA cement fully debonded after 20,000 thermocycles. Stacking two surface treatments (alumina airborne-particle abrasion first, then non-thermal atmospheric plasma immediately before cementation) is the only protocol that reliably survives aging for both MMA-based luting systems tested.

2. **Nanoceramic CAD-CAM fabrication route** (Omar et al. 2026, in vitro, n=20 implant crowns): A novel hybrid nanoceramic material family (Nanoksa — nano-zirconia + nanocarbon-reinforced polymer matrix) is available in both milled (G-Plus discs) and 3D-printable (BioGuard resin) forms using identical digital design. Milled crowns showed marginal gaps nearly half the size of 3D-printed crowns at every measurement point, both before and after thermomechanical aging, with p<0.001 throughout.

3. **Cavity liner under posterior composite** (Schenkel & Veitz-Keenan 2019, Cochrane SR+MA, 8 RCTs, >700 participants): The systematic review that sets the evidence ceiling for the routine liner-under-composite question. Postoperative hypersensitivity reduction was inconsistent across trials and time points; restoration longevity at 1–2 years was identical regardless of liner placement. Authors' conclusion: "inconsistent, low-quality evidence" — routine lining is not justified.

Together, these three axes illustrate a broader principle in restorative material handling: the rationale for an extra procedural step (plasma pre-treatment, additive printing, cavity lining) must be verified against aging/durability outcomes, not just baseline bench data or historical habit.

## Axis 1 — PEEK Bonding: Airborne Abrasion + Plasma Pre-treatment

### Background

Poly-ether-ether-ketone (PEEK) has emerged as a high-performance CAD-CAM framework and prosthetic material — radiolucent, low elastic modulus near dentin, and biocompatible. Its chemical inertness that makes it tissue-friendly is precisely what makes bonding luting cements to it difficult. Debonding of PEEK crowns and bridges is a recognized clinical failure mode.

The MMA-based luting chemistry tested by Mukaibo et al. sits inside a broader functional-monomer literature. Delgado et al. (2025) map the physico-chemical basis of the acidic adhesive monomers that drive chemical adhesion (10-MDP, GPDM, 4-META), showing that spacer length, acidic-group type, and hydrophobicity — not marketing — determine bond durability; the 4-META/MMA-TBB chemistry underlying the Super-Bond family used here resists acid-base inhibition of borate (TBB) initiators, but forms a more soluble, less hydrolytically stable calcium salt than 10-MDP, which explains why the aging-durability gap in Mukaibo's data matters clinically ([[resin-bonding/delgado-2025-functional-monomers-dental-adhesive-technology]]). Kodaira et al. (2022) reviewed the analogous bonding problem on another chemically resistant substrate, titanium, and found that carboxyl/phosphate-group monomers such as MDP and 4-META chemically engage the passive TiO₂ oxide film as durably as tribochemical silica coating, but only when paired with alumina air-abrasion pretreatment — the same mechanical-plus-chemical synergy that AB+PT delivers for PEEK in Axis 1 ([[resin-bonding/kodaira-2022-resin-bonding-titanium-adhesive-monomers-review]]).

### Key findings (Mukaibo et al. 2026)

Two MMA-based luting systems were tested across 4 surface-treatment groups with initial and thermocycle-aged (20,000 cycles, 5–55 °C) shear bond strength (SBS):

| Pre-treatment | Description | Beautylink (dual-cure) | Super-Bond (self-cure) |
|---|---|---|---|
| UT — untreated | No treatment | **Fully debonded after aging** | Significantly lower SBS after aging |
| PT — plasma only | NTAP, no abrasion | Improved wettability; SBS not consistently durable | PT alone fails to match AB after aging |
| AB — abrasion only | 50 µm Al₂O₃; no plasma | Roughness ↑, some durability | AB is the dominant durability driver for SB |
| AB+PT — combined | Abrasion first, then plasma | Highest SBS after aging; superior to either alone | AB+PT ≥ AB; best overall durability |

**Mechanism**: AB supplies micromechanical interlocking (surface roughness increase, residual alumina); NTAP plasma adds surface energy and polar functional groups (–C=O, –COOH, –OH) that improve primer wettability. Critically, plasma does not change the roughness already produced by abrasion — the two mechanisms are additive, not redundant.

**Durability trap**: plasma-induced surface energy is transient — it decays with time on the bench. Plasma treatment applied earlier in the workflow (during try-in) and delayed cementation results in loss of the wettability benefit. **Clinical rule: perform plasma chairside, immediately before applying primer and cement.**

### Clinical decision tree for PEEK luting

```
PEEK restoration ready to cement
       │
       ▼
  Step 1: Airborne-particle abrasion
  (50 µm Al₂O₃, ~10–15 s)
       │
       ▼
  Step 2: NTAP plasma treatment
  (chairside, immediately before luting — do NOT plasma earlier in the workflow)
       │
       ▼
  Step 3: MMA-based luting cement
  (Super-Bond self-cure OR Beautylink dual-cure + MMA-containing primer)
       │
       ▼
  Durable bond after thermocycle aging ✓
```

Do NOT rely on plasma alone (surface energy decays; AB provides the stable roughness substrate). Do NOT cement untreated PEEK with a composite-based cement — high debonding risk after aging.

## Axis 2 — Nanoceramic Implant Crowns: Milling vs 3D Printing

### Background

Hybrid nanoceramic CAD-CAM materials combine ceramic reinforcement particles in a polymer matrix, offering a midpoint between full ceramic (high stiffness, brittle) and pure polymer (low modulus, wear-prone). The Nanoksa material family (nano-zirconia + nanocarbon in a polymer matrix) is now available in both milled disc and 3D-printable resin formats, enabling direct fabrication-method comparison within the same material chemistry — which prior studies could not do (they always compared different material chemistries across routes).

Two further sources reinforce why the fabrication route matters clinically. El-Shazly et al. (2026), an in-vitro micro-shear bond strength study of a different 3D-printed hybrid ceramic (Saremc Print Crowntec), found uniformly low resin-cement bond strengths (2.12–2.70 MPa) even with the best surface treatment tested (50 µm sandblasting + non-thermal atmospheric plasma), underscoring that 3D-printed hybrid materials carry adhesion challenges on top of the marginal-gap deficit Omar documented for the milling-vs-printing comparison ([[dental-materials/el-shazly-2026-3d-printed-hybrid-plasma-bonding]]). Ali et al. (2023), an SR+MA of CAD/CAM restoration failure, found fracture (55% of failures) and debonding/detachment (20%) as the dominant clinical failure modes, with occlusal thickness <1 mm and adhesive-protocol deviations as the leading risk drivers — mechanistically tying the cement-layer degradation and marginal-gap widening Omar measured after aging to the debonding failure pathway seen at the population level ([[dental-materials/ali-2023-cadcam-restoration-failure-reasons-sr-ma]]).

### Key findings (Omar et al. 2026)

| Measurement point | Milled (Nanoksa G-Plus) | 3D-printed (Nanoksa BioGuard) | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before cementation | 34.08 ± 0.85 µm | 59.71 ± 0.79 µm | <0.001 |
| After thermomechanical aging | 37.76 ± 0.64 µm | 63.83 ± 0.57 µm | <0.001 |
| Gap increase with aging | +3.67 µm (p<0.001) | +4.12 µm (p<0.001) | — |
| Within 120 µm threshold? | Yes | Yes | — |

**Interpretation**: Milled crowns achieved approximately half the marginal gap of 3D-printed crowns — a difference large enough to be clinically meaningful (~26 µm at baseline) even though both groups remained under the 120 µm acceptability benchmark (McLean & von Fraunhofer criterion). The aging effect (gap widening) was similar in magnitude for both groups, consistent with cement layer degradation rather than material-specific fatigue.

**Why 3D printing has larger gaps**: Additive manufacturing builds layer-by-layer; marginal accuracy depends on layer resolution, post-cure shrinkage, and surface finish of the printed part. Subtractive milling removes material from a pre-polymerized block, producing smoother margins with dimensional accuracy constrained by the milling bur diameter rather than layer thickness.

### Fabrication selection guidance

| Clinical scenario | Recommended route | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Esthetic zone implant crown, fit precision critical | **Milling** | ~34 µm gap vs ~60 µm; smaller cement reservoir → less marginal staining, less cement dissolution |
| High-volume lab, rapid turnaround, marginal fit secondary | 3D printing acceptable | Stays within 120 µm; material chemistry available in printable form |
| Anterior span with complex geometry | Milling preferred | Subtractive precision on complex curves |
| Research / iterative prototyping | 3D printing efficient | Rapid iteration without disc cost |

**Note**: this study used one brand (Nanoksa) in one lab. The gap magnitude hierarchy (milling superior) is likely generalizable across nanoceramic materials, but the absolute values are material- and workflow-specific.

## Axis 3 — Cavity Liner Under Posterior Composite: Evidence Ceiling

### Background

Cavity liners (glass ionomer, resin-modified glass ionomer, calcium hydroxide, flowable composite) placed under Class I/II posterior resin-based composite (RBC) have been used since the 19th century on rationales of pulp protection, thermal insulation, stress relief, and fluoride release. The Cochrane review by Schenkel & Veitz-Keenan (2019, updated from 2016) is the highest-tier available synthesis, pooling 8 RCTs in permanent teeth.

The biological and clinical-application literature explains why the liner habit persisted despite the weak longevity/sensitivity evidence found here. Singh et al. (2024), a systematic review of 9 studies, found conventional GIC to be less cytotoxic than RMGIC across cell-viability, odontoblastic-change, inflammatory-response, and tertiary-dentin-formation endpoints — a cellular-level biocompatibility signal that plausibly (but, per the Cochrane data below, not sufficiently) supports lining near the pulp ([[glass-ionomer/singh-2024-gic-rmgic-biological-response-sr]]). Ge et al. (2024) reviewed GIC's broader clinical-application evidence and concluded that restorative-cement, luting-cement, and sealant uses are well supported, while explicitly flagging the liner/base indication as remaining "debatable" even before the Cochrane synthesis is applied — foreshadowing this axis's conclusion that cellular biocompatibility does not, by itself, establish a clinical benefit for routine liner placement ([[glass-ionomer/ge-2024-gic-clinical-application-restorative-preventive]]).

### Key findings (Schenkel & Veitz-Keenan 2019)

| Outcome | Trials (n) | Result | GRADE certainty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Postoperative hypersensitivity | 7 | Inconsistent — benefit at some time points but not others; no consistent direction | Low |
| Restoration failure @ 1 year | 4 | No difference; 3/4 trials had zero failures both arms; 4th RR 1.00 (95% CI 0.07–15.00) | Low |
| Restoration failure @ 2 years | 3 | No failures in either group across all trials | Low |
| Adverse events | 8 | None in any trial | — |

Authors' verbatim conclusion: "There is inconsistent, low-quality evidence regarding the difference in postoperative hypersensitivity… Furthermore, no evidence was found to demonstrate a difference in the longevity of restorations placed with or without dental cavity liners."

### Clinical implication: redefine the liner indication

The Cochrane SR does not say liners are harmful — it says routine placement is not supported by evidence of benefit. The correct clinical frame shifts from "liner is the default" to "liner requires a case-specific justification":

| Indication | Liner justified? | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Deep cavity approaching pulp | Yes | Dentin sealing, thermal buffer near pulp — case-specific protection rationale remains |
| Blocking out undercuts for composite | Yes | Purely mechanical — not about sensitivity/longevity |
| Dentin sealing under indirect restoration | Contextual | Depends on adhesive system and cementation protocol |
| Routine shallow-to-moderate posterior composite | **No** | No evidence of sensitivity reduction or longevity benefit vs no liner |
| All posterior composites as default | **No** | Routine use unsupported by high-certainty evidence |

**Ionosit-type RMGI liner-base products** (e.g., DMG Ionosit-Baseliner) retain legitimate indications for the case-specific reasons above — the Cochrane SR does not eliminate them. It eliminates the rationale that lining uniformly reduces postoperative sensitivity or makes restorations last longer.

## Cross-axis Synthesis

These three axes share a structural pattern: each involves a procedural step or product whose historical or theoretical rationale has been refined by direct experimental or systematic evidence:

| Axis | Traditional rationale | Evidence update | Revised protocol |
|---|---|---|---|
| PEEK pre-treatment | Plasma alone improves wettability, therefore bonds better | Plasma-only is not durable after aging; AB is the mechanical foundation | AB first → plasma chairside → MMA cement |
| Nanoceramic fabrication | 3D printing enables the same precision as milling with more flexibility | Milling produces ~half the marginal gap within the same material family | Prefer milling when fit precision is the primary criterion |
| Cavity liner | Liner protects pulp and reduces sensitivity under composite | No consistent benefit for sensitivity or longevity across 8 RCTs | Reserve liner for case-specific indications; abandon routine placement |

The common thread is that **bench plausibility does not equal clinical durability**: plasma-induced surface energy decays (plausible → not durable alone), 3D printing is flexible but less precise than milling (available → suboptimal marginal fit), and liner materials are biocompatible but don't translate to measurable clinical benefit in the composite-under-liner context.

## 한국어 핵심요약

> [!summary] 한국어 핵심요약
> - **핵심 명제**: 전치부 협측판(buccal plate)은 생물학적으로 발치 후 소실이 불가피하며, 두께가 충분한 경우는 처음부터 극소수다 — 임상가는 "어떻게 지킬까"가 아니라 "이미 없다는 전제로 어떻게 계획할까"를 출발점으로 삼아야 한다.
> - **협측판 두께의 해부학적 실태**: 상악 전치부에서 대부분 위치가 협측벽 ≤1 mm이고 약 50%는 ≤0.5 mm (Januário 2011, CBCT n=250). 발치 직접계측에서 전치부 87%가 ≤1 mm·평균 0.8 mm·≥2 mm는 3%뿐 (Huynh-Ba 2010, n=93). micro-CT에서 0–9 mm 전 구간 협측이 거의 항상 <0.5 mm·bundle bone만·1/3에서 fenestration (Todorovic 2023, n=72 두개골). **≥2 mm 협측판 전제는 전치부에서 해부학적으로 성립하지 않는다.**
> - **협측판이 왜 이렇게 얇은가 — 생물학적 이유**: 협측 치조정(alveolar crest)은 다발골(bundle bone)만으로 구성되며, 이 골은 치주인대(periodontal ligament, PDL)의 혈관에 의존한다. 발치 시 PDL이 소멸하면 bundle bone은 생물학적으로 흡수가 예정된 조직이다 (Araújo & Lindhe 2005). 파골세포(osteoclast)가 이 흡수의 중심 effector이며, 치조골은 배아세포 기원·골화 형태·PDL 존재에서 일반 골격골과 구별된다 (Omi & Mishina 2022).
> - **발치 후 흡수의 2단계 기전 (Araújo & Lindhe 2005)**: Phase 1 — PDL 소실로 bundle bone 급속 흡수 (협측 치조정의 유일 구성요소이므로 협측이 훨씬 심하게 소실). Phase 2 — 파골세포 외표면 흡수(협측·설측 모두). **즉시식립 단독으로도 이 흡수를 막지 못한다.**
> - **발치 후 소켓 치유 timeline (Cardaropoli 2003)**: D1–3 혈병 → D7 육아조직(provisional connective tissue, PCT) → D14 신생골 → D30 광화 정점 88% → D180 골수 85%. ARP·GBR·즉시식립 타이밍 결정의 생물학적 기준선.
> - **인체 발치와 치유 조직학 (Trombelli 2008)**: 발치 2–4주: 육아조직 지배 → 4–6주: 잠정기질+직조골 → 6–8주: 조골세포 밀도 정점 → 전 시점에서 파골세포 지속 존재. 흡수와 형성이 동시에 진행됨을 인체 in-vivo로 확인.
> - **협측벽 두께가 발치 후 골소실의 핵심 예측인자 (Chappuis 2013)**: 박벽형(≤1 mm) 발치 후 8주 수직골 소실 **중앙값 7.5 mm** vs 후벽형 1.1 mm (~3.5배). 협측 중앙부위가 가장 심각. **≤1 mm 박벽형이면 전치부 IIP를 재고하고 ARP+지연식립을 검토해야 한다.**
> - **연조직이 골소실을 가린다 — 임상 함정 (Chappuis 2015)**: 발치 시 연조직 두께는 박벽/후벽형 동일 (0.7 vs 0.8 mm). 그러나 8주 후 박벽형에서만 연조직이 **약 7배 두꺼워져** 7.5 mm 골소실을 1.6 mm의 연조직 소실로 위장한다. 변화의 51% 이상이 **발치 후 2주 이내** 발생. 연조직 윤곽이 양호해 보여도 골부족을 배제할 수 없다.
> - **구개측(palatal)은 다르다**: 협측과 달리 구개측 골은 치조정 <1 mm에서 시작해 9 mm 수준에서 중앙치 3.77 mm / 측절치 4.56 mm / 견치 5.43 mm로 두꺼워진다 (Todorovic 2023). 즉시식립 시 구개측 bone engagement가 1차 안정성의 실제 공급원.
> - **임상 takeaway 3줄**: ① 발치 전 CBCT로 협측벽 두께를 반드시 계측 — ≤1 mm이면 즉시식립 심미 위험 최고등급. ② 발치 후 연조직 평가 단독으로 골상태 판단 금지 — 연조직 이상이 없어도 이미 7 mm 이상 골소실이 진행 중일 수 있다. ③ 구개측 골에 의존한 깊고 팔라탈한 식립 위치 선정이 안정성과 심미 모두의 기반.

## Three-line Summary

Synthesis of 12 papers spanning buccal plate morphometry (Januário 2011, Huynh-Ba 2010, Altarawneh 2018, Todorovic 2023), extraction socket healing biology (Araújo & Lindhe 2005, Cardaropoli 2003, Trombelli 2008, Omi & Mishina 2022), post-extraction esthetic outcomes (Chappuis 2013, 2015, 2017), and buccal-gap management (Greenstein 2013): the anterior maxillary buccal plate is almost always ≤1 mm thick and composed entirely of bundle bone — a tissue structurally condemned to resorb when the periodonal ligament is lost, with osteoclasts as the central effector of an alveolar bone whose biology is distinct from the rest of the skeleton.

The downstream consequence is quantified by Chappuis 2013: thin-wall (≤1 mm) sites lose a median 7.5 mm of vertical bone by 8 weeks vs 1.1 mm for thick-wall sites (~3.5×), and this massive bone loss is masked by spontaneous soft-tissue thickening (~7-fold in thin-phenotype sites) that makes the ridge look clinically acceptable while concealing the deficit — more than 51% of the dimensional change occurs within 2 weeks (Chappuis 2015).

The palatal wall is the structural opposite and the actual source of primary stability: <1 mm at the crest but rising to 3.77–5.43 mm at 9 mm depth (Todorovic 2023), which is precisely why deep palatal implant placement is both anatomically safer and mechanically sounder than following the native tooth trajectory.

## 세줄요약

## Summary

This overview synthesizes twelve papers that, taken together, explain *why* the anterior maxillary buccal plate fails so consistently after extraction and what that failure looks like clinically. The papers cluster into three mutually reinforcing axes: the morphometric reality of how thin the buccal wall is before any intervention, the biological machinery that drives its loss, and the clinical evidence quantifying the consequences — including a soft-tissue masking effect that makes the outcome look better than it is.

### Axis 1 — The buccal plate is nearly never adequate to begin with

Three independent datasets converge on the same finding through different methods and different populations. Januário 2011 used CBCT to survey 250 intact anterior dentitions and found the facial bone wall ≤1 mm at most measured locations in every tooth site, with close to 50% of sites ≤0.5 mm — a population-level statement about how often an anterior extraction site starts below any protective threshold. Huynh-Ba 2010 moved the measurement to the moment of extraction, recording 93 esthetic-zone sockets intraoperatively and finding the anterior buccal wall averaged 0.8 mm with 87% ≤1 mm and only 3% reaching the ≥2 mm criterion that is routinely cited as adequate. Todorovic 2023 applied micro-CT to 72 dry skulls and found the buccal bone rarely exceeded 0.5 mm at any apicocoronal level from the crest down to 9 mm, consisted entirely of bundle bone throughout that corridor, and showed fenestrations in up to one-third of teeth — while the palatal wall increased from <1 mm at the crest to 3.77 mm (central incisor), 4.56 mm (lateral incisor), and 5.43 mm (canine) at 9 mm. Together these three establish that the ≥2 mm anterior buccal plate is not a baseline the clinician should expect to find: it is the exception, not the norm, and treatment planning must start from that premise.

The Todorovic palatal data also carries a positive implication: the palatal wall has the bone that can actually engage an immediately placed implant, which is the anatomical basis for the universal recommendation to place anteriorly angled implants with a palatal offset and at depth.

### Axis 2 — The biology explains why the buccal plate is both thin and doomed

The thinness is not random — it has a structural cause. Araújo & Lindhe 2005 demonstrated in a serial-sacrifice dog model that the buccal alveolar crest is composed exclusively of bundle bone, a tissue that is vascularized and maintained by the periodontal ligament. When the tooth is removed and the PDL is lost, bundle bone loses its blood supply and undergoes rapid resorption (Phase 1), followed by slower external-surface osteoclastic resorption on both buccal and lingual plates (Phase 2). Because the lingual crest also contains lamellar bone, it survives Phase 1; the buccal crest does not. Immediate implant placement alone does not prevent this sequence — the buccal bundle bone is gone regardless of what is placed in the socket.

Omi & Mishina 2022 supply the cellular mechanism: the osteoclast is the central effector of alveolar bone remodeling, and the alveolar bone differs from the rest of the skeleton in its embryonic cellular origin, its mode of ossification, and the presence of teeth and periodontal tissues. These distinctions mean that remodeling principles from general bone biology cannot be directly imported into the alveolar context — and that excessive osteoclastic resorption, the proximate cause of what Araújo & Lindhe described histologically, is also the mechanism behind periodontitis and other jaw bone diseases.

Cardaropoli 2003 and Trombelli 2008 complete the picture with the healing side. Cardaropoli's nine-timepoint dog model established the canonical timeline: blood clot (days 1–3), provisional connective tissue (day 7), woven bone emergence (day 14), peak mineralization of 88% (day 30), and marrow replacement at 85% by day 180. Trombelli's human biopsy series confirmed the same sequence in living patients, with granulation tissue dominating weeks 2–4, provisional matrix and woven bone appearing at weeks 4–6, osteoblast density peaking at weeks 6–8, and a consistent small osteoclast presence at every timepoint demonstrating that remodeling runs in parallel with formation throughout. The socket is never simply "healing" — it is simultaneously forming and resorbing, and the buccal wall has already lost the structure that would have kept it intact.

### Axis 3 — The clinical consequence: massive bone loss, masked by soft tissue

Chappuis 2013 is the paper that converts the biology into a planning number. In a prospective CBCT study of 39 esthetic-zone extraction sites stratified by buccal bone phenotype, thin-wall sites (≤1 mm) lost a median 7.5 mm of vertical bone at the central buccal measurement point by 8 weeks, versus 1.1 mm for thick-wall sites — approximately a 3.5-fold difference. The central buccal site was the most severely affected location. This is not a marginal clinical effect; 7.5 mm of vertical bone loss in 8 weeks is the structural collapse of the esthetic zone.

Chappuis 2015 adds a finding that is, if anything, more clinically dangerous: the soft tissue does not tell the truth. In the same study design with sequential 3D digital surface model superimposition, both thin-wall and thick-wall phenotypes started with essentially identical soft tissue thickness (0.7 vs 0.8 mm). By 8 weeks, thin-wall sites had thickened spontaneously to roughly seven times their baseline — masking 7.5 mm of underlying bone resorption behind a measured vertical soft tissue loss of only 1.6 mm. The result was no significant difference in total tissue loss between phenotypes at any timepoint from 2 to 8 weeks, and no visible clinical signal that separates the high-risk from the low-risk case. More than 51% of this dimensional alteration occurred within the first 2 weeks. The implication is direct: a clinically unremarkable mucosal contour after extraction is not evidence of adequate bone, and cannot substitute for preoperative bone assessment.

## Cross-Paper Synthesis

**The convergence of morphometry and biology.** Januário and Huynh-Ba establish the starting condition (the buccal wall is already below any protective threshold in the vast majority of anterior sites). Todorovic explains why: it is composed entirely of bundle bone, the same tissue Araújo & Lindhe identified as the structurally condemned component of the buccal crest. Omi & Mishina supply the cellular effector — the osteoclast operating in an alveolar microenvironment that is biologically unlike other skeletal sites. These four papers together explain why the loss is not a complication to be prevented case-by-case but a predictable biological outcome operating from a nearly universal starting point.

**The timeline papers (Cardaropoli, Trombelli) define the treatment window.** The canonical healing sequence, confirmed in both the animal model and in human biopsies, shows that the critical bone-formation window peaks at weeks 2–4 and is substantially complete by 6–8 weeks. This is the biological context for Chappuis 2015's observation that >51% of dimensional changes occur within the first 2 weeks: the bone resorption and the soft-tissue response that masks it are both early, fast, and clinically invisible without active 3D imaging.

**Chappuis 2015 reframes clinical assessment.** The conventional examination — inspecting the mucosal contour after healing — is biased by a tissue response that specifically compensates for the worst bone loss phenotype. A thin-wall site that has lost 7.5 mm of bone looks, on a mucosal inspection 8 weeks later, approximately as good as a thick-wall site that lost 1.1 mm. Bone assessment (preoperative CBCT or intraoperative measurement) is not a convenience — it is the only clinically reliable measure.

**Palatal wall as the planning pivot.** Todorovic's finding that the palatal wall rises to 3.77–5.43 mm at 9 mm depth, while the buccal wall stays below 0.5 mm throughout, directly explains why deep palatal placement is the standard recommendation for immediate anterior implants: it is not merely a safety rule for avoiding labial perforation, but a positive statement about where the bone actually exists in this anatomy.

## Clinical Rules (derived from this evidence set)

1. **Assess before, not after.** Preoperative CBCT morphometry of the buccal plate thickness is the gate. A wall ≤1 mm identifies the high-risk phenotype; ~50% of anterior sites will be ≤0.5 mm.
2. **Do not trust the soft tissue after extraction.** Thin-phenotype sites thicken spontaneously to mask their bone loss. Mucosal appearance at 6–8 weeks does not exclude 7+ mm of vertical bone deficit.
3. **Act in the first 2 weeks or plan accordingly.** More than half of dimensional change occurs before week 2. If augmentation or socket preservation is planned, the biological window is very early.
4. **Anchor in palatal bone.** The palatal wall has the volume; the buccal wall does not. Deep palatal placement engages the bone that exists. Gap grafting addresses the buccal void on a dimensional, not stability, axis.
5. **Immediate implant placement does not rescue buccal bone.** Araújo & Lindhe 2005 established this; Chappuis 2013 confirmed it clinically. The buccal bundle bone resorbs on its own biological schedule regardless of what is placed in the socket.

## Evidence Gaps

- All three morphometric datasets are preoperative or cadaveric — no post-extraction anatomy study compares the wall thickness before and after at the same site (the consequence is inferred from Chappuis 2013's phenotype comparison).
- Chappuis 2015 did not report the sample size in the abstract; the full text is not held. The scale of the masking effect is well-supported in direction, but precision is limited.
- Todorovic 2023 is cadaveric; dry skull morphometry systematically reads thinner than in-vivo CBCT for soft-tissue-bound measurements. The direction is confirmed across methods; the exact numbers are not directly transferable to clinical planning.
- Omi & Mishina 2022 is a narrative review without quantitative synthesis; the osteoclast-as-effector framework is the canonical understanding but is not derived from a systematic search or primary data here.
- No Korean-population-specific morphometric dataset is held for the anterior maxilla.

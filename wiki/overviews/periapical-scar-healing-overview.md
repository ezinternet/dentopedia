## Three-line Summary

Periapical scar (PS) is a benign fibrous healing variant after endodontic treatment — forming dense collagenous tissue instead of alveolar bone — with an incidence of ~9% in surgical series (Grung 1990, n=477) and 1.6–12% in biopsy-based reports; it presents as a persistent well-defined periapical radiolucency indistinguishable from treatment failure on routine radiographs, and is currently identified definitively only by histopathology.

Clinical diagnosis without surgery is feasible when five criteria are simultaneously met (Lee 2021): adequate obturation, no symptoms/signs, stable well-defined radiolucency, no root fracture, healthy periodontium — all five together warrant conservative monitoring rather than retreatment or surgery.

The core surveillance rule: a true PS never enlarges; any size increase on serial radiographs invalidates the diagnosis and mandates reassessment.

## 1. What Is Periapical Scar?

Periapical scar (PS) is one of six recognized causes of persistent post-treatment apical periodontitis (Nair 2006). Unlike the other five causes — which represent disease (intraradicular biofilm, extraradicular infection, foreign body, cholesterol crystals) or structural pathology (true cyst) — PS represents **successful alternative healing**. The periapical inflammatory lesion resolves, but the periosteum/endosteum cannot regenerate the alveolar bone defect. Dense fibrous collagenous connective tissue fills the space instead.

The result is radiographically identical to treatment failure: a persistent periapical radiolucency. Histopathologically, it is unambiguously benign: fibrous collagenous tissue with absent or minimal chronic inflammation (Lee 2021).

**Why it matters clinically:** PS is systematically misdiagnosed as failure. In Lee 2021, 7/7 histopathology-confirmed PS cases underwent periapical surgery — 5 diagnosed pre-operatively as radicular cyst, 2 as granuloma, 0 as PS. The surgery was unnecessary in every case.

## 2. Epidemiology

| Source | Denominator | PS Incidence | Setting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grung 1990 | 477 periapically operated teeth | **9%** | Surgical follow-up (radiographic) |
| Lee 2021 | 445 periapical lesion biopsies | **1.6%** | Biopsy series (histopathological) |
| Kasperek 2023 (citing literature) | — | **2.5–12%** | Range across surgical + nonsurgical series |

**Denominator matters:** Grung's 9% counts PS among teeth that underwent surgery. Lee's 1.6% counts PS among all periapical lesion biopsies (dominated by granulomas 44.3% and radicular cysts 53.7%). These are not contradictory — they measure different things.

**Practical implication:** In a population of patients with persistent post-treatment radiolucency, ~1 in 10 may have PS rather than persistent disease. This is a clinically non-trivial rate that justifies systematic consideration before retreatment or surgery.

## 3. Pathophysiology and Risk Factors

The mechanism of fibrous scar formation is incompletely understood but converges on a **cellular competition model** (Nair 2006, Lee 2021):

> *Connective tissue-forming cells colonize the periapical wound before the cells responsible for generating bone regenerate the structural components of the apical periodontium.*

This colonization priority is shaped by:

| Risk Factor | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Loss of both cortical plates** (facial + lingual) | Removes periosteum/endosteum — the osteoblast reservoir. Without adjacent bone-forming cells, only fibroblasts can populate the wound. |
| **Large preoperative lesion** | Greater osseous defect → higher scar probability (Grung 1990: scar rate strongly influenced by large lesion size) |
| **Prior periapical surgery** | Surgical trauma damages or removes periosteal tissue; wound healing then proceeds via fibroblast-dominant rather than osteoblast-dominant pathway |
| **Systemic conditions (CCD, RUNX2 mutation)** | Impaired bone morphogenetic protein signaling → reduced osteoblast differentiation capacity; combined with the surgical management of CCD-associated supernumerary teeth, creates a maximally scar-prone environment (Kasperek 2023) |

**Molecular layer (Lee 2021 hypothesis, unvalidated):**
- Excess type III collagen production, inadequately replaced by type I collagen
- MMP (Matrix Metalloproteinase) deficiency → extracellular matrix not remodeled
- TGF-β over-production by macrophages → myofibroblast over-activation → excess collagenous protein

## 4. Clinical and Radiographic Presentation

**Consistent features across all reports:**

| Feature | Finding |
|---|---|
| Radiographic appearance | Persistent, well-defined periapical radiolucency — 100% of cases |
| Symptomatic | 14–22% mild pain/swelling (most series report 78–86% asymptomatic) |
| Lesion size | 3–10 mm greatest dimension (mean 5.6 mm, Lee 2021) |
| Duration | Long — detectable for months to years after treatment completion |
| Clinical exam | Healthy periodontium, no sinus tract, no swelling (in quiescent cases) |
| Root fracture | Absent |

**Histopathological hallmarks (Lee 2021):**
- Dense fibrous collagenous tissue: defining feature
- Inflammation: absent or focal minimal chronic inflammation only
- Hyalinization: in long-standing lesions
- Foreign body inclusions: possible (amalgam, gutta-percha) if prior retrograde filling

---

## 5. Differential Diagnosis

PS must be distinguished from active periapical disease entities that share the radiographic presentation:

| Entity | Key differentiator from PS |
|---|---|
| Periapical granuloma | Active inflammation → may shrink with retreatment; PS will not change with retreatment |
| Radicular cyst (pocket/bay type) | May heal with NSRCT; large volume (>247 mm³) on CBCT favors cyst (Pitcher 2017) |
| True (enclosed-lumen) cyst | Self-sustaining; does not resolve with orthograde retreatment; requires surgery |
| Persistent intraradicular infection | Retreatment reduces/resolves the radiolucency; PS will not respond |
| Malignancy | Progressive growth; cortical destruction; biopsy mandatory if any doubt |

**What imaging cannot do:**
- CBCT grey density cannot differentiate PS from other entities (AUC 0.44, AlMadi 2020)
- CBCT texture analysis shows promise for cyst vs. granuloma (AUC 81.2%, De Rosa 2020) but PS was not included in those studies
- CBCT volume >247 mm³ raises cyst probability to 80% but cannot confirm PS

**Current gold standard:** Histopathology of surgically removed tissue remains the only definitive diagnosis. The clinical criteria below are a validated surrogate for avoiding unnecessary surgery.

## 6. Clinical Diagnosis Without Histopathology — The Five Criteria

Lee 2021 proposes five criteria that, when **all simultaneously present**, support PS as the working diagnosis and conservative monitoring as the management strategy:

| # | Criterion | Clinical assessment |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Adequate root canal filling** | Radiographic: obturation to within 0–2 mm of radiographic apex; no voids; no short fill |
| 2 | **Asymptomatic; no clinical signs** | No pain, swelling, sinus tract, or tenderness to palpation/percussion |
| 3 | **No root fracture; healthy periodontium** | Probing depths normal; no mobility beyond physiological; fracture excluded clinically/radiographically |
| 4 | **Stable, well-defined radiolucency** | Size unchanged on serial radiographs over long follow-up (months to years) |
| 5 | **Adequate prior periapical surgery** *(if applicable)* | Retrograde filling is properly placed and sealed |

**When all 5 criteria met:** PS is the most parsimonious diagnosis. Conservative long-term monitoring is the appropriate strategy.

**When criteria are not all met:** A specific unmet criterion guides the diagnostic workup — inadequate obturation → retreatment first; size increasing → suspect cyst or infection; symptoms → suspect reinfection.

> **Reinfection caveat (Lee 2021):** A quiescent PS can acquire reinfection from intraradicular or extraradicular bacterial sources. If PS was previously established as the diagnosis but new symptoms develop, do not automatically maintain the scar diagnosis — reassess for intraradicular failure, coronal leakage, or new periapical infection.

## 7. Surveillance Protocol

Once PS is diagnosed clinically (or confirmed histopathologically):

**Active treatment:** None. PS is biologically inert — collagenous fibrous tissue with no pathological activity.

**Radiographic monitoring:**

| Interval | Recommended action |
|---|---|
| 6–12 months post-diagnosis | Periapical radiograph to establish baseline for serial comparison |
| Annual (or biannual) | Serial periapical radiograph — same angulation for valid size comparison |
| Any appointment if symptoms develop | Periapical radiograph + clinical re-examination |

**Trigger for reassessment:**

- **Any increase in radiolucency size** → PS invalidated; biopsy or retreatment depending on other features
- **New pain, swelling, sinus tract** → suspect bacterial reinfection; reassess obturation and coronal seal
- **Cortical plate expansion** or **changed radiographic character** → rule out cyst, malignancy; biopsy indicated

**The core surveillance rule:** A true PS never enlarges. If the lesion grows, it is not (or is no longer) a scar.

## 8. Special Scenario: Systemic Conditions and Surgical History

Kasperek 2023 highlights that **systemic conditions affecting bone healing + a history of extensive periapical surgery** create a maximally scar-prone scenario.

**Cleidocranial Dysplasia (CCD)** example:
- RUNX2 gene mutation → impaired osteoblast differentiation + hypoplastic clavicles + multiple supernumerary teeth
- Supernumerary tooth management → repeated childhood periapical surgery → periosteal disruption
- Result: fibrous scar in the periapical region of permanent teeth that is then misdiagnosed as failure in adulthood

**Generalizable principle:** When taking a dental history, explicitly ask about:
1. Prior periapical surgery (how many episodes, which teeth, age at time)
2. Childhood dental surgery for any reason (trauma, supernumerary teeth, cysts)
3. Conditions affecting bone healing (bisphosphonates, radiation, metabolic bone disease)

A patient with any of these should have PS higher on the differential diagnosis list for a persistent post-treatment radiolucency.

## 9. Evidence Gaps

| Gap | Current state | Needed |
|---|---|---|
| Non-surgical PS incidence | Grung 1990 covers surgical series only; most post-NSRCT scar estimates are from case series | Prospective cohort with systematic histopathological confirmation |
| CBCT-based PS identification | No study has targeted PS specifically (De Rosa 2020 and AlMadi 2020 compare cyst vs granuloma only) | CBCT texture or volumetric features specific to fibrous scar |
| Natural history without surgery | Diagnosis currently requires surgery in uncertain cases; PS behavior over decades is unknown | Long-term radiographic follow-up cohort with histopathology-confirmed PS cases |
| Mechanism validation | Lee 2021 mechanisms (TGF-β, MMP, collagen III/I ratio) are proposed, not tested in periapical tissue | Molecular biology studies of periapical scar tissue |
| 5-criterion validation | Lee 2021 framework based on retrospective analysis of 7 cases | Prospective validation with larger n |

## Three-line Summary

Cross-sectional vignette study (Chile, 2023–2024, n=431: 47 dental students, 349 general dental practitioners (GDP), 35 endodontists) presenting seven standardised periapical radiographs of teeth with post-treatment apical periodontitis and asking clinicians to choose retreatment, apical surgery/intentional replantation, or extraction.

GDPs and students were significantly more likely than endodontists to choose extraction (OR 4.37 and 4.41, p<0.001 for both); GDPs aged 36–45 (OR 1.99, p=0.003) and those in the public sector (22.96% vs. 15.24% private) extracted more; every tooth-level variable — indirect restoration (GDP OR 2.12, p<0.001), inadequate root filling (GDP OR 1.81, p<0.001), medium (GDP OR 7.26, p<0.001) and large (GDP OR 1.56, p=0.001) lesions — pushed GDPs toward extraction, while no endodontist chose extraction regardless of lesion size.

The design measures stated intent from a radiograph-only vignette, not delivered treatment or outcome, and cannot separate skill/confidence/self-selection from the authors' framing that the endodontist's answer is the "correct" one; the public-sector association nonetheless hints that resource/access constraints, not just knowledge gaps, shape the generalist extraction bias.

## Summary

According to PubMed, this cross-sectional study ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.4317/jced.63471); PMID 42136705; PMCID PMC13170950) asked whether clinicians looking at the *same tooth* reach the same treatment decision. It is not a study of technique quality (that question is answered, for the endodontist-vs-GDP axis specifically, by [[practice-management/nosrat-2025-endodontist-gp-nsrct-clinical-approaches-cohort]]) — it is a study of the decision itself, before any technique is ever applied.

431 Chilean clinicians (47 final-year dental students, 349 general dental practitioners, 35 endodontists) were shown seven standardised periapical radiographs of teeth with post-treatment apical periodontitis — the same fictional 50-year-old patient, positive percussion, no systemic disease — and asked to pick one of three options: root canal retreatment, apical surgery/intentional replantation, or extraction. The radiographs varied only in root canal filling quality, restoration type (direct/indirect), and apical lesion size (small/medium/large); nothing about the patient changed.

The headline finding is not subtle: general dental practitioners and dental students were dramatically more likely than endodontists to choose extraction (OR 4.37 and 4.41 respectively, both p<0.001). Endodontists extracted almost never — as low as 5.3% overall, and **0% regardless of how large the apical lesion was**. Every radiographic feature that made a tooth look worse (indirect restoration, inadequate root filling, bigger lesion) pushed GDPs and students further toward extraction, but left endodontists comparatively unmoved. Add an older GDP (36–45 y, OR 1.99, p=0.003) or a public-sector workplace (22.96% vs. 15.24% extraction in private practice) and the extraction odds climb further. Same tooth, same radiograph — a different fate depending on who is holding it.

## Key Contributions

- **Quantifies clinician-group bias in the decision itself**, not in technique — the axis this wiki had not yet measured. GDP/student vs. endodontist extraction odds: OR 4.37 / 4.41 (both p<0.001); endodontist extraction rate as low as 5.3%, falling to 0% at any lesion size.
- **Isolates which radiographic cues drive the generalist bias**: indirect restoration (GDP OR 2.12, p<0.001), inadequate root canal filling (GDP OR 1.81, p<0.001; student extraction rate 23.4% vs. endodontist 7.43%), medium lesion (GDP OR 7.26, p<0.001), large lesion (GDP OR 1.56, p=0.001).
- **Surfaces a health-systems signal alongside the training-gap story**: public-sector GDPs extracted more (22.96%) than private (15.24%) or mixed-practice (15.95%) GDPs — plausibly reflecting long public-system wait times rather than a knowledge deficit alone. The paper's own "reduce unnecessary extractions" framing assumes the endodontist's answer is correct; the design cannot establish that, only that the answers differ.
- **National baseline for Chile**, filling a documented regional evidence gap and, per the authors, directly relevant to a reported association between endodontic decision errors and dental malpractice claims in the country.

## Methodology

Cross-sectional, PROBE-guideline-adherent, anonymous nationwide survey (Google Forms + in-person forms), July–September 2023, approved by Universidad Andrés Bello (Resolution 163/23). Target population 31,943 (1,600 final-year dental students, 28,495 GDPs, 1,848 endodontists nationally); required minimum n=379 (95% CI, 5% precision), achieved n=431 after excluding 13 incomplete/non-compliant responses (99.7% response rate, all 16 Chilean regions represented).

Each of 11 survey items covered clinician characteristics (professional group, age category, years of experience, workplace: public/private/mixed) plus seven forced-choice, single-answer case items. Every case shared an identical clinical vignette (50-year-old, systemically healthy, pain on biting, previously root-filled tooth, positive percussion, normal soft tissue) paired with a standardised periapical radiograph manipulated in Adobe Photoshop to vary: root canal filling quality (per European Society of Endodontology criteria — adequate/inadequate), restoration type (direct/indirect), and apical lesion size (small <3mm / medium 4–5mm / large >5mm, categories not disclosed to participants). Time elapsed since the original root canal treatment was deliberately withheld from every scenario to avoid anchoring bias — a design choice the authors acknowledge limits realism, since experienced clinicians typically weigh treatment age when judging prognosis.

Responses were dichotomized into "save and treat" (retreatment or apical surgery/intentional replantation) vs. "extract." Primary analysis used a generalized linear mixed-effects logistic regression (random intercept per participant, accounting for the 7 repeated case-ratings per respondent) with professional group plus clinician- and tooth-level covariates as fixed effects; results reported as odds ratios (OR, 95% CI) via Wald tests. Simple logistic regression supplemented group-specific associations. R v4.3.1; two-sided p<0.05.

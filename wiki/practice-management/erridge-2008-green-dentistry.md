## Three-line Summary

2008 British Dental Journal letter (P. Erridge, East Grinstead; DOI 10.1038/sj.bdj.2008.703) questioning whether dentistry has ever been environmentally audited, responding to press criticism over mercury and the pollution impact of the profession.

Contends dental mercury is a minor pollution source relative to volcanic emissions and amalgam use had been declining for two decades; calls for wide-ranging environmental audits (manufacturing to clinic waste, lobby to extracted air) and raises whether treatment planning, training curricula, and publishing should go green — including cost-effectiveness of "being green".

Editor's note: BDJ is printed on certified sustainable source paper; Vital's summer issue had featured the first carbon-neutral dental practice.

## Overview

A readers' letter published in the British Dental Journal (BDJ, 2008;183(7):703) under the title "Green Dentistry". Triggered by a national newspaper item accusing dentistry of being a serious polluter through mercury use, the letter asks whether environmental audits of dentistry have ever been carried out.

## Key Findings

- **Mercury position**: the author argues that, relative to volcanic emissions, manufacture/use/disposal of dental mercury is a minor source of world pollution, and that amalgam use had declined further over the previous two decades (as of 2008).
- **Proposed audit scope**: wide-ranging environmental audits covering dental manufacturing through clinic waste containers, from the clinic front door to extracted air; questions on treatment plans guided by environmental concerns, environmental awareness in dental training, and cost-effectiveness of being "green".
- **Journal-level suggestions**: BDJ printed on recycled paper or online-only; sharing of "greener dentistry" tips through the journal.
- **Editor's response**: BDJ is printed on paper from well-managed sustainable sources conforming to internationally recognised certification bodies; the summer issue of Vital featured the first carbon-neutral dental practice.

## Clinical Implications

1. The letter is a historical (2008) statement of the sustainability agenda in dentistry, valuable as a baseline for modern green dentistry and amalgam phase-down debates — it has no clinical efficacy claims.
2. Practice-management relevance: environmental auditing (waste handling, extraction/DUWL air quality, procurement) was already a standing question for clinics; today's compliance frameworks (mercury separator laws, amalgam phase-down) echo the letter's proposals.
3. For evidence use: treat as opinion/position content (letters to the editor), not as clinical evidence; the quantitative claims cited (volcanic vs dental mercury contribution) are the author's assertions and were not peer-reviewed data.

## Three-line Summary

Narrative review / perspective (BDJ 2025) by the same lead author as Bailey 2021, summarising a decade of patient safety development in dentistry: introduces Safety I vs Safety II (resilience-focused), HALT/IMSAFE human-factors frameworks, and crew resource management (CRM) principles for hierarchy management.

Wrong-tooth extraction was removed from NHS never events list in 2021 (systemic barriers insufficient); checklists alone are ineffective without positive safety culture + team engagement; PSIRF (2023–24) and NHS primary care patient safety strategy (September 2024, explicitly including primary dental care) and Project Sphere mark major policy milestones.

Next decade challenges: dental access crisis (DIY dentistry, oral cancer risk), facial aesthetics expansion, AI/technology integration, overseas registration reform — curriculum and legislative change required; gamified education shown to improve early-career safety knowledge.

## Summary

Bailey and Dungarwalla (2025) mark the ten-year anniversary of their 2014 and 2021 publications on patient safety in dentistry with a reflective update spanning paradigm shifts, policy milestones, and future challenges. The paper's central contribution is the introduction of **Safety II** — a move away from retrospective failure analysis (Safety I) toward understanding why the vast majority of care goes well, and building resilience into systems that are already working. This complements rather than replaces the Swiss cheese model. Human factors frameworks (HALT, IMSAFE) are introduced to help teams recognise when staff may become unsafe, and crew resource management (CRM) principles from aviation — including Korean Air 8509 as an authority-gradient cautionary tale — are applied to dental team hierarchy. Practical mitigation includes induction talks on speaking up, pre-clinic huddles, and visible name/role badges. Policy landmarks include: wrong-tooth extraction removed from NHS never events (2021); PSIRF rolled out 2023–24; NHS primary care patient safety strategy (September 2024) explicitly mentions primary dental care and aims to give dentists access to National Care Records Service; Project Sphere (NHS England + Chief Dental Officer) developing dental-specific safety tools. The paper concludes that patient safety in dentistry is about far more than checklists, and the coming decade will require reform in curriculum, legislation, and technology integration.

## Key Contributions

- Safety I → Safety II paradigm shift in dental patient safety thinking
- HALT / IMSAFE acronyms for recognising staff at risk of becoming unsafe
- Wrong-tooth extraction removed from NHS never events (2021) — systemic barriers insufficient
- PSIRF (2023–24): changes reporting from Datix blame-model to LFPSE (learn from patient safety events)
- NHS primary care patient safety strategy (Sept 2024): first explicit policy inclusion of primary dental care; plans National Care Records Service access for dentists
- Project Sphere: NHS England + Chief Dental Officer dental-specific safety culture initiative, huddle sheets published June 2023
- Freedom-to-speak-up guardians in all NHS organisations; Lucy Letby case highlights failure consequences
- Burnout: 5.6% NHS absence rate 2022; 170,000 staff left/considering leaving; Maslow-based NHS wellbeing framework
- Gamified patient safety education improves early-career dentists' knowledge (Song et al. cited)

## Methodology

Narrative review / perspective. No systematic search; based on authors' 10-year experience in UK secondary care/academic dental setting, with reference to NHS policies and published literature.

## Results

- Checklist use in dentistry confined largely to oral surgery in specialist/academic settings; rarely used in primary care
- Patient safety culture in dentistry lags medical specialties, especially in general dental practice
- Fear of litigation and regulatory action suppresses reporting — "punishment myth"; PSIRF aims to shift to supportive model
- Patient safety incidents could be #3 cause of death in the USA after heart disease and cancer (if fully counted); up to 50% of adverse events preventable

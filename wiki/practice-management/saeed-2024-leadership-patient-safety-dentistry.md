## Three-line Summary

Cross-sectional study at Riphah Dental Hospital, Islamabad, Pakistan (n=104 dentists, response rate 86.7%): measured transformational leadership (TLS, 15 items) and patient safety culture (DMOSOPS, 44 items) in a teaching dental hospital.

Overall leadership correlated moderately with patient safety culture (r=0.455, p<0.001); inspirational communication was strongest predictor (r=0.504); vision dimension showed no significant correlation; teamwork (82.91%) and organizational learning (78.41%) were highest-scoring safety dimensions; work pressure and pace (32.02%) was lowest.

Blame culture persists: only 17.2% felt errors were not held against them; 41.67% afraid to ask questions; 33.33% found it difficult to disagree — punishment myth undermines safety reporting in a dental hospital setting.

## Summary

Saeed et al. (2024) conducted the first study to quantify the relationship between transformational leadership style and patient safety culture specifically in a dental hospital, using validated instruments: the Transformational Leadership Scale (TLS, 5 subdimensions) and the dental-adapted Medical Office Survey on Patient Safety Culture (DMOSOPS, 10 dimensions). At Riphah Dental Hospital, Islamabad (n=104 dentists, 86.7% response), overall leadership correlated moderately with overall patient safety (r=0.455, p<0.001). Among leadership subdimensions, **inspirational communication** (r=0.504) and **supportive leadership** (r=0.373) drove most of the association, while **vision** showed no/very weak correlation with safety outcomes — suggesting that having a long-term practice vision does not itself translate into safer day-to-day patient care. The highest-scoring safety dimensions were teamwork (82.91%) and organizational learning (78.41%), replicating Yansane's (2020) finding that these are dentistry's relative strengths. However, the lowest was work pressure and pace (32.02%), with 19.23% feeling they were "often rushed" — a structural risk factor independent of leadership style. Critically, only 17.2% felt errors were not held against them, and 41.67% were afraid to ask questions — indicators of persisting blame culture that undermine the safety-reporting system. Authors recommend the Co-Lead (Collective Leadership for Safety Cultures) programme as applicable to dental multidisciplinary teams.

## Key Contributions

- First dental-specific quantification of leadership → patient safety culture: r=0.455 (p<0.001)
- Inspirational communication strongest leadership predictor of safety (r=0.504)
- Vision has no significant correlation with safety — leadership vision ≠ safer care
- Teamwork (82.91%) and organizational learning (78.41%) are dental strengths — consistent with Yansane 2020
- Work pressure/pace (32%) is a structural risk regardless of leadership
- Blame culture persists even with supportive leadership scores
- Co-Lead programme as potential dental team-leadership intervention

## Methodology

| Attribute | Detail |
|---|---|
| Design | Hospital-based cross-sectional study |
| Setting | Riphah Dental Hospital (RDH), Islamabad, Pakistan |
| n | 104 dentists (120 eligible; 10 on leave, 6 refused) |
| Response rate | 86.7% |
| Composition | House officers 57.7%, PG residents 24%, demonstrators 6.7%, consultants 11.6% |
| Leadership instrument | TLS (15 items; 5 subdimensions) |
| Safety instrument | DMOSOPS (44 items; 10 dimensions + 2 supplementary) |
| Analysis | Pearson's correlation; dichotomised positive/negative responses |

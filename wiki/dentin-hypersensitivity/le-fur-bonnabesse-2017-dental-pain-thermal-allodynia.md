## Three-line Summary

An ambient cold-to-warm thermal gradient (outdoor → indoor) can trigger delayed, diffuse, sustained dental pain — a form of thermal allodynia distinct from classical dentin hypersensitivity (DH), documented in three female patients aged 49–55 whose pain was unresponsive to standard DH treatments.
The authors propose a four-step mechanism: repeated thermal cycling shifts dentinal fluid outward (140% greater velocity in cold→warm sequences), induces phenotypic neuroplastic changes in C-fiber nociceptors, lowers TRPV1 activation threshold into the innocuous warm range via peripheral sensitization and NGF upregulation, and drives central sensitization producing diffuse, midline-crossing pain.
Effective management targets pulpal thermal insulation (veneers, mouthguards) rather than tubule occlusion, and TRP channel antagonists are proposed as a future pharmacological avenue.

## Summary

Le Fur-Bonnabesse et al. describe three patients presenting with a stereotyped seasonal pain pattern: diffuse, dull, throbbing dental pain beginning approximately 15 minutes after returning indoors from prolonged cold outdoor exposure, peaking in 5–10 minutes, lasting 30 minutes to 3 hours, and resolving spontaneously. Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores ranged 6–8/10. Pain crossed the midline and was not precisely localizable. Standard sensibility tests (air blast, Endo-Ice, dental probe) were normal; no typical hot/cold beverage sensitivity. Electrical pulp testing revealed at least one hyperresponsive tooth per patient. Prior desensitizing treatments (Duraphat, Gluma, desensitizing toothpaste) had all failed.

The paper frames this presentation as **thermal allodynia** — pain in response to a normally innocuous thermal stimulus — mechanistically distinct from classical DH, which involves Aδ-fiber activation via hydrodynamic shear from direct stimulation (air blast, cold food). By contrast, the ambient gradient triggers C-fiber nociceptors after a delay consistent with the time required for thermomechanical events at the dentin-pulp interface to accumulate under sensitized conditions.

## Key Contributions

1. **Novel clinical entity**: First formal description of ambient-temperature-differential-induced dental allodynia as a distinct pain syndrome, with a reproducible confirmatory test (cold water ≥30 s then hot water ≥1 min reproducing pain).
2. **Mechanistic synthesis**: Integration of hydrodynamic theory, TRP channel biology (TRPA1/TRPV1/TRPM8/TRPV2), odontoblastic transduction (PANX-1/P2X3), and central sensitization into a coherent four-step cascade.
3. **Differential diagnosis table**: Explicit comparison of thermal allodynia vs. typical DH across pain quality, fiber type, stimulus modality, onset latency, duration, seasonality, and treatment response.
4. **Treatment rationale**: Pivots from tubule-occlusion logic to pulpal insulation strategy; veneers and orthodontic wax prevented recurrence.
5. **Analogy framework**: Bleaching sensitivity (TRPA1 activation by free radicals → dull unprovoked pain) and Raynaud's phenomenon (pulpal vasoconstriction → rewarming vasodilation → intrapulpal pressure rise) as mechanistic parallels.

## Methodology

Narrative review with three embedded clinical case descriptions. No control group, no prospective data collection, no blinding. Cases were collected at University of Western Brittany (Brest, France) in collaboration with Université Laval (Quebec, Canada). Literature reviewed spans pulpal physiology, TRP channel pharmacology, hydrodynamic theory (Brännström), and pain neuroscience (Sessle). Evidence synthesis is expert opinion supported by mechanistically relevant citations rather than empirical data on this specific presentation.

**Confirmatory diagnostic maneuver** (authors' proposal): ice-cold water held in the mouth ≥30 seconds immediately followed by hot water ≥1 minute. If this reproduces the patient's chief complaint, the diagnosis is supported.

**Preventive test**: orthodontic wax applied to maxillary central incisors before outdoor exposure prevented pain onset — supporting the thermal shield hypothesis.

### Pathophysiological Steps

**Step 1 — Thermal cycling:** Cold-to-warm gradient activates the tooth's thermomechanical response. Enamel contracts with cold → dentinal fluid shifts outward. Lin et al. (2011) showed the cold-then-warm sequence produces outward fluid flow 140% greater than cooling alone, amplifying hydrodynamic shear on sensitized nociceptors.

**Step 2 — Neuroplastic phenotype shift:** Repeated noxious stimulation induces phenotypic changes in peptidergic C-fiber nociceptors — a sub-threshold "silent" neurogenic inflammation state develops without frank inflammatory signs.

**Step 3 — Peripheral sensitization (TRP channel threshold lowering):**
- **TRPV1** (threshold normally >43°C) becomes activated at innocuous warm temperatures after peripheral sensitization
- **TRPA1** (threshold <25°C, cold/mechanosensitive, co-expressed with TRPV1) may be primed during the cold phase
- Substance P (SP) and Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide (CGRP) released → vasodilation, plasma extravasation → neurogenic inflammation → further threshold reduction (positive feedback loop)
- Nerve Growth Factor (NGF) upregulated in inflamed tissue → additional TRPV1 sensitization

**Step 4 — Central sensitization:** Sustained C-fiber barrage to the trigeminal brainstem nuclear complex → neuroplastic alterations → central sensitization explaining diffuse, midline-crossing, non-localizable pain character.

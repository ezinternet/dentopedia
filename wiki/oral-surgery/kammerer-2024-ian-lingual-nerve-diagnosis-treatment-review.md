## Three-line Summary

Scoping review of international literature on traumatic inferior alveolar nerve (IAN) and lingual nerve injury from mandibular dental procedures — epidemiology, diagnosis workup, conservative and surgical management.

IAN injury rates 0.4–5.5% in wisdom tooth removal (transient 8–12%), >30% transient in implantology; no diagnostic gold standard; surgical (decompression/reconstruction) and pharmacological (TCAs, SNRIs) interventions both effective when applied promptly.

No universal guideline exists; treatment must be individualized; immediate action for reversible causes and prompt specialist referral are the strongest clinical levers for favorable outcomes.

## Summary

This 2024 scoping review by Kämmerer et al. (University Medical Center Mainz and collaborating centers) synthesizes the international literature on traumatic injuries to the inferior alveolar nerve (IAN) and lingual nerve — the two trigeminal branches most commonly injured during mandibular dental procedures. The review covers the full clinical arc from risk quantification and Seddon classification, through diagnostic workup, to conservative and surgical management options.

**Epidemiological context:** IAN injury during wisdom tooth removal is reported in 0.4–5.5% of cases on a persistent basis; transient injury affects 8–12%. Lingual nerve injury ranges 0.06–10%. In implantology, transient neurosensory disturbance exceeds 30%; in orthognathic surgery it can reach 60%. Persistent impairment (symptoms >3–6 months) is estimated at up to 1% of all oral/maxillofacial surgical procedures. Under-reporting is substantial, making true incidence difficult to determine. Neurovascular bundle visualization during surgery alone carries a ~20% postoperative paresthesia risk.

**Nerve injury classification (Seddon):**
- Neuropraxia: temporary conduction block, no structural damage — best prognosis
- Axonotmesis: axon and myelin disrupted, connective tissue framework intact — partial recovery expected
- Neurotmesis: complete nerve severance — requires surgical reconstruction for any recovery

**Clinical presentation:** Patients present with primary sensory deficits (anesthesia, hypoesthesia, paresthesia) and/or neuropathic pain symptoms (dysesthesia, allodynia, hyperalgesia). IAN injury produces functional consequences including lip biting, uncontrolled salivation, and speech difficulties. Lingual nerve injury additionally causes taste loss, tongue biting, and eating difficulties. Quality-of-life impairment is consistently documented, with psychological and social dimensions requiring interdisciplinary attention.

**Diagnosis:** No gold standard exists. The recommended workup includes:
1. Comprehensive history: onset, duration, character, location, severity, precipitating/alleviating factors, prior treatments, functional limitations
2. Clinical-neurological evaluation using neurosensory tests
3. Radiographic imaging: panoramic as screening; MRI as the preferred modality for detailed nerve anatomy (conventional radiograph shows only osseous boundaries of the mandibular canal)
4. Comorbidity assessment: anxiety, depression, sleep disorders are frequent and require interdisciplinary management

Neuropathic pain diagnosis requires all four Schlereth criteria: (1) history consistent with a lesion; (2) pain in a neuroanatomically plausible distribution; (3) pathologic sensory findings in that distribution; (4) an examination method identifying a peripheral somatosensory lesion.

At first presentation, injury probability should be graded: certain / probable / possible / unlikely.

**Treatment — surgical:** For reversible causes, immediate intervention is recommended. Surgical options include decompression (for compression injuries), readaptation (end-to-end neurorrhaphy), and reconstruction (nerve grafting). Timely surgical intervention yields favorable outcomes; delays worsen prognosis.

**Treatment — conservative (neuropathic pain):**
- Topical: local anesthetic agents, capsaicin
- Systemic: tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), antipsychotics, serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs)
- Goals: reduce dysesthesia and allodynia, restore function, improve quality of life

**Conclusions:** Traumatic nerve injuries are common but frequently unreported in dental practice. A meticulous, individualized examination protocol — combining history, clinical testing, and imaging — is essential. No universal solution exists; outcomes are optimized by prompt identification, cause-specific intervention, and multidisciplinary pain management.

## Key Contributions

- Establishes the only comprehensive 2024 scoping review covering both IAN and lingual nerve injury across the full clinical spectrum
- Provides the most current epidemiological summary of injury rates by procedure type
- Applies Seddon classification framework explicitly to the dental context
- Synthesizes the pharmacological landscape for neuropathic orofacial pain management
- Argues for urgent development of a standardized diagnostic and treatment guideline (currently absent)

## Methodology

- **Design**: Scoping review
- **Databases**: PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library + manual national/international guideline search
- **Search terms**: nerve injury/damage, anesthesia, hypoesthesia, paresthesia, neuropathic pain, diagnosis/diagnostic, examination, treatment, medication, surgery, conservative
- **Data extraction**: study design, sample size, intervention, outcome measures, results
- **Synthesis**: descriptive narrative; no PRISMA flow or formal quality grading reported

## Results

### Injury rate by procedure

| Procedure | IAN injury (transient) | Persistent |
|---|---|---|
| Wisdom tooth removal | 8–12% | 0.4–5.5% |
| Cystectomy / IANB | ~0.15% | ~0.15% |
| Dental implantology | >30% | lower (unspecified) |
| Orthognathic surgery | up to 60% | lower (unspecified) |

- All oral-maxillofacial procedures combined: up to 1% persistent impairment
- Lingual nerve: 0.06–10% from wisdom tooth removal

### Key diagnostic thresholds
- Symptoms >3–6 months → classify as persistent/permanent injury
- Neurovascular bundle visualized intraoperatively → ~20% paresthesia risk

### Treatment hierarchy
1. Reversible cause identified → immediate surgical action
2. Decompression → readaptation → reconstruction (escalating severity)
3. Neuropathic pain: topical agents → systemic TCAs/SNRIs/antipsychotics
4. Comorbid psychological conditions → interdisciplinary referral

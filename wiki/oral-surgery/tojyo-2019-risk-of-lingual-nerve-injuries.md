## Three-line Summary

Retrospective case-control study of 79 patients (24 males, 58 females; mean age 36.5y) who underwent microsurgical repair of severe iatrogenic lingual nerve injury after mandibular third molar removal, with impaction type (Winter's / Pell and Gregory classification) compared against six literature control cohorts.

Distoangular impaction was significantly overrepresented in the lingual nerve injury group (30.8% vs 0.2–15.8% in most control groups), and female sex (72.1% vs 47–59% in controls, P=0.001) and older age (mean 36.5y vs 27–34y in controls, P<0.0001 in most comparisons) were also significantly associated with injury; injury side showed no significant association.

Distoangular impaction of the mandibular third molar in women in their 30s–50s may be a higher-risk configuration for severe lingual nerve injury during extraction, but the control groups were literature-derived and heterogeneous rather than a matched contemporaneous cohort.

## Summary

This 2019 retrospective case-control study from Wakayama Medical University examined 79 patients who required microsurgical repair of severe, iatrogenic lingual nerve injury sustained during mandibular third molar removal (injuries occurred at other facilities; the repairs were performed at the authors' center, 2003–2016). Because severe lingual nerve injury is rare, the authors compared their case-series data against six independently published control-group datasets covering sex, age, injury side, and Winter's classification distribution, rather than using a single matched cohort.

The central finding is that **distoangular impaction** of the mandibular third molar was significantly overrepresented among severe lingual nerve injury cases relative to most control groups, alongside significant associations with **female sex** and **older age**. The authors propose an anatomical mechanism: distoangular position places the tooth crown anatomically closer to the lingual nerve/plate, increasing exposure during bone removal and tooth dislocation, particularly when the distolingual bone is shaved (observed in 5/8 distoangular cases in this series).

## Key Contributions

- Isolates impaction-type risk specifically for **severe** lingual nerve injury requiring surgical nerve repair, a distinct and much rarer endpoint than transient neurosensory disturbance.
- Cross-validates findings against six separately published control datasets rather than a single reference population, showing the distoangular association holds across most (though not all) comparators.
- Proposes and illustrates (Fig. 3 in the source paper) an anatomical rationale linking distoangular tooth position to lingual plate/nerve exposure during bone removal.

## Methodology

- Retrospective chart review, 79 patients (2003–2016), all having undergone microsurgical lingual nerve repair (epineural end-to-end suture, no grafts) after injury during third molar removal elsewhere.
- Indication for microsurgery: witnessed transection, OR persistent (>3 months) deficit on ≥1 of: 2-point discrimination >20mm, temperature/taste/pin-prick loss, or Semmes-Weinstein monofilament asymmetry.
- Winter's classification (angulation) and Pell and Gregory classification (depth/space) applied to the 26/79 cases with available orthopantomographs.
- Chi-squared and t-tests vs. six literature control-group datasets (Almendros-Marqués 2006, Uematsu 2015, Cheung 2010, Blondeau 2007, Smith 2013, Oguma 2013).

## Results

- Female 72.1% vs. control range 47–61% (significant in 4/5 comparisons, P as low as 0.001).
- Mean age 36.5y vs. control range 27–34y (significant, P<0.0001, in 3/4 comparisons).
- Injury side (right 55.7% / left 44.3%) not significantly different from control removal-side distribution (P=0.28).
- Distoangular impaction 30.8% of imaged cases (n=26) vs. control range 0.2–31.0%; significant in 4/5 comparisons (P=0.009 to <0.0001), not significant against the one control group (Smith 2013) that itself had a high distoangular rate (31.0%).
- Depth/space: Position B (69.2%) and Class II (61.5%) were the most common Pell and Gregory categories.

## Limitations

- Control groups are heterogeneous, literature-derived, and not contemporaneous/matched — a major limitation the authors acknowledge.
- Only 26/79 cases (33%) had imaging available for Winter's/Pell and Gregory classification.
- Whether a lingual flap or retractor was used during the index (injury-causing) extraction is unknown for this cohort.
- Single-center Japanese cohort of only severe, surgically-repaired injuries; cannot estimate absolute incidence rates or generalize to transient/milder lingual nerve injury.

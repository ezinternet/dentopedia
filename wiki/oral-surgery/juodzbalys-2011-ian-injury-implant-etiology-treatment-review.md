## Three-line Summary

Narrative review of 136 sources (1972–2010) systematically classifying IAN injury aetiology, mechanism, clinical presentation, diagnostics, and treatment guidelines for implant-related inferior alveolar nerve injury.

Incidence 0–40%; intraoperative causes (mechanical, thermal, chemical) and postoperative causes (thermal, infection, haematoma) identified; nerve damage graded neuropraxia → neurotmesis per Seddon/Sunderland.

Maintain ≥2 mm radiographic safety margin from mandibular canal; if direct injury suspected, remove/reposition implant within 30–36 h; microsurgical repair for Grade IV–V injuries unresolved at 3 months.

## Summary

This foundational narrative review by Juodzbalys, Wang, and Sabalys (University of Michigan / Lithuanian University of Health Sciences) synthesizes 136 published sources to create the first comprehensive clinical framework for managing IAN injury during dental implant placement. It remains a widely cited reference for classification and management of this complication.

IAN injury is reported at 0–40% incidence across series. The IAN is the most commonly injured nerve in oral implantology (64.4%), followed by the lingual nerve (28.8%). Injuries may be transient (neuropraxia) or permanent (neurotmesis). Many injuries go unrecognized until the patient reports numbness postoperatively.

## Key Contributions

**Aetiology classification**:
- *Intraoperative — mechanical*: injection needle trauma, implant drill (partial or full mandibular canal [MC] intrusion), implant itself (chronic pressure), scalpel, soft-tissue retraction
- *Intraoperative — thermal*: bone overheating without adequate irrigation → inflammation → secondary ischaemia
- *Intraoperative — chemical*: cytotoxic local anaesthetic or irrigation solution → direct nerve degeneration
- *Postoperative*: thermal, peri-implant infection, haematoma with reactive fibrosis and scarring → secondary ischaemia

**Nerve injury grading** (Seddon/Sunderland):
- Neuropraxia (Grade I): reversible; best prognosis
- Axonotmesis (Grades II–IV): varying degrees of axon and endoneurial disruption; partial spontaneous recovery possible
- Neurotmesis (Grade V): complete severance; microsurgical repair required

**Sensory examination protocol**: subjective patient report → tactile (light touch, brush direction), pain (pinprick), two-point discrimination, thermal — combined to characterise deficit severity

## Methodology

Narrative review; PubMed, Embase, Cochrane searched 1972–November 2010; English-language restriction; 136 sources reviewed. Clinical, human anatomy, and physiology studies included. Manual search of implant, periodontal, and oral surgery journals and textbooks.

## Results

**Safety margin**: ≥2 mm from implant apex to superior cortex of mandibular canal recommended (radiographic measurement)

**Management timeline and algorithm**:

| Time | Action |
|---|---|
| Intraoperative / within 30–36 h | Suspected direct contact → remove or reposition implant immediately |
| 0–3 months | Pharmacologic: corticosteroids (dexamethasone) + B-vitamins (B1, B6, B12) ± NSAIDs; monitor |
| 3 months, no improvement | Consider microsurgical exploration |
| Confirmed Grade IV–V | Microsurgery: neurolysis, neurorrhaphy, or nerve grafting |

**Pharmacologic support**: systemic corticosteroids reduce endoneurial oedema; B-vitamins (especially B12 methylcobalamin) support axonal regeneration; NSAIDs for anti-inflammatory effect

**Microsurgical options**: neurolysis (decompression of nerve from scar), neurorrhaphy (end-to-end suture), nerve grafting (sural nerve graft for gaps >10 mm)

**Presurgical prevention**: CBCT for accurate MC identification, 3D implant planning, patient risk stratification (mandibular atrophy, anatomic variants)

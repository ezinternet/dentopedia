## Three-line Summary

FDI World Dental Federation policy statement (Int Dent J 2024;74(1):163-164; PubMed type: Practice Guideline) on tooth wear, defined as cumulative non-carious loss of mineralized tooth substance from erosion, attrition, abrasion and possibly abfraction.

It states that wear progression can be controlled if the aetiology is recognized (acid exposure, brushing, malocclusion/parafunction, low saliva, GERD, bulimia), and that prevalence has been reported as high as 80%.

Management is sequenced: address behaviours and underlying medical causes first, restore afterwards; the retrieved PMC text lacks the formal recommendation list and grades.

## Summary

Source: PubMed ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.identj.2023.10.007)), full text via PMC. This two-page FDI policy statement frames tooth wear as the umbrella for non-carious surface loss, comprising erosion, attrition, abrasion and potentially abfraction, which may act independently or together. Mechanisms include non-bacterial acid exposure and non-physiological mechanical forces from toothbrushing, malocclusion and parafunction; low salivary flow and buffering capacity are modifying factors. Erosive tooth wear may be a symptom of GERD or eating disorders such as bulimia nervosa. The statement holds that progression can be controlled or prevented once the aetiology is recognized, and that restoration becomes appropriate after the underlying cause has been addressed.

## Key Contributions

- Umbrella definition of tooth wear and separate definitions of its component mechanisms.
- Erosion defined as a chemical-mechanical process not caused by bacteria; acid sources split into extrinsic (dietary, environmental, drug) and intrinsic (gastric).
- Notes that the term abfraction has been questioned as a separate process given the level of supporting clinical evidence.
- Preventive principles: local oral situation, behaviour change (eating, drinking, toothbrushing), collaboration with medical specialists for GERD and eating disorders with frequent vomiting.

## Results

No quantitative findings. The only figure is a reported prevalence of tooth wear "as high as 80%", described as geographically related. Stated principle: successful management depends on accurate diagnosis and understanding of aetiological factors.

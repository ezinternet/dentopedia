## Three-line Summary

Narrative overview (Current Oncology 2023, literature search Jan 2012–Jan 2021 across PubMed/EMBASE/Web of Science/ScienceDirect/Google Scholar) summarizing 10 years of systemic pharmacological and traditional-medicine (TM) approaches to xerostomia prevention/treatment, mainly in head-and-neck cancer (HNC) and Sjögren's syndrome (SS) populations.

Amifostine (cytoprotective, free-radical scavenger) is the most-discussed HNC-radiotherapy preventive agent but has unclear net benefit and frequent side effects (nausea, hypotension, vomiting); pilocarpine/cevimeline/bethanechol (para-sympathomimetics) and Sjögren's biologics (rituximab, epratuzumab, belimumab, interferon alpha) show low-to-inconsistent efficacy with substantial side-effect burden; TM (East Asian herbal decoctions, Western herb/mouthwash mixtures) has too few and too low-quality clinical trials to validate efficacy or safety.

No systemic chemical treatment or TM approach currently has robust evidence for preventing or resolving xerostomia — clinical guidelines remain absent, and management of xerostomia and its complications is described by the authors as "a very significant void in daily clinical practice."

## Summary

According to PubMed ([DOI 10.3390/curroncol30050336](https://doi.org/10.3390/curroncol30050336)), this narrative overview synthesizes 10 years (Jan 2012–Jan 2021) of published clinical experience on systemic pharmacological and traditional-medicine (TM) management of xerostomia, focused mainly on head-and-neck cancer (HNC) radiotherapy patients and Sjögren's syndrome (SS) patients. Amifostine — a cytoprotective organic thiophosphate that scavenges free radicals, protects/accelerates repair of DNA, and induces cellular hypoxia — is the most-discussed *preventive* agent against radiotherapy-induced xerostomia, but evidence for net benefit is inconsistent (no clear advantage over IV saline placebo in some trials) and is offset by vomiting, hypotension, nausea, and allergic reactions, plus unresolved debate over its effect on cancer-treatment survival outcomes. Antioxidants (CoQ10, vitamin E ± vitamin C, lycopene) target the oxidative stress common to SS, systemic sclerosis, and radiotherapy-damaged glands, generally improving salivary flow/composition versus baseline, though without established dosing guidelines.

For established disease, para-sympathomimetic sialagogues (pilocarpine, cevimeline, bethanechol) stimulate residual gland function via non-selective mAChR agonism (M1–M5 subtypes, M3 dominant), but a Cochrane review found pilocarpine's benefit over placebo statistically unclear, and all three drugs carry substantial systemic side-effect burden (headache, urinary frequency, vasodilatation, GI upset, and contraindications including hyperthyroidism, asthma, and cardiac disease) that limits use and drives study withdrawal. For SS specifically, a roster of biologics targeting lymphocytic gland infiltration (rituximab, epratuzumab, belimumab, ianalumab, baminercept, abatacept, efalizumab, TNF/IL-6/IL-1 inhibitors, interferon alpha) has been tried; most show either no significant benefit or only transient/partial improvement, several carry serious infection/autoimmune-toxicity risk, and efalizumab was withdrawn from market entirely. On the TM side, East Asian herbal decoctions/formulas (Shennongbaijie, Xuanmaizengyehuadu, Yunnanbaiyao, and herbs like Plantago major, ginger, quercetin, Malva sylvestris) show some subjective dry-mouth improvement during radiotherapy, but evidence quality is very-low-to-moderate with high risk of bias; Western TM (chamomile, sage, mallow, propolis mixtures) is almost entirely tested in vitro/cell-line rather than in clinical trials, limiting real-world validation.

Clinically, the review's bottom line is stark: **no systemic chemical drug and no TM approach currently has robust evidence for preventing or resolving xerostomia.** No clinical guideline exists, and every agent reviewed trades incomplete/inconsistent efficacy against a real side-effect burden — reinforcing that current xerostomia management is symptomatic and supportive (topical/local measures, careful medication review) rather than curative.

## Key Contributions

- Consolidates 10 years (2012–2021) of systemic pharmacological literature on xerostomia across 5 drug classes: cytoprotective (amifostine), antioxidant (CoQ10, vitamin E/C, lycopene), para-sympathomimetic (pilocarpine, cevimeline, bethanechol), para-sympatholytic (protective secretion-inhibition, animal data only), and biological (Sjögren's-targeted monoclonal antibodies/cytokine modulators).
- Explains the mechanistic reason non-selective sialagogues (pilocarpine et al.) carry broad systemic side effects: stimulation is not restricted to the acinar-dominant M3 mAChR subtype but spreads across M1–M5.
- First English-language overview (per the retrieved text) to separately catalogue East Asian TM herbal decoctions and Western TM herb/mouthwash mixtures for xerostomia/oral mucositis, flagging that Western TM evidence is overwhelmingly in vitro/cell-line rather than clinical.
- Frames amifostine's cancer-survival-outcome controversy as an open question rather than settled fact — useful nuance beyond simply listing it as "the main preventive agent."
- Bottom-line conclusion useful for chairside triage: xerostomia management currently has no guideline-backed systemic cure; expectations for pharmacological/TM intervention should be set accordingly (symptomatic/supportive care remains primary).

## Methodology

- **Design**: Narrative literature overview — a stated search period and set of databases, but not a registered systematic review (no PRISMA flow diagram, no explicit a priori inclusion/exclusion criteria beyond publication date, no formal risk-of-bias or GRADE assessment reported).
- **Search period**: January 1, 2012 – January 30, 2021.
- **Databases**: PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, Google Scholar.
- **Scope**: systemic (oral/IV) pharmacological agents and traditional/complementary medicine approaches for xerostomia prevention and treatment; local/topical salivary substitutes and device-based sialogogue methods fall outside scope.
- **Populations**: predominantly HNC radiotherapy patients and primary/secondary Sjögren's syndrome patients; general drug-induced xerostomia (>400 xerogenic medications) discussed as etiological background rather than a treatment-trial population.
- **Interpretive caveat**: because this is a narrative rather than systematic review, treat its efficacy statements as a clinical-experience synthesis (hypothesis-generating), not as graded systematic evidence.

## Results

- **Xerostomia epidemiology**: ~30% incidence at age ≥65 (rising to 61% with comorbidity/polypharmacy); up to 40% of HNC radiotherapy patients affected; 45.9% of SARS-CoV-2-infected subjects reported xerostomia.
- **Amifostine**: mixed trial results (some show reduced post-radiotherapy dryness; others show no benefit vs IV saline placebo); side effects include vomiting, hypotension, nausea, allergic reactions; unresolved debate on impact on survival/QoL outcomes.
- **Antioxidants**: CoQ10 (ubiquinol > ubiquinone) increased salivary production in SS; vitamin E ± vitamin C improved salivary pH/potassium/amylase/flow and VAS during radiotherapy; lycopene-enriched olive oil outperformed isolated lycopene.
- **Pilocarpine**: Cochrane review found efficacy vs placebo statistically unclear; side effects at 2.5–10 mg up to 3×/day — headache 15%, urinary frequency 14%, vasodilatation 12%, dizziness 10%, dyspepsia 10%, nausea 8%, asthenia 8%, diarrhea 5% (leading cause of withdrawal).
- **Cevimeline**: 2 RCTs found "quite effective" (less granular side-effect data reported).
- **Bethanechol**: increased basal flow vs artificial saliva in irradiated patients, but contraindicated in hyperthyroidism/peptic ulcer/asthma/bradycardia/hypotension/coronary disease/epilepsy/Parkinsonism.
- **Sjögren's biologics**: rituximab improved flow/lacrimal function transiently (lost by 48 weeks); epratuzumab improved lacrimal/salivary flow but caused infusion reactions/infections; belimumab showed no salivary-flow change and caused serious infection (pneumococcal meningitis); ianalumab/baminercept low efficacy with toxicity; abatacept controversial; efalizumab withdrawn from market (multifocal leukoencephalopathy); TNF/IL-6/IL-1 inhibitors showed no significant benefit; interferon alpha improved dryness/flow in some trials (possible AQP5 upregulation) but limited by GI adverse effects.
- **TM (East Asian)**: Plantago major, Kampo mixtures, ginger, quercetin, Malva sylvestris + Althaea officinalis (significant VAS efficacy in a 4-week trial), and multi-herb decoctions (Shennongbaijie, Xuanmaizengyehuadu, Yunnanbaiyao, Jiaweizengye, Sanganhuayin) show some benefit during radiotherapy, but very-low-to-moderate evidence quality with high risk of bias.
- **TM (Western)**: 15–73% of European cancer patients self-medicate with TM; most European evidence is in vitro/cell-line (antimicrobial/anti-inflammatory/antioxidant assays); few clinical trials exist (Aloe vera + honey; propolis + Ficus carica + Malva sylvestris + Althaea officinalis mouthwash; sage/Echinacea sprays).

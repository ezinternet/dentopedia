## Three-line Summary

Case report, n=1, 70-year-old edentulous male with a lingual mandibular bony exostosis discovered on routine dental check-up.

Brownish-yellow, bony-hard, non-tender, sessile protuberance (~1×1.5 cm) at the lingual left mandibular second molar region; orthopantomogram showed a radiopaque mass blending with adjacent cortical bone; diagnosis made on history and clinical exam alone (no CBCT, no biopsy).

Careful history of no bisphosphonate/radiotherapy exposure distinguished this benign, treatment-not-required exostosis from osteonecrosis/osteoradionecrosis; the main limitation is the lack of confirmatory imaging or histopathology.

## Summary

This case report describes a 70-year-old edentulous male who presented for a routine check-up with an incidentally discovered bony protuberance on the lingual aspect of the left mandibular second molar region, above the mylohyoid ridge. The lesion (~1 × 1.5 cm, brownish-yellow, bony-hard, sessile, non-tender) was diagnosed clinically and radiographically as a mandibular bony exostosis (hyperostosis), with the key diagnostic task being exclusion of more serious differentials — osteonecrosis, osteoradionecrosis, osteosarcoma, chronic osteomyelitis, and bone spur/dense bone island — based on history (no bisphosphonate/antiresorptive drug use, no radiotherapy, no diabetes/hypertension) and radiographic density (blending with adjacent cortical bone, unlike osteoma's dense cortical border or osteosarcoma's sunburst pattern). No treatment was performed since the patient was asymptomatic; exostoses only warrant intervention if they interfere with denture insertion or periodontal procedures.

Background epidemiology cited: oral exostosis prevalence 14.4% (Sawair et al. 2009, Jordan, n=618), palatal exostosis 25–47.5% across ethnic dry-skull samples (Nery et al.), and 1.8% among twins (Auškalnis et al.), supporting that exostoses are a common, usually incidental finding whose main clinical relevance is diagnostic differentiation and, in denture wearers, mechanical interference.

## Key Contributions

- A compact bedside differential-diagnosis table for a bony jaw lesion: exostosis/hyperostosis vs. dense bone island, bone spur, chronic osteomyelitis, osteoma/osteochondroma, osteosarcoma, osteonecrosis, and osteoradionecrosis — each with its distinguishing radiographic/clinical feature.
- Demonstrates that a thorough drug and radiotherapy history can be sufficient, in an asymptomatic patient, to rule out MRONJ/osteoradionecrosis without biopsy.
- Reiterates proposed etiologic mechanisms for exostosis: embryologic (endochondral ossification near Meckel's cartilage at the mylohyoid muscle attachment, weeks 10–13 of gestation), functional (occlusal overload, bruxism/attrition, TMJ dysfunction), and the subpontic hyperostosis theory (bone apposition beneath a pontic opposing occlusal load).

## Methodology

Single clinical case: history-taking, extraoral and intraoral examination, palpation, and orthopantomography (OPG). No CBCT or histopathology was performed — a limitation the authors explicitly acknowledge, since the patient (asymptomatic) declined biopsy/excision.

## Results

- Clinical: brownish-yellow, bony-hard, sessile, non-tender mass, ~1 × 1.5 cm, lingual aspect of the left mandibular second molar region, above the mylohyoid ridge; adjacent whitish, non-scrapable area consistent with denture-related ridge keratosis.
- Radiographic: OPG showed a radiopaque mass whose density blends into the adjacent cortical mandibular bone — the key feature separating simple exostosis from osteoma/osteochondroma (dense outer cortical border) and osteosarcoma (sunburst periosteal reaction).
- Outcome: no treatment; exostoses are managed expectantly unless they interfere with denture function or periodontal/gingivectomy procedures, in which case surgical removal (mallet and chisel) is an option, with excised bone occasionally reused as autograft for shallow bone-crater defects.

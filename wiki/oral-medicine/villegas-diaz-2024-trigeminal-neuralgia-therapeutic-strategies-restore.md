## Three-line Summary

Narrative review (32 articles selected from 20,121 search hits, through 31 May 2024) summarizing trigeminal neuralgia (TN) etiology, diagnosis, and pharmacological/surgical/alternative treatment strategies for dentists.

Carbamazepine (100–1200 mg/day) remains the gold-standard first-line drug with near-100% reported symptom reduction; oxcarbazepine (900–2400 mg/day) substitutes on intolerance; microvascular decompression (MVD) achieves 92.7% initial success (0.7% mortality, 2%/year recurrence) versus radiofrequency rhizotomy's 97% immediate relief but 42% five-year recurrence.

TN incidence is 4.3/100,000/year (women>men, 5.9:3.4) with trigger zones and provoking stimuli (chewing, tooth brushing) overlapping dental pain, making dentists frequent first-contact clinicians — the authors argue pharmacological management should stay central in dental practice, with surgery reserved for medication-refractory cases; the review itself is non-systematic (no PRISMA/appraisal), so its treatment hierarchy is lower-confidence than a formal SR.

## Summary

This is a dentist-oriented narrative review on trigeminal neuralgia (TN, tic douloureux) — paroxysmal, unilateral, electric-shock-like facial pain along one or more branches (V1 ophthalmic, V2 maxillary, V3 mandibular) of the trigeminal nerve. TN affects 4.3/100,000 people annually (women more than men, 5.9:3.4) and is classified as idiopathic, classical (vascular root compression — the most common and best-understood mechanism), or secondary (major neurological disease such as a cerebellopontine angle tumor or multiple sclerosis). Because trigger zones (nasolabial area, lips, chin, cheek, alveolar gingiva) and provoking stimuli (light touch, talking, chewing, tooth brushing) overlap heavily with dental pain, dentists are frequently the first clinicians consulted, making differential diagnosis a clinically important skill.

The paper walks through the full treatment ladder: pharmacological (carbamazepine as gold standard, with oxcarbazepine, gabapentin, pregabalin, lamotrigine, phenytoin, baclofen, and botulinum toxin as alternatives/adjuncts), surgical (rhizotomy techniques and microvascular decompression, MVD), and alternative/adjunct therapies (acupuncture, low-level laser therapy). The authors take an explicit editorial stance — favoring sustained pharmacological management in dental practice and pushing back against authors who advocate early surgical escalation, arguing surgery should be reserved for medication-refractory cases. As a narrative (not systematic) review with no PRISMA flow or formal quality appraisal, its evidence weight is below a systematic review, but its therapeutic-ladder framing and dosing table closely track and independently corroborate the SR-derived ladder in [[oral-medicine/khan-2023-trigeminal-neuralgia-therapeutic-approach-sr]].

## Key Contributions

- Consolidated single-table dosing reference for eight TN pharmacological agents (anticonvulsants, antispasmodic, toxin).
- Clear articulation of the dentist's differential-diagnosis role given trigger-zone/provoking-stimulus overlap with dental pain.
- Head-to-head numeric contrast of surgical modalities: MVD (92.7% success, 0.7% mortality, 2%/year recurrence) vs. radiofrequency rhizotomy (97% immediate relief, 42% five-year recurrence) vs. gamma-knife (MVD reported superior for drug-resistant TN).
- Critical editorial synthesis contrasting prior authors' surgery-first vs. pharmacology-first positions, landing on a pharmacology-centered recommendation for dental practice.

## Methodology

Narrative literature review via the BIDI UNAM Digital Library (PubMed, EBSCO, ScienceDirect, Springer, ProQuest, BMJ, Health Library, Elsevier, SciELO), search through 31 May 2024. Six keyword combinations retrieved 20,121 total hits; 32 articles were hand-selected based on recency and representativeness — no PRISMA flow diagram, no formal risk-of-bias/quality appraisal, and no registered protocol (unlike khan-2023's PROSPERO-registered umbrella SR). This methodological gap is the key reason its confidence label is `narrative-review` rather than `sr`.

## Results

**Epidemiology & classification**: Incidence 4.3/100,000/year, female:male 5.9:3.4; right-side predominance in MS-associated TN only; V2/V3 branches affected in 36–42% of patients; classification into idiopathic / classical (vascular compression, most common) / secondary (tumor, MS — cerebellopontine angle tumors in 15% of secondary cases).

**Pharmacological dosing** (see source page for full table): carbamazepine 100–1200 mg/day (gold standard, ~100% symptom reduction reported in treated patients per cited sources, but with drowsiness/dizziness/rash/liver injury/ataxia risk); oxcarbazepine 900–2400 mg/day (substitute on intolerance, main drawback cost); gabapentin 900–3600 mg/day, pregabalin 75–600 mg/day, lamotrigine 100–600 mg/day, phenytoin 300–600 mg/day, baclofen 25–135 mg/day, botulinum toxin 25–75 IU/infiltration — used in combination when carbamazepine/oxcarbazepine reach maximum tolerated doses.

**Surgical outcomes**: radiofrequency rhizotomy — up to 97% immediate relief, 42% recurrence at 5 years, ablative (causes facial anesthesia proportional to relief depth). MVD — 92.7% initial success, 0.7% mortality, 2%/year recurrence, <1% major complication rate (cerebellar injury, hearing loss, facial paralysis, CSF fistula); no significant compression found in 15% of explored cases; reported superior to gamma-knife radiosurgery for drug-resistant TN.

**Alternative/adjunct therapies**: acupuncture — reduces pain/response-rate/adverse-events vs. carbamazepine only at very-low evidence confidence, insufficient to recommend. BTX-A — clinically significant benefit vs. placebo on responder proportion, paroxysm frequency, and VAS scores, effect lasting ≥3 months. LLLT — proposed mechanism via reduced histamine/bradykinin/acetylcholine/PGE2 and increased endorphin/ATP/enkephalin mRNA expression; animal-model evidence for axonal regrowth; framed as a painless, non-invasive option for patients who tolerate pharmacotherapy poorly.

**Editorial synthesis**: authors critique surgery-first proponents (Leclercq et al.) as extreme, align more closely with pharmacology-first proponents (Wang et al.), and conclude that in dentistry the pharmacological role is likely the most prominent, with surgery reserved as a last resort.

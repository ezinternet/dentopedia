## Three-line Summary

This 16-year prospective cohort (SHIP-START, n = 364) found a statistically significant cross-sectional association between occlusal wear (OW) size and non-carious cervical lesion (NCCL) size (OR = 1.74, 95% CI: 1.27–2.38), measured on cast models using mixed-effects ordinal logistic models. However, the longitudinal association across 16 years was non-significant (OR = 1.14, 95% CI: 0.99–1.30), indicating that OW change does not significantly predict NCCL progression over time in this cohort. The findings suggest cross-sectional co-occurrence of OW and NCCLs but do not provide sufficient causal evidence for the abfraction hypothesis; larger longitudinal studies are warranted.

## Background

The abfraction hypothesis proposes that occlusal loading generates stress concentrations at the cervical fulcrum of teeth, causing fatigue-related enamel and dentin loss (NCCL). While cross-sectional studies have repeatedly shown co-occurrence of occlusal wear (OW) and NCCLs, longitudinal evidence on whether OW *causes* or *precedes* NCCL formation has remained scarce. The SHIP-START cohort provides an unusually long (16-year) prospective window to address this gap directly.

## Methods

- **Design**: Prospective cohort study; SHIP-START population, Pomerania, Germany
- **Sample**: 364 participants; 728 cast models (upper and lower jaw pairs); baseline + 16-year follow-up
- **Cross-sectional analysis**: 1308 teeth / 291 subjects
- **Longitudinal analysis**: 718 teeth / 226 subjects
- **Measurement**: Occlusal and cervical defects measured on cast models (objective, quantitative)
- **Statistical model**: Adjusted mixed-effects ordinal logistic regression at tooth level
- **Adjustment**: Covariates included in mixed-effects models (subject-level random effects)

## Key Findings

| Outcome | OR | 95% CI | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cross-sectional OW size → NCCL size | 1.74 | 1.27–2.38 | Significant |
| Cross-sectional OW² (non-linearity) | 0.97 | 0.94–0.99 | Significant |
| Longitudinal OW change → NCCL change | 1.14 | 0.99–1.30 | **Non-significant** |

- **Predicted probability** of NCCL = 0 decreased from ~0.996 (OW = 0) to ~0.010 (OW = 25) in cross-sectional analysis, illustrating the magnitude of the cross-sectional gradient.
- The negative quadratic term (OW²) indicates a **curvilinear** relationship: at very high OW levels, the incremental increase in NCCL probability diminishes.
- Longitudinal direction is consistent with cross-sectional findings, but the confidence interval straddles 1.0 — the study is likely underpowered for longitudinal detection.

## Clinical Implications

1. **Screening**: Patients presenting with high occlusal wear have substantially elevated cross-sectional probability of NCCLs; occlusal wear assessment should prompt cervical examination.
2. **Caution on causal attribution**: Current evidence does not support occlusal adjustment or splint therapy as a proven strategy for *preventing NCCL progression*. The longitudinal null finding does not rule out a weak causal effect, but it does not support it at the effect size seen cross-sectionally.
3. **Multifactorial etiology**: The dissociation between cross-sectional and longitudinal findings is consistent with NCCLs having multiple etiological contributors (abrasion, erosion, abfraction), none of which alone may be sufficient or necessary.

## Limitations

- Relatively small longitudinal subsample (226 subjects, 718 teeth) — may be underpowered
- Cast model measurement has excellent objectivity but requires clinical model fabrication (resource-intensive); follow-up completeness may introduce selection bias
- No direct measurement of occlusal force or bruxism activity
- German general adult population — generalizability to other populations unclear

## Relation to Abfraction Debate

This paper directly addresses the central empirical question of the abfraction controversy: *does occlusal wear precede and predict NCCL formation over time?* The 16-year longitudinal null result (OR = 1.14, p NS) is important evidence that the OW–NCCL relationship is not as strongly causal as abfraction theory predicts. Combined with the findings from [[nccl/dioguardi-2024-abfraction-theory-controversy-scoping-review]] (scoping review concluding abfraction is contested) and earlier systematic reviews ([[nccl/senna-2012-nccl-occlusion-systematic-review]], [[nccl/silva-2013-occlusal-factors-nccl-systematic-review]]), the balance of evidence does not support occlusal wear as the primary driver of NCCL progression.
